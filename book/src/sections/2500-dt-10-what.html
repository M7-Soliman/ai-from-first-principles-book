Part AWhat a dataset is

§1 The assumption everything so far has rested on

In 60 seconds

Every guarantee in Parts V and VI assumed training and test data are drawn independently from the same distribution. That assumption is not a technicality; it is what makes a test score mean anything. It is also, in nearly every real project, false in ways nobody checked.

Part VI §5 stated it and moved on, because there was nothing useful to say until the rest of the machinery existed. Now there is.

What i.i.d. is doing for you

Part V's whole apparatus (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, the bootstrap) assumes your sample is drawn independently from the population you care about. Part VI's generalisation argument assumes the test set is drawn from the same distribution as the training set.

Together they license the one inference that matters: a score measured on held-out data predicts performance in deployment. Break either assumption and the number is still a number; it just stops being about anything you wanted to know.

Why this part exists, and why it is thirteen parts late

The book could not have been written in this order. To say something useful about data you need estimators (Part V), generalisation (Part VI), regularization (Part VIII) and enough architecture to know what data does to a model.

But the ordering has a cost worth naming: twelve parts have quietly treated the dataset as a given, and that is how the subject is usually taught. The result is a field in which the model is scrutinised in enormous detail and the input is not, where a paper will ablate five architectural choices and describe its corpus in one sentence.

That asymmetry is not defensible. On most real problems, changing the data changes the outcome more than changing the model, and this part is the argument for treating it with the same seriousness as everything else in the book.

§2 Population, sample, and the frame

Three distinct things are routinely called "the data", and separating them is most of the work.

The three

The population. What you actually care about: every input the deployed system will see. Usually not enumerable, often not even well defined until you try to write it down.

The sampling frame. The part of the population you were able to reach. Everything on the public web; every patient at these three hospitals; every user who agreed to logging.

The sample. What you actually drew from the frame, and labelled, and kept.

Statistics gives you tools for the gap between frame and sample: that is what Part V is. It gives you nothing at all for the gap between population and frame. That gap is a modelling assumption, and it is the one that ends projects.

Pitfall: a bigger sample does not close the frame gap

Part V §3's $1/\sqrt{n}$ describes sampling error: the gap between your sample and the frame it came from. More data shrinks it.

Frame error does not shrink. If your frame excludes a population, collecting ten times as much data from the same frame excludes them ten times as thoroughly, and it makes your confidence intervals narrower, so you become more confident in a number that is measuring the wrong thing.

This is the single most important idea in the part. Scale improves precision and does nothing for validity, and precision without validity is worse than neither, because it is persuasive.

§3 Coverage, and how you would know

The natural question is how to detect a frame gap. The honest answer is that you generally cannot from the data itself: a dataset cannot tell you what is missing from it.

What you can do is enumerate the axes you care about and check each deliberately.

A coverage checklist that is not a formality
  • Who is represented? Which people, dialects, devices, institutions (and which are not).
  • Under what conditions? Lighting, microphone, season, load. Data collected in one condition rarely transfers.
  • Over what period? A frame is a snapshot. Part XIV returns to what happens when the world moves.
  • What is rare? The cases that matter most (failures, emergencies, fraud) are by construction the ones you have fewest of.
  • What was excluded, and by whom? Filters, quality thresholds and deletions are sampling decisions, and they are usually undocumented.
Why the last item is the one people miss

Collection is scrutinised. Filtering is not, and it is a second sampling step applied after the first.

Dropping records with missing fields selects against whoever is most likely to have missing fields. Removing text a quality classifier scores low removes dialects the classifier was not trained on. Discarding images below a resolution threshold selects for people with better cameras.

Every one of these is defensible in isolation and none of them is recorded as a sampling decision. §17 is about doing it deliberately; the point here is that you have a frame you did not choose unless you wrote the filters down.

§4 Labels are measurements

The supervised setting of Part VI presumes a label $y$ for each input. Where does it come from?

Occasionally from the world: a sensor reading, a recorded outcome, whether the loan was repaid. Usually from a person making a judgement, and a judgement is a measurement, with everything Part V says about measurements.

Label noise, in three kinds

Random. Independent mistakes: attention lapses, misclicks. Symmetric, and §9 shows models tolerate a remarkable amount of it.

Systematic. An annotator, or a guideline, consistently wrong in one direction. This is bias, it does not average out with more annotators drawn the same way, and it is learned by the model as signal.

Irreducible. The label is genuinely ambiguous. Two competent people disagree because the question does not have one answer.

The first is tolerable, the second is dangerous, and the third means your task definition is incomplete. Telling them apart requires measuring, which is §5.

§5 Agreement, and what it does not tell you

Go and read

Aroyo & Welty (2015), Truth is a lie: crowd truth and the seven myths of human annotation. Seven assumptions about annotation, each stated and then dismantled, including the one this section is about, that disagreement is noise to be resolved. The argument is that disagreement is signal about the task, and that collapsing it to a majority vote throws away the most informative thing you collected.

Have several people label the same items and measure how often they agree. This is the standard instrument and it is routinely over-read.

Chance-corrected agreement

Raw agreement is uninterpretable: with two classes at 90/10, two annotators who both always say "majority" agree 81% of the time while conveying nothing.

Cohen's $\kappa$ corrects for that:

$$\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}$$

with $p_o$ the observed agreement and $p_e$ the agreement expected by chance given each annotator's marginal rates. $\kappa = 1$ is perfect, $0$ is chance, and negative is worse than chance.

Pitfall: high agreement is not correctness

Annotators agree when they share a guideline, a training session and a set of assumptions. Those are exactly the conditions under which they share the same systematic error.

So high $\kappa$ tells you the task is well specified and the guideline is being followed. It does not tell you the guideline is right, and it is precisely uninformative about the systematic component of §4, the one that matters most.

Two things do help. Have a separate group label a sample with a different guideline and compare. And check agreement per class: an aggregate $\kappa$ of 0.8 routinely hides a rare class at 0.2, and the rare class is usually the one you care about.

Why disagreement is information rather than noise

The standard move is to take a majority vote and discard the rest. That throws away the most useful signal in the annotation process.

Items with high disagreement are telling you something: the guideline is ambiguous there, or the item is genuinely borderline, or the label set is wrong. Part V §16's point applies (a spread is data, not an inconvenience), and the disagreement rate per item is worth keeping as a column rather than collapsing.

It also gives you the one thing you otherwise lack: an estimate of the irreducible error of Part V §20. If competent annotators agree only 85% of the time, a model scoring 95% against a single annotator's labels is not doing what that number suggests.

§6 The label defines the task

A last observation about labels, and the one with the largest consequences.

You do not have a task and then choose labels for it. The labels are the task. A model learns to predict the thing you recorded, exactly, including everything about how you recorded it.

Worked: three ways to label the same problem

You want to detect "harmful content". Consider three labelling schemes.

One. Ask annotators "is this harmful?": you have built a model of your annotators' aggregate intuition, including whatever they disagree with each other about.

Two. Write a twenty-page guideline and ask "does this violate clause 4.2?": you have built a model of the guideline. Now the guideline is the specification, it can be audited, and it can be changed without relabelling everything from scratch.

Three. Use whether the content was removed by moderators in production: you have built a model of the moderation process, including its backlog, its priorities and whatever it never saw. This is the cheapest option, it produces the most data, and it answers a different question from the one you asked.

Three datasets, three tasks, one problem statement. The choice is not a detail of implementation and it cannot be recovered from the resulting numbers.

Why proxy labels are the most common silent failure

Option three is a proxy: you wanted $y$, you could not measure $y$, so you measured $z$ and hoped it was close.

Proxies are often necessary. What makes them dangerous is that the substitution is invisible downstream: every metric, every plot, every comparison is about $z$, and nothing in the pipeline records that $z$ is not what you wanted.

The habit worth building is to write the substitution down, in the dataset's documentation (§19), in the form: we wanted X, we measured Y, they differ when Z. That sentence is cheap to write once and nearly impossible to reconstruct later.
