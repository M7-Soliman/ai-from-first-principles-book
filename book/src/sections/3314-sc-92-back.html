BuildThe project

Ten to fourteen hours. You produce a complete training plan from arithmetic alone, then run a scaling study small enough to fit on one machine and large enough to catch the methodological error that cost the field four years.

P.1 What you are building

scaleplan: a cost-and-capacity calculator, and a scaling study on a source whose entropy you know.

The rule: every number in the plan is computed before anything is run, and written down. The second half of the project measures how wrong the plan was, which is only possible if it was committed to first.

P.2 Stage 1: the calculator

  1. Parameter count by part, from width, depth, vocabulary and context.
  2. Training and inference FLOPs, and lifetime cost given a serving volume.
  3. Memory: parameters, gradients, optimiser states, activations, with and without a materialised attention matrix, and under each ZeRO stage.
  4. Communication: all-reduce volume, data-parallel efficiency, pipeline bubble.

Deliverable: one function that takes a configuration and returns GiB per device, tokens per second and a bottleneck label. Validate it against the numbers in this part's figures.

P.3 Stage 2: the plan

  1. Pick a device count, memory per device, interconnect bandwidth and compute budget.
  2. Enumerate every (tensor, pipeline, data) factorisation with tensor $\le 8$ and score each.
  3. Choose $N$ and $D$ by the compute-optimal rule, then again by the lifetime rule for a serving volume you specify.
  4. Set batch size, checkpoint interval and precision, with a reason for each.

Deliverable: a one-page plan with a predicted wall-clock, a predicted memory figure, and the sustained-FLOPs fraction you assumed. Commit to it.

P.4 Stage 3: a source with a known floor

  1. Build a synthetic source with compositional structure: conditionals generated by a fixed random teacher network, not drawn independently per context.
  2. Compute its entropy rate exactly, via the stationary distribution over contexts.
  3. Verify: a context-blind model should reach the marginal entropy, and the gap to the entropy rate is the room capacity has to work in. If that gap is small, sharpen the teacher.

Deliverable: the entropy rate, the marginal entropy, and the gap. Also build the independent-Dirichlet version and confirm it produces no power law; that failure is worth seeing, because it shows what the law needs from the data.

P.5 Stage 4: the scaling study

  1. Train across at least two orders of magnitude of model size, tuning the learning rate at every size.
  2. Fit $L = A N^{-\alpha} + E$ with $E$ free. Compare the fitted $E$ to the true entropy.
  3. Re-fit with $E$ pinned to the truth and report how $\alpha$ moves.
  4. Then re-run the whole study with one learning rate for all sizes and report how $\alpha$ moves again.

Deliverable: three exponents and one entropy comparison. Step four is the point: it is the schedule error in miniature, and it should shift your answer measurably.

P.6 Stage 5: the schedule error

  1. Fix a model size. Train at five token budgets spanning at least a factor of sixteen.
  2. Do it twice: schedule matched to each budget, and schedule sized for the longest budget with short runs stopped early.
  3. Plot the penalty against budget.
  4. Fit the allocation rule from each set of runs and report both answers.

Deliverable: the penalty curve, and the two allocation exponents. If the penalty is a constant offset rather than a tilt, say so; it means your setup did not reproduce the mechanism, and that is a finding about your setup worth writing down.

P.7 Stage 6: measure the plan

  1. Run the largest configuration your hardware allows, instrumented per §36.
  2. Compare measured memory to predicted, and measured tokens per second to predicted.
  3. Compute the sustained-FLOPs fraction you actually achieved.

Deliverable: a table of predicted against measured, with the ratio for each. The ratio you got wrong by the largest factor is the term your calculator models worst, and fixing it is the most valuable thing in this project.

P.8 Stretch

  • μP. Sweep the learning rate at several widths, under standard parameterisation and under μP, and plot the optimum against width.
  • An MoE layer, with and without the balancing loss, reporting expert load and drop rate.
  • Emergence. Measure a multi-step task with exact-match and with per-step loss, and produce the sharp curve and the smooth one from the same runs.
  • The gradient noise scale, measured during a run, and the critical batch size it implies.
  • Provoke a loss spike and fix it four ways.

P.9 Done when

  • Your calculator reproduces this part's figures.
  • You committed to a plan before running anything.
  • Your source's entropy rate is computed exactly, and the gap to the marginal is large enough to measure.
  • Your fitted $E$ can be compared to a known truth, and you report the error.
  • You know how much the exponent moves when the learning rate is not tuned per size.
  • You have the penalty curve from the schedule error, and both allocation answers.
  • You have predicted-against-measured for memory and throughput.
  • Your README names the term your calculator got most wrong.
Why this project

Stage 4 and Stage 5 are the reason. Both reproduce, at a scale that fits on one machine, an error that changed how the field spent its compute, and both are procedural rather than conceptual, which is what makes them worth doing rather than reading about.

Both are also where the setup matters most. A source without compositional structure produces no power law at all; one learning rate across sizes makes the largest models look worse than they are; runs far from convergence invert the schedule effect entirely. Stage 3's checks exist so you find those before you fit anything, and knowing the floor is what makes them findable.

The one thing to carry forward

Scale is arithmetic before it is anything else.

Memory is $16N$ bytes plus activations you can compute. The pipeline bubble is $(P-1)/(M+P-1)$. The all-reduce moves $2N$ values per step regardless of batch. Training costs $6N$ FLOPs per token. The compute-optimal split follows from a fitted surface and a constraint. None of it needs hardware to work out, and all of it decides whether a run is possible.

And the second thing: the empirical laws are fits, and a fit inherits every choice made in the runs behind it. The most consequential scaling result of the decade was wrong for four years because of a learning-rate schedule. When you read a law, ask what was held fixed.
