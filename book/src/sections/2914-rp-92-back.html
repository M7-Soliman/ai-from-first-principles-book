BuildThe project

Eight to twelve hours. You build one encoder four ways, and then a set of instruments for finding out what each of them kept, including the instruments that report confidently on representations containing nothing.

P.1 What you are building

repr: four self-supervised encoders on one dataset, and an instrument panel that measures what they hold.

The rule: before each measurement, write down what you expect it to say. This part contains three results that contradict the obvious expectation, and the only way to find out whether you would have caught them is to have committed first.

P.2 Stage 1: the data, with known factors

Do not start with images. Generate data from factors you control, so that "did it keep the right thing" is a question with an answer.

  1. Two or three independent factors: one you will call the signal, the others nuisance.
  2. Push them through a fixed nonlinearity before observing. Skip this and the signal is linearly readable from the raw input, which quietly removes most of what you are trying to measure.
  3. Verify the difficulty: a linear probe on the raw input should be well short of perfect, and a strong nonlinear one should be near it. That gap is the room your representation has to work in.

Deliverable: a generator, plus the two raw-input probe numbers that establish the task is neither trivial nor impossible.

P.3 Stage 2: four encoders

  1. Undercomplete autoencoder. Also fit PCA at the same rank and compare, by principal angles and per-axis cosine.
  2. Denoising autoencoder at a fixed $\sigma$.
  3. Contrastive, InfoNCE, with augmentation that varies the nuisance.
  4. Positives only, which is your collapse control.

Deliverable: four encoders and one table: effective rank, linear probe on the signal, linear probe on the nuisance, for each. Predict the table before you fill it in.

P.4 Stage 3: the score

  1. Use a Gaussian mixture, so the score is available in closed form.
  2. Train the denoiser and compare $(r(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{x})/\sigma^2$ to the analytic score.
  3. Compare against both the smoothed and the unsmoothed score, reporting cosine and magnitude ratio separately for each.
  4. Sweep $\sigma$ across two orders of magnitude and plot both agreements against it.

Deliverable: the four numbers, and a sentence on why the magnitude ratio against the unsmoothed score is the one that goes wrong. If you reported only cosine you would have concluded the identity holds exactly; it does, for a distribution that is not the one you sampled from.

P.5 Stage 4: the instruments

  1. Effective rank, logged during training rather than at the end.
  2. Linear probe, with the regularization strength recorded.
  3. Control task, fixed random label per identity, and selectivity.
  4. Alignment and uniformity, reported as a pair.
  5. Anisotropy, before and after centring.
  6. Hubness: the $N_k$ distribution, its skew, and the orphan fraction.

Deliverable: six functions with a common interface, each documenting what it does not detect. Then run all six on all four encoders, which is the table the rest of the project argues from.

P.6 Stage 5: two instruments that lie

  1. Train the positives-only encoder and log effective rank and probe accuracy together. Then rebuild the data so the signal is linearly readable from the raw input, and repeat. Find the task on which the probe misses the collapse entirely.
  2. Run the control task with a linear probe and with an MLP probe, on the raw input and on each encoder. Find the combination whose selectivity is approximately zero while its accuracy is above 0.95.

Deliverable: two concrete cases, from your own runs, where a standard measurement reports a result that is not true. Write down what you would have concluded from each in isolation.

P.7 Stage 6: superposition

  1. Implement the toy model: $n$ features, $m < n$ dimensions, importance decaying across features.
  2. Sweep sparsity from dense to 99% and count stored features and mean interference.
  3. Find the sparsity at which the count first exceeds $m$.
  4. Then train a wide sparse autoencoder on the toy model's own hidden activations and check whether it recovers the original feature directions.

Deliverable: the count-against-sparsity curve, and (from step four) the fraction of the planted features the sparse autoencoder recovered, plus its dead-feature fraction. This is the smallest honest end-to-end test of the §34 method there is, and it has ground truth, which the real application does not.

P.8 Stretch

  • Many noise levels. Condition the denoiser on $\sigma$, train across a schedule, then sample by following the field while annealing. You have built a diffusion model.
  • Ablate the stop-gradient. Predictor with and without, stop-gradient with and without. Four runs, effective rank for each.
  • Mask ratio. Sweep it on a redundant signal and on a dense one, and find where the task stops being solvable by local interpolation.
  • A real encoder. Take a published pretrained model and run the whole panel on it. Report its anisotropy and its hub statistics.
  • Layer sweep. Probe every layer for a property the pretraining task needed and one it did not.

P.9 Done when

  • Four encoders exist and you predicted the comparison table before filling it in.
  • Your linear autoencoder matches PCA's error and not PCA's axes, and you can say why.
  • You have measured the denoiser against both the smoothed and unsmoothed score.
  • Six instruments exist, each documenting what it cannot see.
  • You have one case where a probe misses a collapse, from your own run.
  • You have one case where a probe's accuracy is high and its selectivity is zero.
  • You know the sparsity at which your toy model starts storing more features than dimensions.
  • Your README names one measurement whose result you predicted wrongly.
Why this project

Stage 5 is the reason. Every other stage builds something that works; that stage establishes that two of the field's standard instruments will report a confident result on a representation that contains nothing, and that you can construct the situation deliberately.

The last checklist item is the second reason. This part's own figures were rebuilt twice because the first version measured something easier than intended: a collapse lag that turned out to be an artifact of a task too simple, and a label-efficiency gap that was small because the signal was linearly available from the start. Both looked fine until the generator was made honest. You should expect at least one of yours to go the same way.

The one thing to carry forward

The objective decides what the representation keeps, and it decides exactly.

An augmentation that varies a factor removes that factor. A loss invariant under rotation fixes the code only up to rotation. A loss with nothing pushing outward collapses to a point. In every case the representation is the exact solution to the problem posed, and every surprise in this part came from the problem being subtly different from the one intended.

So the question to ask of any method here is not whether it produces good features. It is what this objective requires to be preserved and what it permits to be discarded, a question you can answer on paper, before spending the compute.
