Part EBayesian estimation

§17 MAP and MLE

In 60 seconds

Maximum likelihood asks which parameters make the data most probable. Maximum a posteriori asks which parameters are most probable given the data, which by Bayes' rule means multiplying the likelihood by a prior. The prior pulls the estimate toward where you expected it to be, and the pull weakens as data accumulates.

From Part II §21, the posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. Taking logs, as Part 0 §11 always recommends:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \argmax_{\theta} \Big[\underbrace{\log p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)}_{\text{fit the data}} + \underbrace{\log p(\theta)}_{\text{stay plausible}}\Big]$$

Compare with maximum likelihood, which is the first term alone. The prior is the only difference, and it enters as an additive term in exactly the position a regularization penalty occupies.

§18 Priors are regularization

This is the section that ties Part I, Part II and this one together.

Worked: weight decay is a Gaussian prior

Put a zero-mean Gaussian prior on the weights: $p(\mathbf{w}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \tau^2 I)$, expressing a belief that weights are probably small.

Its log, dropping constants that do not depend on $\mathbf{w}$:

$\log p(\mathbf{w}) = -\dfrac{\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2}{2\tau^2} + \text{const}$

Substituting into the MAP objective and flipping the sign to minimise:

$$-\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\mathbf{w}) + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$$

The first term is the cross-entropy or squared error of Part II §26. The second is exactly $L^2$ weight decay, with $\lambda = 1/2\tau^2$.

So the regularization strength is the reciprocal of your prior variance. A strong belief that weights are small (small $\tau$) is a large $\lambda$. This is not an analogy; the two procedures are the same optimisation.

The same substitution with a Laplace prior yields $\|\mathbf{w}\|_1$ and its sparsity, completing the argument begun in Part I §15 and continued in Part II §17. Three parts, three angles (geometric, probabilistic, and now estimation-theoretic) on one phenomenon.

The header's claim runs one way only, and it is worth being precise about which. $L^2$ and $L^1$ above are priors because the penalty is exactly $-\log p(\mathbf{w})$ for a distribution fixed before any data is seen. Not every regularizer has that form. Dropout and data augmentation regularize by acting on the data or the computation, not by adding a fixed $-\log p(\mathbf{w})$ term to the objective, so nothing here converts them into a stated prior. "Priors are regularization" is the correct direction; its converse, that all regularization is secretly a prior, is not claimed.

Why this matters: regularization is a bias you chose

Section 4 said a little bias can reduce total error. Section 18 says where that bias comes from: a prior is a deliberate, stated bias.

That reframing is useful in practice. Choosing $\lambda$ is choosing how strongly to believe your prior against the data. Too large and the prior dominates and you underfit; too small and you are back to maximum likelihood and its variance. Cross-validation, which Part VI covers properly, is the empirical procedure for locating that balance when you cannot reason it out.

§19 Keeping the whole posterior

MAP takes the single most probable parameter setting and discards everything else. The fully Bayesian alternative keeps the entire posterior distribution and averages predictions over it:

$$p(y \mid x, \mathcal{D}) = \int p(y \mid x, \theta)\, p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})\, d\theta$$

Rather than committing to one model, you consult every model weighted by how plausible it is. The immediate benefit is honest uncertainty: where the posterior is broad, predictions disagree, and the spread of that disagreement is a genuine measure of what you do not know.

Pitfall: the integral is the problem

That integral is intractable for anything with more than a handful of parameters, and hopeless for a neural network with millions. This is why full Bayesian treatment remains rare in deep learning despite being the more principled procedure.

The approximations you will meet later (variational inference, Monte Carlo sampling, ensembling, dropout at prediction time) are all attempts to get some of the benefit without the integral. Ensembling is the most honest of them: training several models and averaging is a crude posterior average, and it works for exactly that reason.

Part FThe payoff

§20 The bias–variance decomposition

Go and read

Belkin, Hsu, Ma & Mandal (2019), Reconciling modern machine-learning practice and the classical bias–variance trade-off. Double descent, named. Then Nakkiran et al. (2019), which demonstrates it across modern architectures and finds it in model size, data size and training time alike. These are the papers behind this section's caveat, and they are the honest current state of a question this part cannot close.

Section 4 decomposed the error of estimating a number. This section does the same for a whole predictive model, and the result organises everything in the parts that follow.

The setup: data comes from $y = f(x) + \epsilon$ with noise of variance $\sigma^2$. You fit a model $\hat{f}$ on a random training set. What is your expected squared error at a fixed test point $x$, averaged over training sets?

The decomposition

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\big] \;=\; \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}}$$

The derivation is §4's, with $\hat{f}(x)$ in place of $\hat{\theta}$ and one extra term for the noise, which is independent of everything and simply adds. Three sources of error, and they mean quite different things:

Bias: the error from being too simple
Your model class cannot represent $f$. Averaged over all training sets you still miss. A straight line fitting a curve is biased no matter how much data it gets.
Variance: the error from being too sensitive
Your model changes a lot when the training data changes. It is fitting the noise rather than the signal, and would have fit different noise differently.
Irreducible error: the noise itself
$\sigma^2$ is a property of the world, not of your model. No amount of data, model capacity or cleverness removes it. A model achieving zero error on noisy data has memorised, not learned.
Why this matters: it organises everything after this

Nearly every technique in the remaining parts is a move on one of these two terms.

  • Regularization: accept bias to cut variance (§18).
  • More training data: cuts variance, leaves bias alone.
  • A richer model class: cuts bias, raises variance.
  • Ensembling: averages several models, cutting variance directly.
  • Early stopping: halts before variance grows.
  • Feature engineering: cuts bias if the features are good ones.

When a model performs badly, the first diagnostic question is which term. High training error and high test error means bias: the model cannot represent the problem. Low training error and high test error means variance. The two call for opposite remedies, and applying the wrong one makes things worse.

Where the classical picture breaks

Figure 12 is the textbook story, it is correct as far as it goes, and it does not describe modern deep learning. This is worth stating plainly, because many treatments stop at the U-curve and leave the reader with a model of the world that contradicts what they will observe.

The classical picture predicts that a model with far more parameters than data points should be a catastrophe of variance. Large neural networks have vastly more parameters than training examples and generalise well. That is not a small discrepancy.

Intuition: beyond the threshold, capacity buys choice

At exactly the interpolation threshold there is essentially one way to fit the training data perfectly, and the model is forced into it however contorted it is. That is the peak.

Past the threshold there are infinitely many perfect fits, and the optimiser gets to pick among them. It picks a smooth one, minimum-norm, or whatever the training procedure implicitly prefers. Extra capacity stops being extra freedom to fit noise and starts being extra freedom to choose a well-behaved solution.

The bias–variance identity is still exactly true; it is an identity. What fails is the assumption that variance rises monotonically with parameter count. It does not, once the model can interpolate.

Try it: the last one

Your model gets 2% training error and 25% test error. Which term dominates, and what do you do? Now instead it gets 24% training and 25% test. Same questions.

Answer. First case: the gap is the diagnosis; it fits the training data and not the test data, so variance dominates. Remedies: more data, stronger regularization, a smaller model, ensembling, early stopping. Adding capacity would make it worse.

Second case: it fails on both, and roughly equally. That is bias: the model cannot represent the problem, and it is not overfitting anything. Remedies: a richer model class, better features, training longer, weaker regularization. Here more data buys almost nothing, which is the counter-intuitive part and the reason the diagnosis is worth making before spending money on labels.

One caveat worth carrying: if the irreducible noise is itself around 24%, the second model is already optimal and no remedy exists. Knowing $\sigma^2$ (or having a human baseline that estimates it) is what tells the difference between a model that is underfitting and one that has finished.
