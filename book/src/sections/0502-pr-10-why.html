Part AWhy probability

§1 Three kinds of uncertainty

History: probability began as an argument about an interrupted game

Mathematics ignored chance for most of its history. Games of chance were thousands of years old, but the idea that uncertainty could be calculated arrived remarkably late.

The usual starting point is 1654, and a question put to Blaise Pascal by a gambler, Antoine Gombaud. Two players are partway through a game of chance for a pot of money when the game must be abandoned. One is ahead. How should the pot be divided? The old answer split it by rounds already won, which is obviously unfair to a player about to catch up.

Pascal wrote to Pierre de Fermat and the two worked it out in an exchange of letters over that summer. Their insight was that the fair split depends not on what has already happened but on the possible futures: enumerate the ways the remaining rounds could go, and divide the pot in proportion. That is expected value, and it is §10 of this part.

Girolamo Cardano had reached much of this a century earlier, being a compulsive gambler with mathematical training, but his manuscript went unpublished for about a hundred years and had no influence on anyone.

So the field that now underwrites insurance, statistical physics, and the loss function of every classifier began with two men trying to settle a bet.

Computer science normally deals in certainty. A sorted list is sorted. A hash lookup either finds the key or does not. Machine learning breaks this, and it is worth being precise about why, because the three reasons call for the same tool but come from very different places.

1. The system is genuinely random

Some processes have no hidden answer to uncover. Quantum measurements are the textbook case; an idealised coin flip is the everyday one. There is nothing more to learn, because there is nothing more to know. This is sometimes called aleatoric uncertainty, from the Latin for dice.

2. We cannot observe everything

The coin is not really random. Given the exact force, spin, air density and table elasticity, the outcome is determined. But we do not have those numbers and never will, so from where we stand the outcome behaves as if random. Most uncertainty in the physical world is of this kind: deterministic underneath, unobservable in practice.

3. Our model is simpler than reality

This is the one that matters most for machine learning, and it is the least discussed. Suppose you predict house prices from square footage alone. Two identical houses sell for different amounts, because price also depends on the neighbours, the kitchen, the season, and the buyer's mood. Your model is not wrong so much as incomplete, and incompleteness looks exactly like randomness from inside the model. This is epistemic uncertainty, and unlike the first kind, more data or a better model can reduce it.

Intuition: why a simple uncertain model beats a complex certain one

You could try to eliminate case 3 by modelling everything: every buyer, every neighbour, every mood. Nobody does, for two reasons. Such a model would need impossible amounts of data to fit, and it would be so specific to the past that it would fail on anything new: the overfitting problem of Part V §20.

So the field makes a trade that is worth naming explicitly: use a simple model and be honest about the uncertainty it leaves behind. Probability is the language for that honesty. A model that says "probably about \$400k, give or take \$50k" is more useful, and more truthful, than one that insists on \$412,388.

§2 Two schools, and why the argument matters

History: the argument was genuinely bitter

The frequentist–Bayesian split is presented today as a difference of interpretation. For most of the twentieth century it was a professional feud.

Ronald Fisher, who built much of modern statistics in the 1920s and 30s, regarded Bayesian reasoning as an error to be stamped out, the objection being that a prior is somebody's opinion, and opinions have no business inside a scientific calculation. His attacks on the approach, and on the people who used it, were sustained and personal.

The Bayesian reply was that the prior is not smuggled in; it is stated openly, and the alternative is not objectivity but an assumption nobody has bothered to write down. Harold Jeffreys, and later Savage, Jaynes and de Finetti, argued that consistent reasoning under uncertainty forces the Bayesian rules on you.

Two things ended the war rather than settling it. Andrey Kolmogorov's 1933 axiomatisation showed that both camps use the same mathematics and disagree only about what it refers to. And computers arrived: Bayesian methods require integrals that are hopeless by hand and routine by simulation, so the practical objection dissolved.

Machine learning inherited the truce and is cheerfully unprincipled about it, as this section describes.

There is a genuine philosophical disagreement about what a probability is, and unusually for philosophy, it has practical consequences you will run into.

The frequentist view: probability is a long-run rate

"This coin lands heads with probability 0.5" means: flip it forever and the fraction of heads approaches 0.5. Probability is a property of a repeatable experiment, measured by counting.

Clean, objective, and testable. But it cannot express many statements we obviously want to make. What is the probability that this patient has the flu? You cannot rerun the patient a thousand times. The event happens once.

The Bayesian view: probability is a degree of belief

Here a probability quantifies how strongly you believe something, given what you know. "0.7 chance this patient has the flu" is a statement about your state of knowledge, not about a repeatable experiment. New evidence updates the number, which is exactly what Bayes' rule in §21 does.

The striking result, and the reason this is not merely a matter of taste, is that if you want your degrees of belief to be internally consistent (no combination of bets that loses you money no matter the outcome) you are forced to obey the ordinary rules of probability. The same axioms fall out of both interpretations.

Why deep learning cares

Deep learning is cheerfully pragmatic and uses both. When we write $p(y \mid x)$ for a classifier's output, we mean a degree of belief about this image, which is Bayesian. When we evaluate accuracy over a test set, we mean a long-run rate, which is frequentist. The field mixes them without ceremony, and you should feel free to as well, but knowing which one a given sentence means will save you real confusion when reading papers.

§3 Random variables

Definition: random variable

A random variable is a variable whose value is an outcome of a random process. Written with a capital, $X$; a specific value it takes is written lowercase, $x$.

That capital-versus-lowercase distinction is quietly doing a lot of work, and it is worth slowing down for. $X$ is the whole uncertain quantity: the die, before it is thrown. $x$ is one particular value: the 4 that came up. So:

$$P(X = 4) = \tfrac{1}{6}$$

reads "the probability that the random variable $X$ takes the value 4". Mixing these up produces sentences that look fine and mean nothing, and it is the single most common notational error in a first probability course.

Pitfall: a random variable is not a variable

Despite the name, $X$ is not a placeholder awaiting a value. Formally it is a function, mapping outcomes to numbers. Roll two dice: the outcome is the pair that landed, and $X$ might be the function "add them up", giving a number from 2 to 12. Different functions of the same outcome are different random variables: $Y$ could be "the larger of the two".

You will not need the formal definition to do anything in this chapter. But knowing that $X$ is a function of the underlying outcome makes it much clearer why two random variables can be dependent: they can be reading the same underlying event in two different ways.

Discrete and continuous

Random variables come in two flavours, and nearly everything in Part B is stated twice because of it:

  • Discrete: takes values from a countable set. A die roll, a coin flip, the class label of an image, the number of words in a sentence.
  • Continuous: takes values from a continuous range. A temperature, a pixel intensity treated as real-valued, a network weight, tomorrow's stock price.

The distinction is not pedantry: the two cases need genuinely different mathematics, and the continuous case has a trap in it that §5 is entirely devoted to.

Try it

Classify each as discrete or continuous: (a) the number of emails you get tomorrow, (b) how long you wait for a bus, (c) which of 1,000 categories an image belongs to, (d) a neural network's confidence score.

Answer. (a) discrete: a count, $0, 1, 2, \dots$; (b) continuous: any non-negative real; (c) discrete: one of 1,000 labels, and note the labels need not be numbers at all; (d) continuous: any value in $[0,1]$.

Case (c) is the shape of essentially every classification problem in deep learning, and case (d) is what the model actually outputs. A classifier is a machine that takes continuous inputs and produces a distribution over a discrete set, so you need both halves of this chapter to describe even one forward pass.
