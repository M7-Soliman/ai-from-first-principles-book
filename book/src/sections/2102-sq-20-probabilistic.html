Part BWhat it is, probabilistically

Four sections that cost nothing computationally and change what the model means. The recurrent network turns out to be a graphical model with a remarkable parameterisation, a standard training trick turns out to be maximum likelihood rather than a hack, and both readings expose an assumption that nobody states.

§7 The RNN as a graphical model

In 60 seconds

Take the chain rule of probability from Part II §8 and write the joint distribution over a sequence as a product of conditionals. Done directly, that needs $O(k^\tau)$ numbers. An RNN represents the same full dependency structure, every past value may influence every future one, with a number of parameters that does not grow with $\tau$ at all.

Consider a sequence of discrete values with no inputs, where each step's input is the previous step's output. Part II §8's chain rule factorises the joint exactly:

$$P(\mathbf{y}) = P(y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(\tau)}) = \prod_{t=1}^{\tau} P\big(y^{(t)} \mid y^{(t-1)},\ldots,y^{(1)}\big)$$

This is an identity, not a model: no independence has been assumed. The negative log-likelihood is $L = \sum_t L^{(t)}$, which is the loss of §4, so an RNN trained on next-item prediction is doing maximum likelihood on the whole sequence.

Worked: what the parameterisation costs, three ways

A full table. Store one number per possible assignment. With $k$ values and length $\tau$ that is $O(k^\tau)$ entries. At $k=10$, $\tau=20$: $10^{20}$.

A Markov assumption. Keep only edges from the last $m$ steps. Now it is $O(k^{m+1})$, independent of $\tau$ (affordable), and it has thrown away every dependency longer than $m$. This is the $n$-gram model, and its limitation is exactly §1's.

An RNN. Introduce $\mathbf{h}^{(t)}$ as an intermediate quantity between past and future. Every $y^{(i)}$ can influence every later $y^{(t)}$ through $\mathbf{h}$, so the dependency structure is the complete graph: nothing has been assumed away. And because the same conditional distribution is used at every step, the parameter count is $O(1)$ in $\tau$.

Why the hidden state is doing the work

It is worth being clear about where the saving comes from, because it is not obvious that you can have full connectivity for free.

You cannot. The RNN represents the complete graph, but it does not represent every distribution on that graph: the influence of $y^{(1)}$ on $y^{(20)}$ has to be carried by a fixed-size vector, and §2 already established that vector is lossy. The table can express any joint distribution; the RNN can express those whose long-range structure fits through $\mathbf{h}$.

So the comparison in Figure 1 is not "the same thing for fewer parameters". It is a hypothesis about which sequences occur, bought at a price of $O(k^\tau)$ to $O(1)$, which is Part X §11's infinitely strong prior, in a different currency. Part X forbade non-shared spatial weights; this forbids any dependency that will not fit through a bottleneck.

§8 The assumption nobody states

Sharing $f$ across time assumes something specific, and it is worth writing down because it is false for many real sequences.

Stationarity

The conditional distribution of the variables at $t+1$ given those at $t$ does not depend on $t$. The rule relating one step to the next is the same rule at step 3 and at step 3,000.

For language that is roughly true, which is why the models work. For a financial series with a regime change, a recording with a scene cut, or a process with a diurnal cycle, it is false in a way that matters, and the model has no way to express that it is false, because it has one $f$.

The obvious repair is to feed $t$ itself as an extra input at each step, letting the model learn whatever time-dependence exists while still sharing everything else. That is strictly better than a separate distribution per $t$, and it has a defect: the model must now extrapolate to values of $t$ it never saw, and Part VI §5's i.i.d. assumption gives no guarantee at all about that. Any reader who has watched a model behave strangely beyond its training sequence length has met this.

Why this is Part X's question, asked of time

Part X §11 closed with: what are the symmetries of this task, and which does my architecture already know? A convolutional layer knows translation in space. A recurrent layer knows translation in time: that is precisely what stationarity says.

Which makes the two parts the same argument on different axes, and it makes the failure modes rhyme. Convolution is wrong when the same feature means different things in different places; recurrence is wrong when the same transition means different things at different times. In both cases the model cannot tell you it is wrong; it simply fits worse and you have to know to look.

§9 Teacher forcing

A model with output-to-hidden recurrence (§5) has a choice during training: feed back its own prediction, or feed back the correct answer from the training set. The second is called teacher forcing, and it is usually described as a training trick. It is not: it falls directly out of maximum likelihood.

Worked: teacher forcing is what the likelihood asks for

Take two steps and factorise the conditional likelihood with Part II §8's chain rule:

$$\log p\big(y^{(1)}, y^{(2)} \mid \mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \mathbf{x}^{(2)}\big) = \log p\big(y^{(2)} \mid y^{(1)}, \mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \mathbf{x}^{(2)}\big) + \log p\big(y^{(1)} \mid \mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \mathbf{x}^{(2)}\big)$$

Read the first term. At $t=2$ the model is asked for the probability of $y^{(2)}$ given the true $y^{(1)}$, not given whatever it happened to predict. The conditioning variable is the observed value.

So maximum likelihood specifies that the recurrent connections be fed with the targets during training. Feeding back the model's own output would be optimising a different objective.

It also parallelises, for §5's reason: with the true $y^{(t)}$ supplied, step $t+1$ does not wait for step $t$. That is a large practical win and it is a consequence rather than the motivation.

§10 Exposure bias, and how a sequence ends

Teacher forcing has a failure mode that is entirely predictable once stated, and it took the field some years to state it.

Pitfall: the model never sees its own mistakes

At training time the model is always conditioned on a correct prefix. At generation time it is conditioned on its own output, which contains errors.

So the input distribution at test time is one the model never trained on. And it compounds: one slightly wrong token produces a prefix slightly off the training manifold, which produces a worse next token, and the trajectory drifts away from anything the model has an opinion about. This is exposure bias, and it is why free-running generation from an otherwise well-trained model can degrade into repetition or nonsense after a few dozen steps.

Two mitigations. Train partly on free-running inputs, so the model sees its own outputs and learns to recover: predict the correct target several steps ahead through the output-to-input path, so it learns to steer back. Or use a curriculum: begin fully teacher-forced and randomly substitute generated values with increasing probability, which is Part IX §28's stochastic curriculum applied here, and note that the stochastic form is the one that was found to work there too.

One more piece of bookkeeping the equations do not supply: when does generation stop? The model produces one item per step and nothing in it says how many steps there are. Three answers are used.

Three ways to end a sequence

An end-of-sequence symbol. Add an extra token to the vocabulary and put it in the training data after the last real item. Generation stops when it is emitted. Simple, and only available when the output is symbolic.

A Bernoulli continue-or-stop output. An extra sigmoid unit at each step, trained with cross-entropy on whether the sequence ends here. More general: it works for a sequence of real numbers, where there is no vocabulary to extend.

Predict $\tau$ directly. Factorise as $P(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(\tau)}) = P(\tau)\,P(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(\tau)} \mid \tau)$: sample the length, then generate that many items. This needs the remaining count $\tau - t$ fed in at each step, or the model has no idea it is near the end, and sequences that end abruptly, mid-sentence, are the signature of having forgotten to do it.
