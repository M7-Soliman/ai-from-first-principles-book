PracticeDrills

Thirty-seven problems. More of them are instrumentation than algebra, because this part's central claim (that you should measure before you change anything) is only credible if you have actually built the instruments.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · What the optimiser costs

You are training a model with Adam in float32. Write down how many bytes per parameter the optimiser state costs, and what the total memory is as a multiple of the model.

Answer. 8 bytes, a first and second moment each, making 3× the parameter memory once the weights themselves are counted, and 4× with gradients. This is why the largest model that fits is much smaller than the memory divided by four bytes, and it is the first thing to compute when a run will not fit. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

WarmW1 · Three quantities

Name the three quantities of §1 and say which one your optimiser can evaluate.

Answer. The performance measure $P$ you care about; the risk $J^*$ under the true distribution; the empirical risk $J$ over the training set. The optimiser can evaluate only the third, and even that it replaces with a surrogate.
WarmW2 · Why not just minimise the errors

Why can gradient descent not minimise classification error directly?

Answer. The 0–1 loss is piecewise constant: its derivative is zero wherever it exists and undefined at the jumps. Every step sees a slope of zero. Nor is a cleverer algorithm waiting: exact minimisation of expected 0–1 loss is intractable even for a linear classifier.
WarmW3 · The step ceiling

You measure $\mathbf{g}^\top\mathbf{g} = 0.5$ and $\mathbf{g}^\top H\mathbf{g} = 40$. What is the largest step size that still reduces the cost?

Answer. $\epsilon < 2(0.5)/40 = 0.025$. Above that the curvature term outweighs the gradient term and the step increases the cost, however correct the direction.
WarmW4 · Batch size and noise

Going from $b = 64$ to $b = 1024$: what does it cost, and what does it buy?

Answer. 16× the compute and 16× the memory, for a $\sqrt{16} = 4$× reduction in gradient noise. Stated that way it rarely sounds worth it, which is §5's point.
WarmW5 · How many arrangements

A network has three hidden layers of 64 units. How many parameter settings compute the identical function by permutation alone?

Answer. $64!^{\,3} \approx 10^{267}$. Every one of them is a distinct local minimum with exactly the same cost, which is why counting local minima says nothing about difficulty.
WarmW6 · The negative test

You suspect a local minimum. What single measurement settles it, and in which direction?

Answer. The gradient norm. If it is not small you are at no critical point and every such explanation is eliminated. If it is small you have learned almost nothing: the test rules out, it does not rule in.
WarmW7 · Clip by norm or by value

Why is clipping the gradient's norm preferable to clamping each coordinate?

Answer. Rescaling the whole vector preserves the direction exactly and changes only the distance travelled, which is the quantity that was in doubt. Clamping coordinates separately rotates the direction; you end up stepping somewhere the gradient never pointed.
WarmW8 · Which init, forward or backward

A layer has $m = 1024$ inputs and $n = 64$ outputs. Give $s^2$ under fan-in, fan-out and Glorot, and say what each preserves.

Answer. $1/1024$, $1/64$, and $2/1088 \approx 1/544$. Fan-in preserves the forward activation variance, fan-out the backward gradient variance, and Glorot preserves neither but is wrong by less in both directions.
WarmW9 · The output bias

A binary problem is 99% negative. What should the output bias be, and what does setting it save?

Answer. $\log(0.01/0.99) \approx -4.6$, so that the initial prediction is the base rate. It saves the opening phase in which the loss drops sharply and the model has learned nothing but the class frequencies.
WarmW10 · Why drop the bias

Why does a linear layer followed by batch normalisation not need its own bias?

Answer. The bias shifts the mean, the normalisation subtracts the mean, and $\beta$ adds one back. It is exactly redundant with $\beta$, two parameters for one degree of freedom.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Derive the ceiling

From the second-order expansion, derive $\epsilon < 2\mathbf{g}^\top\mathbf{g}/\mathbf{g}^\top H\mathbf{g}$ and say what it means when $H$ has a negative eigenvalue along $\mathbf{g}$.

Answer. The step changes the cost by $-\epsilon\mathbf{g}^\top\mathbf{g} + \tfrac12\epsilon^2\mathbf{g}^\top H\mathbf{g}$; requiring the second term to be smaller gives the bound. If $\mathbf{g}^\top H\mathbf{g} < 0$ the bound is vacuous: the curvature is helping, and a larger step is better, which is the situation on the far side of a saddle.
CoreC2 · Build the instrument

Implement $\mathbf{g}^\top H\mathbf{g}$ by finite differences of the gradient. Verify it against a Hessian-vector product from double backpropagation on a small model.

Answer. $(\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta} + h\mathbf{g}) - \nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}))^\top\mathbf{g}/h$, with $h$ scaled by $1/\|\mathbf{g}\|$ so the perturbation has a fixed size. They agree to several digits over a wide range of $h$, with cancellation error taking over below about $\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{mach}}}$, Part III §3, in a setting where it bites.
CoreC3 · Watch the ceiling fall

Log $\|\mathbf{g}\|$ and $\mathbf{g}^\top H\mathbf{g}$ every epoch of a real run. Plot the implied maximum step size against your actual learning rate.

Answer. On the run behind Figure 3 the ceiling fell from 0.63 to 0.116 while the rate stayed at 0.05, from twelvefold headroom to twofold. If your curves cross, you have found why the run destabilised, and no amount of restarting will fix it.
CoreC4 · The gradient norm does not shrink

Train a small network on a task it cannot fit exactly and plot the gradient norm across training. What happens, and why does it not contradict "training succeeded"?

Answer. It rises, 4.3× in Figure 3, while validation error fell from 57% to 5%. There is no contradiction because the network never approaches a critical point: success is measured on held-out data, not by the gradient going to zero.
CoreC5 · The scaling hyperbola

Fit $\hat{y} = w_2\,\text{ReLU}(w_1 x)$ and evaluate the loss along $w_1 w_2 = c$. How much does it vary? Now add $\alpha\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ and find the minimum.

Answer. It varies by a relative $10^{-15}$, machine noise; the models are exactly equivalent. With the penalty the minimum becomes the unique point on the hyperbola closest to the origin, which for a symmetric problem is near $w_1 = w_2$. Weight decay is selecting a parameterisation, not just shrinking one.
CoreC6 · Build a cliff

Take $J(w) = \tfrac12(w^L - 1)^2$ for $L = 6$. Find a point where one gradient step at a reasonable learning rate increases the loss by six orders of magnitude, then repeat with clipping.

Answer. From $w = 1.45$ at $\epsilon = 0.02$ the step lands at $w = -4.93$ and the loss goes from 34 to $1.03\times10^8$. Clipping the norm at 1.0 lands at $w = 1.43$ with the loss at 28.5, the same direction, a survivable distance.
CoreC7 · Powers of a matrix

Take a random $W$ with spectral radius 1.1 and another with 0.9. Plot $\|\mathbf{x}^\top W^t\|$ against $t$, and the angle between $\mathbf{x}^\top W^t$ and the principal eigenvector.

Answer. The norms diverge and vanish exponentially, and in both cases the angle goes to zero; the direction converges to the principal eigenvector regardless. That is the power method, and it is why a recurrent network forgets its input.
CoreC8 · Batch size and the Newton step

On an ill-conditioned quadratic, measure the relative error of $\hat{\mathbf{g}}$ and of $H^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}$ against batch size, with $H$ computed exactly. Explain the gap.

Answer. Both fall like $1/\sqrt{b}$ and the second sits about a decade above the first, Figure 2 measured 10× at $\kappa \approx 10^4$. Multiplying by an ill-conditioned matrix multiplies relative error by up to $\kappa$, so the same gradient noise produces a far noisier step even with $H$ known exactly.
CoreC9 · Nesterov by hand

Implement momentum and Nesterov momentum on the same ill-conditioned quadratic with the same $\epsilon$ and $\alpha$. Report iterations to reach a fixed tolerance.

Answer. On the setup behind Figure 6, 104 against 49 to reach 0.01. Keep $\epsilon\lambda_{\max} < 1$, at the edge of stability the gap flatters Nesterov, and a measurement taken there overstates the method.
CoreC10 · Glorot's compromise, measured

Build a chain of linear layers whose widths halve. Measure forward activation variance and backward gradient variance per layer under fan-in, fan-out and Glorot.

Answer. Fan-in holds the forward flat and loses 230× backward; fan-out is the mirror image; Glorot is off by 8.6× and 29×. Its worst error is an order of magnitude below either specialist's, Figure 7. Alternating widths instead of a funnel makes the effect cancel and measures nothing.
CoreC11 · The initialisation protocol

Implement §17's loop: push one batch through a fresh deep network, find the first layer whose activations have collapsed, raise its scale, repeat.

Answer. A few passes usually suffice. The point is the cost: feedback comes from the untrained model on a single batch, not from a trained model on a validation set, so it is seconds rather than hours, and it should be the first thing you try on a deep network that will not train.
CoreC12 · Conjugate gradients

Implement CG with Polak–Ribière and steepest descent with exact line search on a 12-dimensional quadratic. Compare at $\kappa = 10$ and $\kappa = 1000$.

Answer. At $\kappa = 10$, CG hits $10^{-13}$ at exactly iteration 12. At $\kappa = 1000$ the finite-termination property is destroyed by rounding: the residual at step 12 is still 0.44, and it takes 17 steps to reach $10^{-10}$. The theorem is about exact arithmetic.
CoreC13 · Why the zigzag is forced

Prove that consecutive steepest-descent directions with exact line search are orthogonal, and explain why that is the problem rather than a symptom.

Answer. At the line minimum the directional derivative along $\mathbf{d}_{t-1}$ is zero, and the next direction is that gradient, so $\mathbf{d}_t \perp \mathbf{d}_{t-1}$. It is the problem because moving along $\mathbf{d}_t$ then leaves the minimum along $\mathbf{d}_{t-1}$, so later iterations must redo work already done.
CoreC14 · Damped Newton as a dial

Show that $[H + \alpha I]^{-1}\nabla J \to \nabla J/\alpha$ as $\alpha$ grows, and say what that implies about fixing Newton's method near strong negative curvature.

Answer. $H + \alpha I \to \alpha I$, so the update becomes gradient descent with rate $1/\alpha$. Damping therefore interpolates between Newton and gradient descent, and where the curvature is strongly negative, the $\alpha$ needed for safety makes the step smaller than a well-tuned gradient step, at $O(k^3)$ per iteration.
CoreC15 · Batch norm's learning-rate range

Sweep the learning rate over four decades for a deep network with and without batch normalisation, using the same initialisation for both. Report the range of rates that train and the best loss each reaches.

Answer. Figure 9: a factor of 8 without, 748 with, a 98-fold widening. And the best loss was lower without (0.019 against 0.057). Normalisation buys robustness to the learning rate, not a better model, and using a bad initialisation for the control arm is how that distinction gets lost.
CoreC16 · Coordinate descent's failure

Run exact coordinate descent on $(x_1-x_2)^2 + \alpha(x_1^2+x_2^2)$ for $\alpha \in \{1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001\}$. Count sweeps to $10^{-6}$.

Answer. 11, 73, 695 and 6,912. Each sweep multiplies the pair by $1/(1+\alpha)^2$, so the count grows like $1/\alpha$. Newton solves every one of them in a single step, because the coupling is exactly what a second-order method sees and coordinate descent cannot.
CoreC17 · Averaging the trajectory

Run SGD on a noisy ill-conditioned quadratic. Compare the final iterate, the running mean and an exponential moving average by distance to the optimum.

Answer. Figure 10: 0.843, 0.078 and 0.293. The plain mean wins because the problem is convex and stationary; on a non-convex problem it would be averaging in points from regions the optimiser has left, which is what the exponential form gives up in order to avoid.
CoreC18 · Continuation, end to end

Take a one-dimensional objective with several local minima. Blur it at decreasing $\sigma$ and run local descent on each, starting from the previous solution. Compare with plain descent from the same point.

Answer. On Figure 11's objective, plain descent from $\theta_0 = 5.5$ stops at $J = 1.40$ while continuation reaches the global $J = -2.375$. Verify the failure case too: blur $-\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\theta}^\top\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and confirm the curvature does not move, because convolving a quadratic with a Gaussian only shifts it.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · The surrogate earns its keep

Fit logistic regression on separable data. Find the iteration where training 0–1 error reaches zero and the iteration where test error is best. Then add pure-noise coordinates and repeat.

Answer. Figure 1: the best test error came 31× later on the clean problem, a 9% relative gain that the 0–1 loss could not have asked for. With ten noise coordinates the ordering reverses: best at iteration 80, and 46% worse by the end. Nothing in the training curve distinguishes the two runs, which is the whole argument for a validation set.
StretchS2 · The learning rate is higher than it looks

Sweep $\epsilon_0$, recording the loss after 100 iterations and after a full run. Plot both against $\epsilon_0$. Do the two optima coincide?

Answer. They should not: the full-run optimum sits at a higher rate than the 100-iteration optimum, which is §13's warning made concrete. If they do coincide, your run is too short for the curvature growth of §6 to have taken effect; extend it and look again.
StretchS3 · Batch norm on one example

Feed a single example through a batch-normalised network in training mode, then in evaluation mode. Explain both outcomes from §23.

Answer. Training mode raises: the standard deviation of one example does not exist, and refusing is correct. Evaluation mode works, because it uses the stored running statistics rather than the batch's. The two modes are not a convenience; they are forced by the fact that the training-time operation is defined over a batch.
StretchS4 · Averaging meets normalisation

Apply Polyak averaging to a network containing batch normalisation, first without recomputing the normalisation statistics and then with. Measure both.

Answer. Without recomputation the averaged model is usually worse than the un-averaged one, sometimes far worse. The averaged weights are a point the model never occupied, so the running $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ belong to different weights entirely. One pass of the training data in training mode fixes it, and this is the most common reason the technique gets tried once and abandoned.
StretchS5 · Hints on a middle layer

Train a wide shallow teacher, then a deep thin student on the task alone, then the same student with an added loss regressing the teacher's middle layer. Report training as well as test error for all three.

Answer. The unaided student does poorly on both, which is the informative part: poor test error alone would suggest overfitting or capacity, but poor training error says the function was representable and the optimiser could not find it. The hint removes an optimisation obstacle, not a representational one.
StretchS6 · A curriculum that works and one that does not

On a task with an easy/hard split, compare uniform sampling, a deterministic easy-to-hard ordering, and a stochastic curriculum whose proportion of hard examples rises over time.

Answer. The deterministic ordering is the one most people implement and it has been measured to give no improvement over uniform sampling. The stochastic version (always a mix, with the mixture shifting) is the one that helps. Report all three; the negative result is the useful half.
StretchS7 · The whole ladder

Deliberately break a training run in four ways: bad initialisation scale, learning rate above the ceiling, an untuned output bias on an imbalanced problem, and four regularisers at once, and confirm that §29's ladder identifies each from measurement alone.

Answer. Each is caught by a different rung, and none is caught by switching optimiser. The exercise is worth doing once because the four failures produce similar-looking loss curves, and after this you will stop reading loss curves as though they were diagnostic.
StretchS8 · L-BFGS where it belongs

Fit a small model full-batch with L-BFGS and with Adam, matching wall-clock rather than iterations. Then try L-BFGS with minibatches and explain what goes wrong.

Answer. On a small full-batch problem L-BFGS wins decisively, its closure was called eight times in a single step() in §21's example, taking the loss from 2.54 to $7\times10^{-3}$. With minibatches the line search is minimising a function that changes between evaluations, so the search is meaningless and the curvature estimates built from gradient differences are noise. L-BFGS is a full-batch method.
