PracticeDrills

Thirty-four problems. The Tier 2 set asks you to implement every algorithm in this part from its definition, which is the fastest way to discover that most of them are about fifteen lines.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · The interval on an accuracy

A model scores 90% on 1,000 test examples. Write down the half-width of its 95% confidence interval before computing it.

Answer. ±1.86 percentage points. So 90% on 1,000 examples and 91.5% on 1,000 examples are the same result. Quote this interval every time you quote an accuracy, and half the arguments about model choice end before they start. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

WarmW1 · Name the three

For "predict tomorrow's temperature from today's weather", identify the task, the experience and the performance measure.

Answer. Task: regression. Experience: historical weather records with next-day temperatures. Performance: squared or absolute error on held-out days, noting that absolute error is the better choice if being 10° wrong is exactly twice as bad as being 5° wrong.
WarmW2 · Supervised or not

Classify: (a) grouping customers by behaviour (b) predicting churn from history (c) predicting the next word in a sentence.

Answer. (a) unsupervised. (b) supervised. (c) technically unsupervised data (raw text, no labels) turned into a supervised task by hiding the next word. That is self-supervision, and it is how language models are trained.
WarmW3 · Which error is optimistic

Training error or test error, and why?

Answer. Training error, by construction: the parameters were chosen to make it small. It is a biased estimate of risk for exactly the reason a search-selected maximum is biased (Part V §14).
WarmW4 · Diagnose

Training 2%, test 3%. Training 20%, test 21%. Training 1%, test 18%. Which is which?

Answer. First: good fit. Second: underfitting, small gap but both high. Third: overfitting, small training error, large gap.
WarmW5 · Broken i.i.d.

You shuffle a time series before splitting into train and test. What goes wrong?

Answer. The model trains on the future and is tested on the past. Accuracy is excellent and the model is worthless in deployment, where the future is not available. Split on time, always.
WarmW6 · The k in kNN

Does larger $k$ increase or decrease capacity?

Answer. Decrease. $k=1$ follows every point exactly: maximum capacity. Large $k$ averages over many neighbours and smooths. The dial runs backwards.
WarmW7 · No free lunch

Does the theorem mean all algorithms are equally good in practice?

Answer. No. It says they are equal averaged over all possible target functions, nearly all of which are structureless noise. Real problems are a tiny structured subset, and matching an algorithm's assumptions to that structure is the whole game.
WarmW8 · Counting cells

Ten bins per axis. How many cells in 20 dimensions? How does that compare to any real dataset?

Answer. $10^{20}$. Larger than any dataset that has ever existed by many orders of magnitude, and 20 dimensions is small. This is §21.
WarmW9 · Validation versus test

You pick a learning rate using the test set. What have you lost?

Answer. The test set. It now participated in a decision, so its score is optimistic and no longer estimates generalisation. Use validation for every choice.
WarmW10 · Intrinsic dimension

Images are 784-dimensional. Does that mean learning needs $10^{784}$ examples?

Answer. No: that is the ambient dimension. Real images lie near a far lower-dimensional manifold, so the effective difficulty is set by the intrinsic dimension, not the pixel count. §23.

Tier 2 Core: implement everything

CoreC1 · Linear regression, three ways

Fit by normal equations, by your Part III QR solver, and by gradient descent. Compare answers and timings as $\kappa$ grows.

Answer. All agree when well conditioned. As $\kappa$ rises the normal equations degrade first (Part III §20, now on a learning problem). Gradient descent is slowest per digit but the only one that scales to large $n$.
CoreC2 · Ridge path

Sweep $\lambda$ over seven orders of magnitude. Plot coefficients and test error.

Answer. You reproduce Figure 4. Note the test-error minimum is at a strictly positive $\lambda$: the biased estimator wins, as Part V §4 promised.
CoreC3 · Logistic regression from scratch

Implement the sigmoid, the cross-entropy loss, its gradient, and gradient descent. Verify the gradient with your Part 0 gradient checker.

Answer. The gradient is $X^\top(\sigma(Xw) - y)/n$, strikingly the same form as linear regression's, which is not a coincidence but a property of exponential-family models. Use the stable sigmoid from Part III.
CoreC4 · Capacity curve

Reproduce Figure 1: polynomial degree against training and test error.

Answer. Training error falls monotonically; test error is a U. Try it with 200 training points instead of 18 and watch the U flatten, as more data makes capacity safer.
CoreC5 · Learning curves

Plot error against training set size for a high-bias and a high-variance model.

Answer. Figure 2. The diagnostic value: if both curves have plateaued together at a high value, more data is wasted money. If there is a persistent gap that is still closing, more data is the cheapest available fix.
CoreC6 · k-fold cross-validation

Implement it. Use it to select polynomial degree. Compare against a single holdout.

Answer. CV selects more consistently across repeats, because the estimate averages $k$ folds. A single small holdout picks a different degree depending on which points landed in it; that instability is the variance CV removes.
CoreC7 · Nested CV

Select with an inner loop, evaluate with an outer one. Compare the outer estimate against the best inner score.

Answer. The best inner score is optimistic: it is a maximum over noisy estimates. The outer estimate is honest. The gap is exactly what naive CV reporting hides.
CoreC8 · kNN from scratch

Implement it and plot decision boundaries at $k \in \{1, 5, 35\}$.

Answer. Figure 10. Watch the boundary go from jagged to nearly linear. Then time it: prediction cost grows with the training set, because there is no model, only the data.
CoreC9 · Decision tree

Grow one greedily, choosing splits to minimise impurity. Stop at a depth limit. Plot the partition.

Answer. Figure 11. Every boundary is axis-aligned; try it on data separated by a diagonal and watch the staircase appear. That is the model's assumption becoming visible.
CoreC10 · k-means

Implement Lloyd's algorithm with random restarts. Show that the objective decreases every iteration, and that different initialisations give different answers.

Answer. Figure 12. The monotone decrease is why it terminates; the dependence on initialisation is why you restart. Run it on uniform noise and note that it still returns tidy clusters.
CoreC11 · SVM by subgradient descent

Minimise hinge loss plus $L^2$. Identify the support vectors and confirm that removing a non-support point leaves the boundary unchanged.

Answer. Support vectors are the points with margin near 1. Delete any other point, refit, and the boundary is identical: the model depends on a small subset of the data.
CoreC12 · The kernel trick

Build data that is not linearly separable. Separate it with an explicit quadratic feature map, then again using only a kernel function. Confirm identical predictions.

Answer. They match. The point is what you avoided: for a polynomial kernel of degree $d$ over $n$ features, the explicit map has $O(n^d)$ coordinates you never had to construct.
CoreC13 · PCA as an autoencoder

Fit PCA. Separately, fit a linear encoder–decoder pair by gradient descent on reconstruction error. Compare the subspaces.

Answer. The same subspace, though possibly a different basis within it. This is the bridge to autoencoders: the nonlinearity is the only thing that lets one beat PCA.
CoreC14 · Measure the curse

Sample points in $d$ dimensions and measure the ratio of farthest to nearest distance from a query, for $d$ from 1 to 500.

Answer. Figure 13, left. The ratio collapses toward 1. Once everything is equidistant, "nearest neighbour" has stopped selecting anything meaningful.
CoreC15 · Estimate intrinsic dimension

Generate data on a 2-D manifold embedded in 50 dimensions. Recover the intrinsic dimension from the PCA spectrum.

Answer. Two eigenvalues dominate if the manifold is flat. If it curves, PCA overestimates: a curved sheet needs more than two linear directions. That gap is precisely why nonlinear methods exist.
CoreC16 · Find the bug

This standardises features before cross-validation. What is wrong?

X = (X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0)
scores = cross_validate(model, X, y, k=5)
Answer. The mean and standard deviation were computed on the whole dataset, including each fold's validation portion. Information leaks from validation into training and the scores are optimistic. Standardise inside each fold, using the training portion only.
CoreC17 · No free lunch, measured

Generate random target functions over a small input space. Evaluate three learners averaged over thousands of them.

Answer. All land at chance. Over 40,000 random targets: 0.497, 0.499, 0.501. Figure 3. Then restrict to smooth targets and watch nearest neighbour pull ahead, as the assumption became true and it started paying.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · Ensembling

Train many trees on bootstrap resamples and average them. Measure how error falls with the number of trees.

Answer. That is a random forest. Error falls and plateaus. Averaging reduces variance and leaves bias alone (Part V §20), which is why ensembling helps high-variance models like deep trees and does nothing for a linear fit.
StretchS2 · Beat a network on tabular data

Take a tabular dataset. Fit gradient-boosted trees and a small neural network with equal tuning effort. Compare.

Answer. Trees frequently win, and §7 predicts it: axis-aligned splits match tabular structure, while a network's priors do not. Worth doing once, because it durably cures the assumption that deeper is always better.
StretchS3 · Distribution shift

Train on one region of input space and test on another. Compare against an i.i.d. split.

Answer. Performance collapses, sometimes to below chance. The i.i.d. assumption of §5 was doing more work than it appeared to, and no amount of validation detects a shift your validation set does not contain.
StretchS4 · A hard case for k-means

Construct data where it fails badly. Explain in terms of its assumptions.

Answer. Two concentric rings, or two elongated parallel bands. Assignment by Euclidean distance to a centre can only carve out round regions, so nested or stretched structure is hopeless. Section 7, made concrete.
StretchS5 · Double descent again

Sweep polynomial degree past the number of training points, using minimum-norm fits.

Answer. The classical U, then a peak at degree $=$ $n$, then a second descent (Part V §20). Use the true minimum-norm solution; a default rcond regularizes the peak away.
StretchS6 · The whole recipe

Pick a real dataset. Choose model family, loss and optimiser deliberately; state your assumptions in advance; then evaluate honestly using Part V's checklist.

Answer. The point is the discipline, not the score. Write down what you expect before running anything, and note where you were wrong. That habit is worth more than any individual technique in this part.
