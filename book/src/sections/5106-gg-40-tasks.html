Part DNodes, links and graphs

Three tasks, three different things that count as a training example, and three different ways to split a dataset wrongly. The evaluation problems here are more distinctive than the architectures, and they are where graph projects usually go wrong.

§25 The three tasks

What is being predicted, and what an example is

Node. A label per node: a user's category, a paper's field. Usually one large graph, partly labelled, and every node's prediction depends on its neighbours' features whether or not their labels are known.

Link. Does an edge exist between two nodes. Many graphs, or one; the examples are pairs, and the negatives have to be constructed.

Graph. A label per graph: a molecule's property. Many small graphs, and this is the case that most resembles ordinary supervised learning.

§26 Transductive and inductive

A distinction that does not arise elsewhere in this book

Transductive: the test nodes were present in the graph at training time, with their features and edges, and only their labels were hidden. Inductive: the test nodes were not there at all.

These are different problems and the transductive one is easier, because the model has already seen where the test nodes sit. A method that requires the whole graph at training time (anything using the full spectrum, for instance) is transductive by construction and simply cannot be applied to a node that arrives later.

Reporting a transductive number for a system that will be used inductively is the most common overstatement in this area, and it is Part XXII §1's estimand problem again: the quantity measured is not the quantity that will be faced.

§27 Splitting a graph

Pitfall: a random node split leaks through the edges

Hold out a random 20% of nodes and the remaining graph still contains their edges, so a test node's neighbours are training nodes whose labels the model has seen. On a homophilous graph (where neighbours share labels, which is most of them), that is close to handing over the answer.

The repairs depend on what you will face. For an inductive deployment, remove the test nodes and their edges entirely during training. For a temporal graph, split by time, which is Part XXV §36's measurement in a different shape. For link prediction, remove the test edges from the graph the model sees: an obvious point that is got wrong constantly, because the edge being predicted is right there in the adjacency.

§28 Link prediction, and the negatives

The problem is which non-edges you compare against

A graph gives you positives. The negatives are chosen, and the choice sets the difficulty: uniformly random pairs are nearly all trivially non-adjacent, so a model that has learned only "these two are in different components" scores well.

Harder and more honest negatives come from the same neighbourhood, or from pairs at distance two, the pairs a recommender would actually be deciding between. The reported metric can move by tens of points on this choice alone, so it belongs in the write-up beside the number.

And the base rate is extreme: a graph with a million nodes has 1012 possible pairs and perhaps 107 edges. Part XIV §3's threshold placement and Part XXIV §33's cost ratio are what turn a ranking into a decision here.

§29 Graph-level prediction, and the readout

Where the invariance is finally imposed

Node representations are equivariant; a graph label must be invariant; so a readout collapses the nodes: §2's sum, mean or max, or an attention-weighted sum.

The same trade returns. Sum carries size information, which is right when a bigger molecule genuinely differs and wrong when size is an artefact of how the graph was constructed. Mean discards it. Choosing here is a claim about whether the number of nodes is a fact about the object or about the dataset.

§30 Knowledge graphs

A different object with the same word attached

A knowledge graph is a set of typed triples (subject, relation, object), and the task is usually to score missing ones. The structure that matters is the relation type, which an untyped message-passing network discards entirely.

Embedding methods (scoring a triple by a function of learned vectors for the entities and the relation) remain strong baselines and are what most production systems use. Relational message passing keeps a separate transformation per relation type, which is more expressive and costs parameters linear in the number of relations.

The evaluation convention here has a well-known defect worth knowing: ranking a true triple against corrupted ones, where some corruptions are also true, penalises a model for being right. The standard correction filters known triples out of the candidate set, and results computed without it are not comparable to results computed with it.

§31 What movement D establishes

Three things

Transductive and inductive are different problems, and the easier one is what gets reported. Which you face is decided by whether new nodes arrive.

A random split leaks through the edges. The graph structure carries label information between the halves, and on a homophilous graph that is most of the task.

The negatives are a modelling choice. In link prediction they set the difficulty, and the metric moves by tens of points depending on how they were drawn.
