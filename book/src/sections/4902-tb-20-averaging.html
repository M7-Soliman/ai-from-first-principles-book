Part BAveraging

A deep tree has low bias and high variance, and variance is the failure that averaging removes. This movement measures how much it removes, establishes the identity that says what limits it, and is careful about the one part of the standard story that is a property of the dataset rather than of the method.

§8 Bagging

Bootstrap aggregating

Draw $B$ bootstrap resamples of the training set, fit a deep tree to each, and average their predictions. Part VIII §18 derived why this reduces variance and not bias; trees are the family it works best on, because they are the family with the most variance to remove.

Note the deliberate inversion. A single tree is tuned to a shallow depth because deeper overfits (Figure 1). A bagged tree is grown deep on purpose, because the averaging will handle the variance and the low bias is what you wanted.

§9 The identity that governs it

What averaging can and cannot reach

For $B$ predictors each of variance $\sigma^2$ with pairwise correlation $\rho$, the average has variance

$$\rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2$$

This follows from expanding the variance of the average directly. Writing $\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{B}\sum_i Z_i\right)$ as $\frac{1}{B^2}\sum_i \mathrm{Var}(Z_i) + \frac{1}{B^2}\sum_{i \neq j} \mathrm{Cov}(Z_i, Z_j)$ and substituting $\sigma^2$ for each variance and $\rho\sigma^2$ for each of the $B(B-1)$ covariance terms gives $\frac{1}{B}\sigma^2 + \frac{B-1}{B}\rho\sigma^2$, which regroups into exactly the two terms above.

The second term vanishes as $B$ grows. The first does not. So adding trees has diminishing returns and a floor, and the floor is set by how much the trees agree.

Two consequences worth separating: more trees never hurts accuracy, so $B$ is a compute decision rather than a tuning decision; and past a point the only way to improve is to make the trees differ.

§10 Measuring both terms

§11 Random forests

Go and read

Breiman (2001), Random forests. Where §9's variance identity comes from, stated and proved as a bound in terms of the trees' strength and their pairwise correlation. Read it for the bound itself, and notice precisely what kind of statement it is: an upper bound on the ensemble's error, not a promise that any particular way of decorrelating the trees, feature subsampling included, will lower it on your data. That is the distinction §12's illustrative figure is built around.

Decorrelation, deliberately

A random forest is bagging plus a second source of randomness: at each split, only a random subset of features is considered. Figure 2's right panel is that mechanism working: fewer features offered, less agreement between the resulting trees.

The intent is to attack $\rho$ in §9's identity. The complication is that it attacks $\sigma^2$ at the same time: a tree denied its best feature splits on a worse one, so each individual tree gets worse as the fraction falls.

§12 The trade

So measure it on yours

Sweep the feature fraction on your own data with several repetitions, and plot the ensemble error with error bars alongside the single-tree error. The common defaults ($\sqrt{d}$ for classification, $d/3$ for regression) are starting points inherited from the original papers' datasets, not results.

Report the paired difference between your best fraction and using every feature, with its standard error. If they overlap, the honest statement is that on your data the choice does not matter, and one fewer hyperparameter is worth having.

§13 Out-of-bag estimation

A validation set you did not have to set aside

A bootstrap resample omits about $1/e \approx 37\%$ of the rows. Each row can therefore be scored by the trees that did not see it, giving a held-out estimate for free and without giving up any training data.

The caveat is Part XIII §25's: out-of-bag validity assumes the rows are exchangeable. On data with a time index, or with groups, or with duplicates, the out-of-bag estimate inherits exactly the optimism a random split would; §36 measures how much that can be.

§14 Feature importance, and its two traps

Pitfall: impurity importance is biased, and permutation importance lies about correlated features

Impurity importance (total impurity reduction attributed to each feature) is biased toward features with many distinct values, because a continuous feature offers more thresholds to find a lucky split among. A random continuous column can outrank a genuinely informative binary one. This is Part XIV §20's biased maximum, per feature.

Permutation importance (the drop in accuracy when a column is shuffled) fixes that and introduces another. With two correlated features carrying the same signal, permuting either one alone costs nothing, because the other still carries it. Both look unimportant and the group is essential.

And neither is causal. Part XXII §4's warning applies in full: importance is a statement about the model's reliance, not about the world, and a feature can be important to the model precisely because it is a collider or a descendant of the target.

Named: SHAP values are the attribution most reached for ahead of either measure above (game-theoretic, and consistent under correlated features in a way permutation importance is not), but they inherit the same non-causal reading: consistency about the model's reliance is not evidence about the world.

§15 What movement B establishes

Three things

Averaging removes variance and has a floor. Figure 2 measures 2.22× from sixty trees, flattening as §9's identity says it must, with the floor set by how much the trees agree.

Decorrelating trees is a trade rather than a free improvement, because the same mechanism that makes them differ makes each of them worse, and which side wins is a property of the columns in front of you.

Importance is not evidence. Two standard measures, two different biases, and neither answers a causal question.
