Part FThe parts that are not technical

§32 Provenance, licence and consent

Three questions about every record, none of them answerable retrospectively, and all of them routinely unanswered.

The three, and why they are separate

Provenance. Where did this record come from? Not "the web": which page, retrieved when, under what terms. Without it you cannot answer any of the other questions, cannot honour a deletion request, and cannot reproduce your own corpus.

Licence. What are you permitted to do with it? Copyright attaches by default in most jurisdictions, and "publicly accessible" is not a licence. Whether training constitutes a use that requires one is genuinely contested and is being decided in courts as this is written.

Consent. Did the person whose data this is agree to this use? Distinct from licence: a photograph may be licensed by the photographer and depict someone who never agreed to be in a training set.

These come apart constantly. A record can be lawfully obtained, licensed for reuse, and used without the consent of the person it concerns.

Why provenance is the technical prerequisite for everything else

Every other question in this section is unanswerable without it, and it is the one that has to be recorded at collection time because it cannot be reconstructed later.

Concretely, provenance is what lets you: remove a source that turns out to be impermissible; honour a deletion request; audit which parts of your corpus produced a behaviour; reproduce the dataset; and answer the question a regulator or a court will ask, which is "where did this come from?".

The cost of recording a URL and a timestamp per record is negligible at collection and unbounded afterwards. This is the cheapest insurance in the part and the most commonly skipped.

Everything above treats bad data as an accident: a frame nobody chose, a filter with an opinion. Some of it is not an accident: a source can be deliberately corrupted to damage or backdoor whatever trains on it. That is a different threat model, with its own defences, and it is Part XXXII §35's subject: provenance is the first line against it, since a record you cannot trace is a record you cannot rule out.

§33 Memorisation

Go and read

Carlini et al. (2021), Extracting training data from large language models. Demonstrates extraction of verbatim training text, including personal information, from a deployed model. Then Carlini et al. (2023), Quantifying memorization, which establishes the relationship this section's figure reproduces in miniature: memorisation grows with model scale, with data duplication, and with the length of context used to prompt it.

Models memorise training data verbatim and can be induced to reproduce it. This is a data problem, not a modelling one, and §12's duplicates are the mechanism.

Why a handful of copies is the number that matters

The threshold is low, and that is the finding. A document does not need to be a significant fraction of the corpus to be memorised; it needs to appear a few times, which on a web-scale corpus is the default for anything mirrored, syndicated or quoted.

Three consequences follow directly.

Deduplication is a privacy measure. §21 gave three reasons to deduplicate; this is the fourth and it is the one with legal weight. Reducing a record to one copy moves it below the threshold.

Personal information in a corpus is extractable if it appears more than a few times, and an address or an identifier that appears once in a leaked document may appear many times across mirrors of it.

Memorisation and generalisation are not opposites. The model in the figure learned the corpus rule and memorised the canaries. Part V's bias–variance framing suggests a trade; at this scale the model does both, and a low test loss is not evidence against memorisation.

Pitfall: "it does not memorise, we checked the test loss"

Test loss measures generalisation. Memorisation is a property of specific records and is invisible in an aggregate.

Measuring it requires the method of the figure: insert canaries, random sequences you constructed, at known frequencies, train, and try to extract them. That gives you an estimate of the memorisation threshold for your model and your corpus, which no general claim can supply.

§34 What a dataset commits you to

The part closes on the thing that makes data different from every other component in this book.

Why data decisions are the hardest to reverse

Every other choice in this book is revisable. A bad architecture is retrained. A bad optimiser is swapped. A bad hyperparameter is re-tuned. The cost is compute, and compute is purchasable.

Data decisions are not like that.

A frame is fixed at collection. §2's population–frame gap cannot be closed by any later processing. If the sensors were only in one city, no amount of modelling recovers the others.

Labels encode a task definition (§6) and relabelling is the original cost again.

Provenance not recorded is lost (§32), permanently and immediately.

A model trained on impermissible data is itself a problem, not just its corpus, and removing the data does not remove what the model learned from it.

So the asymmetry named in §1 is not just an oversight in how the subject is taught. It is backwards relative to the actual cost of the decisions: the reversible choices get the scrutiny and the irreversible ones get a sentence in a footnote.

The questions to answer before collecting, not after
  1. What is the population, and what is the frame? Where do they differ? (§2)
  2. What is the label, exactly, and is it a proxy for something else? (§6)
  3. What will be different at deployment, and does the split reflect it? (§25)
  4. What provenance is being recorded, per record? (§32)
  5. What is the irreducible error, estimated from annotator agreement? (§5, §14)
  6. What would make this dataset unusable, and would you find out?

Six questions. Answering them costs a day at the start and cannot be done at all at the end.

The one thing to carry forward

Scale improves precision and does nothing for validity.

Figure 1 is the whole part: an interval of $\pm 0.003$ around an estimate that is wrong by 0.09. Every failure in Part B has this shape: the number gets tighter, more confident and more reportable while the thing it measures drifts away from the thing you wanted.

Which gives the habit worth taking into Part XIV. When a number improves, ask what it is a number about: which frame, which labels, which split, which population. The metric cannot tell you, and by the time deployment does, the decisions that determined it were made years earlier by someone who was not thinking about your problem.
