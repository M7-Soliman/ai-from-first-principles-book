Part DTwo towers

Put images and text (or audio and text) into one embedding space by pulling matched pairs together and pushing everything else apart. It is the simplest multimodal idea that works, it underlies retrieval and zero-shot classification, and two of its properties are routinely misreported.

§25 The construction

Go and read

Radford et al. (2021), Learning transferable visual models from natural language supervision. Already read at Part XV §36 for the pretraining signal it makes free: pairs harvested from the web, with no labelling step; it belongs here too, now that the two-tower construction itself, not a vision backbone, is what is being taught. The batch of 32,768 behind the next section's negatives count is theirs, and so is training the temperature itself, log-parameterised and clipped, rather than fixing it, a choice this book does not follow, so that Figure 6's sweep stays a controlled comparison rather than a moving target.

Two encoders, one loss

Encode each modality separately, normalise both to the unit sphere, and train so that the similarity of a matched pair exceeds its similarity to every other item in the batch:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \log \frac{\exp(\mathbf{a}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_i / \tau)}{\sum_j \exp(\mathbf{a}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_j / \tau)}$$

Symmetrised over both directions. This is Part XV §17's contrastive loss with the two views coming from different modalities rather than from two augmentations, and the change is more consequential than it looks: the negatives are now other examples, so the batch is the supervision.

§26 What the negatives are

Why batch size is an architectural parameter here

Every other item in the batch is a negative, so a batch of 32,768 gives each example 32,767 of them. That is why contrastive systems are trained at batch sizes no other task needs, and why a great deal of engineering exists purely to make the batch bigger: gathering embeddings across devices, or keeping a queue of recent ones.

The assumption underneath is that a random other item is a true negative. For captions it is usually right and sometimes badly wrong: two images of a dog with the caption "a dog" are pushed apart because they were not paired, which is a false negative the loss cannot distinguish from a real one.

§27 The temperature

What the temperature really is

It is usually described as a scale on the logits, which is true and unhelpful. Figure 6 says what it does: it selects how many negatives you are actually training against.

Two consequences. A low temperature is implicit hard-negative mining, with the benefits and the instability: one mislabelled pair can dominate the gradient. And below some temperature, growing the batch stops helping, because the extra negatives receive no weight; a system reporting gains from a larger batch at a very low temperature is reporting something other than more negatives.

§28 Zero-shot classification, and what it is

Retrieval wearing a different name

Write each class as a sentence, embed those sentences, and label an image by whichever is nearest. No classifier is trained, which is the appeal, and the label space becomes editable at inference time, which is the practical value.

What it is sensitive to is the wording. The same class described two ways can differ by several points, which is Part XX §22's prompt sensitivity in another modality; and it means a zero-shot number without the prompts is not reproducible.

§29 Retrieval, and the number you are reporting

Pitfall: a retrieval number is a property of the gallery

The same embedding scores 0.49 or 0.07 depending only on how many things it was searched against. So a recall figure without the gallery size is not a measurement, and two systems evaluated on differently-sized galleries cannot be compared at all.

Unlike Part XXIX §5's threshold, the ordering here was stable: the better embedding was better at every gallery size tested. So the gallery changes the number and not, in this case, the ranking, which is a weaker warning than the detection one and still enough to make an unqualified recall figure meaningless.

§30 What the two towers cannot do

The cost of encoding separately

Each tower sees only its own modality, so the interaction between them is a single dot product at the end. That is what makes retrieval fast (every item can be embedded once and indexed) and it is a hard ceiling on what can be expressed.

Anything requiring the two inputs to be compared in detail is unavailable: which object in the image the phrase refers to, whether a stated relation holds between two things in the scene, counting. These systems are famously weak at exactly those, and it is architecture rather than data.

The alternative is a cross-encoder that attends across both, which can express all of it and cannot be indexed: every query must be run against every candidate. The standard resolution is to use both: two towers to retrieve a shortlist, a cross-encoder to rerank.

§31 Audio, and the same construction

Three places it transfers, and one where it does not

Audio and text gives zero-shot sound classification and text-to-audio search, from the same loss with one tower replaced.

Speech and text gives a shared space in which spoken and written queries match, which is how spoken retrieval works without a recogniser in the path.

Audio and video can be trained with no labels at all, because the two streams arrive synchronised: the pairing is free, which is the most attractive property any of these constructions has.

Where it does not transfer: anything needing precise timing. A single embedding for a ten-second clip cannot say when the dog barked, and the loss gives no reason for it to.
