Part CWhy it fails

§11 The same matrix, over and over

Go and read

Bengio, Simard & Frasconi (1994), Learning long-term dependencies with gradient descent is difficult. The result this whole movement is about, and the title is a fair summary. Read §III for the argument of §12: that storing memories robustly requires the region where gradients vanish, so the two are one condition rather than a defect. Then Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio (2013), which adds the geometric picture of the cliffs and the norm-clipping fix, and is the more practical of the two.

Part IX §10 derived this for a different reason and the derivation is unchanged; what is new is that here it is not a corner case but the central fact about the architecture.

Strip the recurrence to its bones (no nonlinearity, no input):

$$\mathbf{h}^{(t)} = W^\top \mathbf{h}^{(t-1)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{h}^{(t)} = (W^\top)^t \mathbf{h}^{(0)}$$

For the symmetric case $W = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ (Part I §17), this simplifies cleanly:

$$\mathbf{h}^{(t)} = Q\Lambda^t Q^\top \mathbf{h}^{(0)}$$

Every eigenvalue is raised to the power $t$. Anything below one in magnitude decays to nothing; anything above one explodes; only exactly one survives. For a general (non-symmetric) $W$ the story is the same (the Jordan decomposition $W = P J P^{-1}$ gives $W^\top = P^{-\top} J^\top P^\top$, so $(W^\top)^t = (P^{-\top})(J^\top)^t P^\top$, with the Jordan blocks raised to the $t$th power) and the dominant behaviour is still set by the eigenvalue of largest magnitude, the spectral radius $\rho(W)$. If $\rho(W) < 1$ the product eventually vanishes; if $\rho(W) > 1$ it eventually explodes. Gradients travelling backward through the same path are scaled by the same product.

Pitfall: the saturating nonlinearity does not save you

A common and reasonable-sounding intuition: $\tanh$ is bounded, so nothing can explode.

What $\tanh$ bounds is the forward state. The quantity that vanishes or explodes is the product of Jacobians, and nothing bounds that: the right panel of Figure 3 shows it overflowing float32 at gain 2. A bounded function can have an unbounded derivative product, and confusing the two is the most common misreading of this whole problem.

Why this is worse than the feedforward version

Part IX §10 made the distinction and it is the reason this part exists as a separate part.

A deep feedforward network multiplies by a different matrix at each layer. The scale still drifts, but it is a random walk in the log, spreading like $\sqrt{L}$, and Part VII §11's initialisation is enough to control it in moderate depth. In very deep feedforward networks this is usually combined with residual connections (Part X §23), but the initialisation problem is far milder than the recurrent case even without them.

A recurrent network multiplies by the same matrix every step. There is no averaging, no cancellation, no random walk: one set of eigenvalues raised to the power $t$. That is exponential in $t$ rather than $\sqrt{t}$, and it is why recurrent networks needed architectural surgery when feedforward networks needed a better initialisation.

Seen another way, §11's recurrence is the power method, the algorithm for finding a matrix's dominant eigenvector. A long-running RNN converges in direction to that eigenvector and forgets everything else, and it does so because the algorithm is working correctly.

§12 The trade nobody mentions

The obvious hope, having read §11, is to keep the parameters in a region where the eigenvalues sit near one: gradients neither vanish nor explode, and the problem goes away. That hope is provably wrong, and the reason is the most important sentence in this part.

Stable memory requires vanishing gradients

To store a memory robustly, so that a small perturbation of the input does not change what is remembered, the recurrent map must be contractive in the directions carrying that memory. Contractive means eigenvalues below one. Eigenvalues below one means gradients that vanish.

So it is not that vanishing gradients are an unlucky property of a badly chosen $W$. Whenever the network is capable of representing long-term dependencies, the gradient of a long-term interaction is exponentially smaller than that of a short-term one. The two are the same condition.

This does not make learning impossible. It makes the signal about long-range structure exponentially quieter than the signal about local structure, so the local structure is learned first and the long-range structure is buried under the fluctuations of learning it. That is a statement about how long training takes, not about what is representable, and it is why §4's Turing-completeness result is no comfort.

§13 Cliffs

Part IX §9 met cliffs in a network of six multiplied factors and measured a single step raising the loss three million fold. A recurrent network multiplies one factor per time step, so the same structure appears with the sequence length as its exponent.

That last clause is what makes this different from Part IX's version. There the cliff was a property of a deep model, fixed at design time. Here it is a property of the data: the same trained network, given longer sequences, sits on a steeper surface. A learning rate tuned on short examples is not safe on long ones.

Clipping, and the two forms of it

Part IX §9's argument applies unchanged: a gradient specifies a direction and says nothing about how far that direction remains good. Clipping bounds the distance and leaves the direction alone.

By norm. If $\|\mathbf{g}\| > c$, rescale to $c\mathbf{g}/\|\mathbf{g}\|$. Preserves the direction exactly. This is the standard choice and it is why.

Element-wise. Clamp each coordinate independently. Cheaper, and it rotates the update: you step somewhere the gradient never pointed. Used, and worse.

Clipping addresses explosion only. It does nothing whatever for vanishing, which is §12's problem and the harder one.

A separate approach tries to encourage gradient flow directly, not by constraining the step size but by penalising configurations where backpropagated gradients shrink. The idea, due to Pascanu et al. (2013), is to add a regulariser that pulls the norm of the backpropagated signal at each step toward the norm of the gradient at the same step:

$$\Omega = \sum_t \left(\frac{\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}} L\cdot \frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(t)}}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(t-1)}}\right\|} {\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{h}^{(t)}} L\right\|} - 1\right)^2$$

When this ratio is one the gradient vector maintains its magnitude through the Jacobian: the condition §12 established is impossible to hold everywhere for long sequences, so the regulariser does not remove the vanishing but applies a persistent pull against it. Combined with norm clipping, it measurably extends the span of dependencies an RNN can learn. It did not displace gating, which is more effective and requires no tuning of a regularisation coefficient, but it isolates the mechanism, and knowing that a penalty term can substitute partially for a structural solution tells you something about which solution is addressing which problem.

§14 The failure, measured, and what it is actually a failure of

The standard claim is that a vanilla RNN cannot learn dependencies beyond about ten or twenty steps: the probability of a successful run falls to nearly zero. It is a real result and it is worth reproducing rather than repeating, because reproducing it shows that the claim is about something other than the architecture.

Why the textbook comparison does not reproduce, and what that means

Two things are going on, and both are more useful than the claim they complicate.

The 1994 result is partly a statement about the optimiser of 1994. Plain SGD with no clipping is where the failure lives. Adam adapts a per-parameter step size (Part III §16), which is exactly the right medicine for gradients whose scale varies by orders of magnitude across parameters; clipping handles the explosion. Add both and a vanilla RNN handles this task at $T=80$. That does not make §12 false (the vanishing is still there); it means the optimiser can work with a much quieter signal than was once possible.

The gated architectures are being handicapped by an initialisation. §20 is about this, and it is the more surprising half.

The honest summary: the architecture, the optimiser and the initialisation are three variables, and the famous comparison varies all three. That is Part X §26's complaint about ConvNeXt, arriving in a completely different literature, and it is the reason Part IX §29's ladder insists on changing one thing at a time.

Pitfall: what this figure does not show

This is one task, at a small scale, with a signal that must merely survive rather than be manipulated. It is not evidence that vanilla RNNs are fine.

On tasks requiring the state to be selectively updated (remember this, forget that, now overwrite), the gated architectures win decisively, and at the scale of real language modelling the gap was large enough that nobody used vanilla RNNs for a decade. What the figure establishes is narrower and still worth having: the standard demonstration does not isolate the variable it claims to isolate.

§15 Why better optimisers were not the answer

Given §§11–14, an obvious research programme is to fix the optimiser. It was attempted seriously and it is instructive that it lost.

The idea was principled: if first derivatives vanish, second derivatives may vanish at a similar rate, so their ratio, which is what a second-order method uses, could stay well-scaled. Hessian-free optimisation was built on exactly this and produced genuinely promising results.

Then simpler methods with careful initialisation matched it. Then both were replaced by plain SGD applied to LSTMs.

Why this keeps happening

Part IX §27 stated the principle and this is its clearest instance: it is usually easier to design a model that is easy to optimise than to design a more powerful optimiser.

Three separate research efforts (second-order methods, better initialisation, careful momentum) were all made unnecessary by changing the architecture so that the gradient has a path it can travel along. That is the subject of Part D, and the same pattern produced ReLU over sigmoid, batch normalisation over higher-order optimisers, and residual connections over everything that came before them.

It is worth carrying as a prior. When something will not train, the ladder in Part IX §29 puts "a different optimiser" last, and this is the historical evidence for that ordering.
