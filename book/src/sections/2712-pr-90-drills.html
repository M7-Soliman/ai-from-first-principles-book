PracticeDrills

Thirty-two problems. This part is a set of instruments, so most of these are about building one and then establishing what it can and cannot see.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · Is that difference real

Model A scores 71.2% and model B 72.0%, each on the same 2,000 test examples. Before doing any statistics, write down whether you believe the difference.

Answer. You should not. The half-width on a difference at that size is about ±3 percentage points unpaired; the gap is 0.8. Pairing on the same examples helps and does not rescue it. The reflex to build: compute the interval before you form an opinion, not after. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

WarmW1 · The four steps

Name them, and say which one is usually skipped.

Answer. Metric and target; end-to-end baseline; instrument; change one thing. The third is skipped, and skipping it turns a diagnosis into a sequence of guesses.
WarmW2 · Why not accuracy

A condition affects one in a million. What accuracy does a constant "no" achieve, and what do you report instead?

Answer. 99.9999%. Report precision and recall, or the PR curve. And note the threshold is a separate decision from the model: you can move it after training.
WarmW3 · Coverage

What does coverage buy, and what does it require?

Answer. It turns an impossible accuracy target into an achievable optimisation: hold accuracy fixed by refusing when unsure, and maximise the fraction answered. It requires calibrated confidence and a human able to take the remainder.
WarmW4 · What temperature scaling changes

And what it cannot change.

Answer. It changes calibration: expected calibration error 0.320 to 0.058 in Figure 2, one training run's digits and expect yours to differ slightly. It cannot change accuracy, on any run, because dividing every logit by the same positive number leaves the ordering identical.
WarmW5 · Ten examples

What does it mean if a model cannot fit ten examples, and what must you turn off first?

Answer. It means a bug, not underfitting: the correct behaviour is known in advance, which is what makes the test unambiguous. Turn off every regulariser first, or you will chase a failure that means nothing.
WarmW6 · The U-curve

Why does a central difference get worse as $\epsilon$ shrinks past $10^{-5}$?

Answer. Cancellation. The two evaluations agree in more leading digits, so their difference keeps fewer significant ones, and then you divide by a tiny number. Part III §3, in the tool everyone uses to check gradients.
WarmW7 · Why the complex step has no U

State the mechanism in one sentence.

Answer. It never subtracts. The derivative is read off the imaginary part, which is separate storage, so $\epsilon = 10^{-150}$ is fine. Requires $f$ analytic: no abs, no max, no ReLU.
WarmW8 · Train high, test low

What does this pattern almost always mean?

Answer. A measurement error: leakage, an easier test set, or heavy training-time regularization such as dropout, which raises training error by construction.
WarmW9 · The one-percent rule

What ratio, and why is it a better instrument than the learning rate?

Answer. Update magnitude over parameter magnitude, around 1% per step. Better because it is scale-free: the right learning rate depends on parameterisation and layer; the right update ratio does not, so every group is comparable on one axis.
WarmW10 · Best of a hundred

A hundred configurations, all truly identical at 0.80. What does the best one report?

Answer. About 0.85 (Figure 7). The entire 0.05 is selection on validation noise, and it is larger than most claimed improvements.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Metrics against the threshold

On an imbalanced problem, plot accuracy, precision, recall and $F_1$ against the decision threshold. Find where each is maximised.

Answer. They peak at different thresholds. In Figure 1 the accuracy optimum has recall 0.117 and the $F_1$ optimum has 0.377, the same model, and the metric choosing the operating point.
CoreC2 · A reliability diagram

Bin predictions by confidence and plot bin accuracy against bin confidence. Compute the expected calibration error.

Answer. Expect points below the diagonal: overconfidence. Use enough bins to see structure and drop bins with too few points, or you are plotting noise.
CoreC3 · Fit the temperature

Fit a single $T$ by minimising cross-entropy on validation data. Report calibration error and accuracy before and after.

Answer. Calibration improves substantially; accuracy is bit-identical. Assert that in code: if accuracy moved, you fitted on the wrong split or changed something else.
CoreC4 · The coverage curve

Sweep a confidence threshold and plot accuracy against coverage. Then repeat with calibrated confidences.

Answer. The curve improves after calibration even though accuracy at full coverage is unchanged, because the ranking by confidence is what the refusal mechanism uses, and calibration makes the threshold interpretable.
CoreC5 · Break something, then find it

Deliberately break a training loop (detach a gradient, misalign labels by one, freeze a parameter group) and see which instrument catches it first.

Answer. The ten-example test catches most of them in seconds; the loss curve catches almost none. Keep a record of which bug each instrument caught, because that is the ladder of §34 derived from your own experience.
CoreC6 · The epsilon sweep

Plot the relative error of forward, central and complex-step derivatives against $\epsilon$ over fifteen decades.

Answer. Forward and central are U-shaped with a minimum near $10^{-5}$; the complex step falls monotonically and flattens at machine precision. Below about $10^{-20}$ the differences return relative error 1.0: the subtraction has underflowed to zero.
CoreC7 · One difference, whole Jacobian

Check a vector-valued function's Jacobian with a single scalar finite difference using random projections.

Answer. $h(t) = \mathbf{u}^\top g(\mathbf{x} + t\mathbf{v})$, agreeing to about $10^{-10}$. Repeat with several random $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}$: one projection can be blind to an error orthogonal to it.
CoreC8 · Sorted mistakes

Rank training examples by the probability assigned to the correct answer and inspect the worst fifty.

Answer. Confident errors are usually a data or pipeline defect; random errors are usually hard examples. Do this on the training set: a systematic problem shows there most clearly.
CoreC9 · The learning curve

Measure test error against training-set size on a log scale, several seeds per point. Fit a power law to the first half and check it against the second.

Answer. The fit extrapolates usefully for an order of magnitude and then drifts: pessimistic by 1.23× in Figure 4, one training run's factor; expect a similar-sized miss on yours, not that digit. Good enough to distinguish "more data will do nothing" from "ten times would halve it"; not good enough to promise a number.
CoreC10 · Grid against random

Build an objective where one hyperparameter matters and several do not. Compare grid and random at equal budget as the dimension grows.

Answer. Random wins by a growing margin: 81× at eight dimensions. Then count distinct values tested along the influential axis: that is the whole explanation.
CoreC11 · Log-uniform sampling

Sample learning rates uniformly in $[10^{-5}, 10^{-1}]$ and log-uniformly over the same range. Compare the distributions per decade.

Answer. Uniform puts 90% of its samples in the top decade: you have searched one order of magnitude and sampled the rest three times. Log-uniform spreads them evenly, which is what "search over scales" means.
CoreC12 · Successive halving

Implement it and compare against random search at equal total budget, measuring regret.

Answer. Identical at small budgets (halving needs room for at least one elimination round), then a growing win. Report both ends; showing only the right end overstates it.
CoreC13 · Best-of-k inflation

Simulate $k$ configurations with identical true quality and noisy validation scores. Plot the reported best against $k$.

Answer. Rises steadily; a fresh estimate of the winner stays flat at the truth. The inflation grows with the size of the search, so more effort produces a more optimistic number from the same model.
CoreC14 · Seed variance

Train one configuration with ten seeds. Report the spread, then ask what size of improvement a single run could establish.

Answer. Roughly: nothing smaller than the spread. Compare it against the improvements reported in a paper you respect, often the same order, which is the finding.
CoreC15 · Both ablations

On a system with three components, run leave-one-out and add-one-in. Compare.

Answer. They disagree when components interact. A pair that is individually useless and jointly essential looks worthless under add-one-in and essential under leave-one-out, and reporting one of the two is how effects get attributed wrongly.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · The ladder, timed

Take a project someone else broke for you and work the ladder of §34 in order, recording how long each rung took and what it eliminated.

Answer. Rungs one and two should take ten minutes and eliminate the two most common causes. If you find yourself at rung eight, check that you actually did rung one: it is the one people skip because it feels too simple.
StretchS2 · An ablation that changes two things

Remove a regulariser without re-tuning the learning rate, then with. Compare the two attributions.

Answer. The un-tuned version overstates the component's importance, because part of the drop is a learning rate that was chosen for its presence. State which one you did.
StretchS3 · Validate a judge

Use a model to grade outputs. Then measure its agreement with human labels on a sample, and test it for position and length bias.

Answer. Present pairs in both orders: the disagreement rate is the position bias. Correlate score with length. Report agreement against inter-human agreement, which bounds what any judged comparison can establish.
StretchS4 · Slice it

Choose slices in advance from Part XIII §3's checklist and report per-slice performance alongside the aggregate.

Answer. The worst slice is often far below the mean and does not move the mean, because it is small. Report the worst slice as a headline number: it is what a user in it experiences.
StretchS5 · Close a feedback loop

Simulate a system whose decisions determine its future training data. Run it for many rounds with and without a randomised holdout.

Answer. Without randomisation the model becomes confident about a region it selected and blind outside it, and no statistic computed from the observed data detects this. With a random slice the counterfactual is measurable. The cost is permanent and small; the alternative is unfalsifiable.
StretchS6 · Monitor without labels

Build the monitors of §27 and then inject three faults: a shifted input distribution, a changed upstream encoding, and genuine concept drift. See which monitor catches which, and how fast.

Answer. Input monitoring catches the encoding change almost immediately and it is the most common real incident. Concept drift is slowest and only a labelled stream detects it reliably, which is the argument for paying for one.
