BuildThe project

Twelve to sixteen hours. You build one search framework, run five different algorithms inside it, and then discover experimentally how good a verifier has to be before searching against it is worth doing at all.

P.1 What you are building

frontier: one search interface, five algorithms behind it, three domains in front of it, and a measured answer to the question that decides whether test-time compute helps.

The rule: every algorithm reports nodes expanded and solution cost, and no algorithm is compared on time until it is compared on nodes. Wall-clock hides both bugs and constant factors; node counts are the property of the algorithm.

P.2 Stage 1: the interface

  1. Define a problem as five things: initial state, actions, transition, goal test, step cost.
  2. Implement BFS, uniform-cost, iterative deepening and A* behind one signature.
  3. Implement three domains: a grid with obstacles, the 8-puzzle, and one of your choosing.
  4. Assert that all four algorithms return the same cost on unit-cost problems.

Deliverable: a table of nodes and cost, four algorithms by three domains. Step four is the test that matters: if uniform-cost and A* disagree by one, your heuristic is inadmissible or your stale-entry check is wrong, and you want to find that out now rather than in Stage 4.

P.3 Stage 2: heuristics

  1. For the 8-puzzle: misplaced tiles, Manhattan, and a pattern database over one tile subset.
  2. Verify dominance holds pointwise, then confirm the expansion counts are ordered the same way.
  3. Build $h=\alpha h^*$ on the grid and reproduce Figure 1's shape.
  4. Add tie-breaking toward larger $g$ and re-measure.

Deliverable: expansions against heuristic strength, with and without tie-breaking. Step four is the point of the stage: report how the one-line tie-break compares against everything you gained from a better heuristic.

P.4 Stage 3: giving up optimality

  1. Weighted A* at five weights; record expansions, cost ratio, and the bound.
  2. Build an admissible-but-inconsistent heuristic and count re-expansions.
  3. Run the same heuristic with a hard closed list and find an instance where the answer is wrong.
  4. Implement anytime weighted A* and plot cost and bound against time.

Deliverable: the ratio-against-bound plot, and one concrete instance where closing states under an inconsistent heuristic returns a suboptimal path. Step three is worth the effort: it turns a caveat you have read into a failure you have seen.

P.5 Stage 4: big spaces

  1. Beam search over a scoring model, verified against exact dynamic programming at large widths.
  2. Introduce a gap between the model's score and the quality you want; sweep the beam width.
  3. Count the instances where doubling the width made the answer worse.
  4. Branch and bound on knapsack with two bounds, reporting censored runs as censored.

Deliverable: the quality-against-width curve with the exact optimum marked, plus the per-instance harm rate. If your curve is monotone, your model and your metric agree and you have not yet built the interesting case.

P.6 Stage 5: planning in continuous space

  1. A 2D configuration space with obstacles and a collision checker; count checks, not seconds.
  2. PRM with a learning phase and a query phase; RRT and bidirectional RRT.
  3. Shortcut smoothing, reporting path length before and after.
  4. Shrink a corridor until the planner fails, then add obstacle-biased sampling and repeat.

Deliverable: success rate against corridor width for two sampling strategies. This is the number that decides whether a sampling-based planner works on a real problem, and it never appears in an average-case result.

P.7 Stage 6: adversaries

  1. Minimax and alpha–beta on a small game; assert identical values on a thousand random trees.
  2. Count leaves under random, best-first and worst-first ordering across depths.
  3. Check the best-first count against $b^{\lceil d/2\rceil}+b^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}-1$.
  4. Replace perfect ordering with the cheap approximation (best move from a one-ply-shallower search) and measure how much of the benefit survives.

Deliverable: leaves against depth for four orderings. Step four is the one that transfers: perfect ordering is unavailable and most of its value is not.

P.8 Stage 7: the verifier

  1. Simulate a candidate population with a base solve rate and an attractive-error mode.
  2. Sweep the attractive share; measure verifier accuracy empirically rather than setting it.
  3. Find the break-even accuracy at $k=2,4,8,16,32$.
  4. Compare argmax selection against majority voting among accepted candidates.
  5. Now replace the simulation with something real: a generator you have (any model), a task with a checkable answer, and a deliberately imperfect verifier. Measure the same curve.

Deliverable: break-even accuracy against $k$, simulated and real, on the same axes. Step five is the whole project: the simulation tells you what shape to expect and only the real measurement tells you where your system sits on it.

P.9 Stretch

  1. Learn a heuristic, calibrate it to be probabilistically admissible, and price the calibration.
  2. Implement RRT* and show the cost curve decaying where RRT's is flat.
  3. UCT with a learned prior over actions; measure how much prior strength substitutes for budget.
  4. Split a fixed compute budget between generator quality and $k$, and find the optimum at two verifier accuracies.

P.10 Done when

You can answer all of these from your own numbers rather than from this part:

  • State what admissibility buys and prove it in three lines.
  • Say how much work a perfect heuristic saves, and why it is less than expected.
  • Explain why half a perfect heuristic saves almost nothing.
  • Give the ratio between a weighted A* bound and its behaviour, from your own runs.
  • Show an instance where closing states under an inconsistent heuristic is wrong.
  • Explain when consistency is worth more than tightness, and when it is not.
  • Show a beam width where widening made the answer worse, and say why.
  • Distinguish a valid bound from a tight one, with node counts.
  • Say what censoring is and mark it on a plot.
  • Give the Knuth–Moore count and check it against your own measurement.
  • Explain why move ordering is worth a doubling of depth.
  • State the break-even verifier accuracy at $k=32$ and why it rises with $k$.
  • Name the four questions to ask before adding search to a system.
What this part is for, if you are building learned systems

None of the algorithms here will appear verbatim in a modern stack. What appears is their vocabulary, and it is the vocabulary that is missing from most discussions of test-time compute.

When someone proposes sampling more candidates, the questions are: what is the branching factor, what is the selection rule, is it admissible in any sense, what is the ceiling, and how good is the scorer. Four of those five have measurable answers and one of them (the ceiling) costs a single run. A team that asks them is doing search engineering. A team that does not is turning $k$ up until the metric stops improving, which is the same thing done blind, at greater cost, and with no way to tell a search problem from a model problem.

The framework, and the thing outside it

Dataset, model family, loss, optimiser. Every part of this book until now has chosen among those and then applied the result in one pass. This part is about the computation that happens after all four choices are made: at the moment the system is used, on the particular question it was asked.

That computation obeys its own laws, and they are older than the framework. Optimism buys optimality; a bound describes the worst case rather than the behaviour; the order in which you try things is worth more than the rule you use to prune them; and a selector is only worth using if it is right more often than the thing it is selecting from is. Training decides what your system knows; search decides how much of it you can spend on a hard question, and the second is the half that scales with the problem rather than with the budget you had months ago.
