Part FClosing

The last five sections of this part. What remains genuinely unknown, how to hold that honestly, what to carry forward from everything before this point, and where this part's own thread leads next.

§41 What is genuinely unknown

It is worth separating three kinds of not-knowing, because they are routinely conflated and they call for different responses.

Three kinds, and what each one licenses

Not yet measured. Somebody could run the experiment tomorrow. Most of what this book presents as open is of this kind, and the correct response is to run it: several of this part's drills are exactly that.

Not currently measurable. The experiment is not available: the quantity has no agreed operationalisation (Part XX §43), or measuring it requires capability we do not have, or the failure is too rare to observe (§27). The correct response is to say so, and to resist the substitution of a measurable proxy for the thing you cannot measure, which is §18's whole subject.

Not settled by evidence at all. How much weight to put on an irreversible harm; whose interests count; what the acceptable failure rate is. These are not empirical, and dressing them as technical questions is a way of removing them from the people entitled to decide them.

The specific list, for this part's subject

Whether interpretability scales: every method in movements A and B was demonstrated on systems small enough to have a ground truth, which is exactly the regime where you do not need them. Whether the features a dictionary finds are the model's features or the dictionary's. Whether oversight can be made to work when the overseer is weaker than the system. What fraction of a model's behaviour any current method actually accounts for: the honest answer is a small one, and it is rarely stated.

None of these is rhetorical. Each is a research programme with people working on it, and each could look different in five years.

§42 How to reason under this uncertainty

Four habits, all of them used throughout this book

Separate the measurement from the interpretation. Figure 4 measures that heavy-tailed proxy error destroys the objective under selection. That mode collapse happens is a measurement; that the loss function causes it is an interpretation, and Part XVI found it to be the wrong one. Keep the two in different sentences and you will be wrong less often, and recoverably.

Say what would change your mind, in advance. Before running the experiment, write down which outcome supports which conclusion. This is the only reliable defence against the after-the-fact story, and the failure to do it is why so much of the literature this book cites did not replicate.

Prefer the intervention to the correlation. Figure 2 is the whole argument: a probe finds what is present, an ablation finds what is used, and only the second is a claim about the mechanism. This generalises far beyond interpretability.

State the uncertainty at the size it is. Not larger, which is a way of avoiding commitment, and not smaller, which is the more common failure. §27's arithmetic is the model: a clean run of a thousand evaluations is real evidence, it is just much weaker evidence than it feels like.

§43 What to carry forward

Twenty-one parts and hundreds of pages stand behind this point, and the great majority of it is detail that you will look up rather than remember. What should survive is smaller.

The five that were load-bearing

Stated at the front of the book, and every part since has been an application of them. The chain rule (Part 0 §17): backpropagation is not analogous to it, it is it. A matrix mixes its columns (Part I §4). Eigenvectors are the natural axes (Part I §17). Logs turn products into sums (Part 0 §11). Training minimises a divergence (Part II §26). Nothing in the twenty parts between that opening and this one required a sixth.

The five the practice taught

The bottleneck is rarely where the theory says. Generation is limited by memory bandwidth, not arithmetic (XIX). Training is limited by data, not parameters (XVII §12). Projects fail on data, not on architecture (XIII).

A phenomenon being real does not make its standard explanation right. Mode collapse is real and is not caused by the loss form (XVI). Emergence is real in the metric and not in the model (XVII). Probes read real information the network does not use (this part, §7). This happened often enough across the book to be a rule rather than an anecdote.

Measure the thing, not the proxy for it. Time, not FLOPs (XIX §1). Held-out performance, not training loss (VI). True objective, not proxy reward (§18). The substitution is always locally reasonable and is where most wrong conclusions enter.

The winner of a search is biased upward. Model selection (XIV §20), benchmark leaders (XX §41), best-of-$n$ against a learned reward (§18). One piece of arithmetic, three places it costs you.

What you cannot measure still exists. Rare failures (§27), unanticipated failure modes (§35), the fraction of behaviour no interpretability method reaches (§41). Absence of a measurement is not a measurement of absence, and systems get deployed on the confusion between them.

§44 Where to go next

Inside the book, the most direct continuation is immediate. Movement B built activation patching and ablation as interventions without ever formalising what an intervention licenses you to conclude about a cause; Part XXII supplies exactly that foundation, and opens by saying so. Outside the book, three honest paths are open, and none of them requires waiting for the next part.

Three honest paths, and what each requires

Build. The projects in this book are deliberately small. Take one to a scale where it breaks (a real dataset, real users, a real latency budget) and the parts of Part XIII and Part XIX that read as caution will convert into experience. This is the path where the book's value compounds fastest.

Read the frontier. The reading list places each paper where its idea is developed, and Part XIV's methodology is what lets you read a new one critically instead of credulously. Start from the papers this part cites: they are the ones whose conclusions are least settled, which makes them the ones where a careful reader can still contribute.

Go deeper on the mathematics. This book took the shortest honest path from arithmetic to the frontier, which meant leaving measure-theoretic probability, convex analysis, and information geometry at the level each result needed. If the derivations were the parts you enjoyed, that is the direction, and Part 0's reading list is the entry.

None of these is the serious one and the others less so. They suit different people, and the book was written to leave all three open.

§45 What is left standing

The book began with arithmetic: with why $\log$ turns products into sums, and what a derivative is. This part ends with a field that cannot yet say what its own systems are computing. That distance is not a gap in the exposition. It is the actual state of the subject, and the honest thing a textbook can do is walk it without smoothing it over.

What has been argued throughout is that the two ends are continuous. There is no point where the mathematics stops and something else begins. A transformer is matrix multiplication and the chain rule; scaling laws are curve fits with confidence intervals; alignment is optimisation against a specification that is wrong in ways you can partly measure; interpretability is linear algebra applied to activations, with all of linear algebra's limits intact. The field's genuine difficulty is not that it left the mathematics behind. It is that we can build systems whose behaviour we can measure far better than we can explain, and every part of this book has tried to be precise about which of those two it was doing at the time.

Sixty seconds

Measure what you claim. Say how you measured it. State the uncertainty at its real size. When the measurement contradicts the explanation you liked, keep the measurement. That discipline is the whole method of this book, it is what produced every result in it that was worth having, and it is the only part that will still be correct when the specific architectures here are of historical interest.
