Part EFrequency in modern architectures

Positional encodings are a frequency basis, the standard trick for extending a model's context is an edit to those frequencies, and the standard fix for spectral bias is to hand the network the basis rather than make it find one. Three constructions in current use that are this part's subject wearing different names.

§33 Positional encoding is a frequency basis

What Part XII §9 wrote down

The sinusoidal encoding gives position $p$ the vector with entries $\sin(p/10000^{2i/d})$ and $\cos(p/10000^{2i/d})$ for $i = 0 \dots d/2$. That is $p$ written in a bank of frequencies spaced geometrically from 1 down to $10^{-4}$, the first terms of a Fourier basis, evaluated at $p$.

The property that made it work is §8's: a shift becomes a phase change, and a phase change is a rotation of each sine–cosine pair. So the encoding of $p + k$ is a fixed linear function of the encoding of $p$, with a matrix depending only on $k$. Relative position is linearly available, which is the whole design goal.

Why geometric spacing, and what the base sets

Geometric spacing covers many scales with few dimensions: the fast components resolve adjacent positions, the slow ones distinguish the start of the sequence from the end. It is a wavelet-like allocation rather than a uniform one, and it suits the data for the reason §27 gives: natural sequences, like natural images, have most of their structure at low frequency.

The base (10,000 in the original) sets the slowest frequency, and therefore the longest distance the encoding can distinguish before it wraps. §35 is what happens when that choice becomes the binding constraint.

§34 Rotary embeddings

Go and read

Su et al. (2021), RoFormer: enhanced transformer with rotary position embedding. Read §3.4 for the derivation that the dot product depends only on relative position, which is the property established below. Then read a context-extension paper and watch it edit the frequency bank.

The same idea moved inside the dot product

Rotary position embedding treats each pair of query and key dimensions as a point in the plane and rotates it by an angle proportional to position: pair $i$ at position $p$ is rotated by $p\theta_i$, with $\theta_i$ geometrically spaced as before.

Because rotations compose, the dot product between a query at $m$ and a key at $n$ depends on $m - n$ alone. Relative position is exact rather than approximately available, and nothing is added to the representation, which is why it survives depth where an additive encoding fades.

§35 Extending context by changing frequencies

Three edits, and what each assumes

A model trained at 4,096 positions has seen its slowest rotary component through part of one cycle. Ask for 32,768 and that component is extrapolating, which is the failure mode.

Position interpolation. Divide every position by the extension factor, so the new range maps onto the trained one. Nothing extrapolates. The cost is that adjacent positions are now closer in angle than any the model was trained on, so fine positional discrimination degrades.

Base scaling. Increase the base, lowering every frequency. Same trade, applied uniformly.

Frequency-dependent scaling. Interpolate the low frequencies, which are the ones that wrap, and leave the high ones alone, which carry local order and were fully trained. This works better than either uniform option and the reason is §33's: the two ends of the bank were doing different jobs.

All three are edits to a frequency bank. Recognising that is the difference between choosing among them and copying a configuration.

§36 Fourier features

Go and read

Tancik et al. (2020), Fourier features let networks learn high frequency functions in low dimensional domains. The remedy for §29's bias, with the bandwidth made an explicit hyperparameter. Unusually candid about the trade: it shows the failure at both ends of the range, which most method papers do not.

The remedy for §29

Rather than feeding a coordinate $x$ to a network, feed $[\sin(2\pi B x), \cos(2\pi B x)]$ for a bank of frequencies $B$. The network now has high-frequency functions available as linear combinations of its inputs rather than something it must synthesise, and Figure 7's problem largely disappears.

This is what makes coordinate networks work for images, signed distance functions and neural radiance fields, all of which need fine spatial detail from a two- or three-number input. It is also, read the other way, a statement about what §29 was measuring: the difficulty was never capacity, it was the basis.

Pitfall: the bandwidth is now a hyperparameter

The bank's highest frequency sets the finest detail representable. Too low and the fit stays blurry for the reason it was blurry before. Too high and the network fits noise immediately, because you have removed exactly the bias that was protecting it.

Which is the honest description of the trick: it does not remove spectral bias, it moves the cutoff into your control. Part XXIII §21's pattern again: the power is bought by giving up a constraint, and the constraint was doing something.

§37 What the first layer learns

A prediction this part can make about Part X

Part X §31 reports that the first layer of a trained vision network fills with oriented, band-limited edge detectors resembling Gabor filters, and treats this as an empirical regularity. §19 names them: band-pass filters at various orientations and scales.

Given §27's measurement, that is close to what an efficient linear code for natural images should look like: a set of filters tiling frequency and orientation, each responding to a narrow band. The network is not discovering something strange. It is finding the basis its input statistics call for, which is also the basis the mammalian visual system uses, and both facts have the same one-line explanation.

§38 Where the frequency view misleads

Four honest limits

Nonlinearity breaks it. The convolution theorem is a statement about linear time-invariant systems. A ReLU is neither, and the neat per-frequency story survives only within a layer.

Attention is not shift-invariant, so its natural basis is not frequency. Positional encodings are frequency-based; the mechanism they feed is not.

Stationarity is assumed and often false. A single spectrum describes a signal whose statistics do not change. Speech, video and text all change constantly, which is why §23's short-time analysis exists and why a global spectrum of a long recording says little.

A spectrum discards phase when you summarise it, and by §18 that is where the content is. Two images with the same power spectrum can look like anything.

§39 What movement E establishes

Three things

Positional encodings are a frequency bank, geometrically spaced, chosen so that a shift acts linearly. Rotary embeddings move the same construction inside the dot product and make relative position exact.

Context extension is an edit to that bank, and the reason frequency-dependent scaling beats uniform scaling is that the fast and slow components were doing different jobs.

Fourier features do not defeat spectral bias, they relocate it. The cutoff becomes a hyperparameter you set instead of a bias the optimiser imposes, and the bias was protecting you from something.
