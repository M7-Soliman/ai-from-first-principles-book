Part ALooking inside

A trained network is a few hundred billion numbers that produce good outputs for reasons nobody wrote down. This movement asks what it would even mean to understand them, finds that the obvious unit of analysis is the wrong one, and measures both the technique that addresses this and the amount it leaves undone.

§1 What a question about internals means

Every part before this one treated the network as a function to be trained, measured and deployed. This part opens it. The first difficulty is that "understand the model" is not yet a question, and different people asking it want incompatible things.

Four questions that get called the same thing

What is this component computing? A claim about a part in isolation: this head attends to the previous occurrence of the current token, this direction encodes sentiment. Verifiable, and the subject of movements A and B.

Why did the model produce this output? A claim about one forward pass. Attribution methods answer it, badly, for reasons §14 gives.

What will the model do on inputs I have not tried? The question everyone actually wants answered, and the one furthest from current reach. §41 is honest about the distance.

Is the model doing what I intended? Not an interpretability question at all: it presupposes a specification, which is movement C's subject and is usually the missing piece.

Why this is not idle

Three concrete reasons, none of them philosophical. Auditing: a behaviour you cannot find by testing might be findable by looking, and §27 shows testing alone is weak for anything rare. Debugging: a model that fails for a known internal reason can be fixed; one that fails opaquely can only be retrained and hoped over. Trust that generalises: a measurement of behaviour is a claim about the distribution you measured on, whereas a measurement of mechanism is, in principle, a claim that transfers.

The word in principle is doing real work in that last sentence, and this movement is about how much.

§2 Neurons are not features

The natural first move is to treat one unit as one concept: find the inputs that make neuron 1,483 fire hardest and name it. This was tried extensively, it produced some famous successes, and it fails as a general method for a reason that turns out to be structural rather than accidental.

Polysemanticity

A polysemantic neuron responds strongly to several unrelated inputs: a unit that fires for legal language, for images of hands, and for the token the in French. Not a rare curiosity: in a large network it is the common case.

The tempting reading is that the network is disorganised. The better reading is that the network has more things to represent than it has neurons, and polysemanticity is what that shortage looks like from the outside.

Pitfall: the highest-activating inputs are a biased sample

The standard way to name a neuron is to collect the inputs that activate it most. That procedure is Part XIV §20's biased maximum, wearing a different hat: you are reporting the extreme of a search, so a coherent-looking story will appear even when the underlying selectivity is weak.

The correction is the one Part XIV gives: check the full distribution of activations, not its top tail, and check whether inputs that should activate the neuron under your proposed interpretation actually do.

§3 Superposition, recalled

Part XV §33 established the phenomenon and measured it, and this part depends on that result rather than repeating it. The short form, because everything in this movement rests on it:

Superposition

A network is in superposition when it represents more features than it has dimensions, by assigning them non-orthogonal directions and tolerating the interference that causes.

Part XV's measurement: twenty features of decaying importance and five dimensions to hold them. With dense inputs exactly five are stored, orthogonally, and the rest are discarded. At 99% sparsity sixteen are stored (more than three times the available dimensions) at a mean interference of 0.345.

Why sparsity is what makes it work

Twenty directions cannot be orthogonal in five dimensions, so storing all twenty guarantees interference: reading feature 3 picks up some of features 7 and 12.

The saving grace is that if features are rarely active, they are rarely active together. Interference costs you only when two overlapping features fire at once, and at 1% density that is rare enough to be worth the extra capacity. The model is making a trade, and the sparsity of the data sets the exchange rate.

This is why the phenomenon is universal in practice. Natural data is sparse in its features: of everything a sentence could be about, a given sentence is about very few.

§4 What superposition costs the analyst

Part XV presented superposition as a fact about representations. Here it is an obstacle, and naming the three specific ways it obstructs is what sets this movement's agenda.

Three consequences, all of them practical

Reading one neuron tells you little. It is a projection of many features, so its activation is a sum you cannot decompose by looking at it.

Ablating one neuron tells you little either. You have removed a mixture, so the damage is spread across everything that mixture participated in. §12 returns to this.

The number of features is not bounded by the number of neurons, so "the model has $d$ dimensions, therefore at most $d$ concepts" is simply false: Part XV's model holds sixteen in five.

Together these say the natural basis is the wrong basis. The task becomes finding the right one, which is §5, and then measuring how much of it you found, which is §6 and is the part that has not previously been done in this book.

§5 Dictionary learning

If the features are directions that are not the coordinate axes, finding them is a decomposition problem: given the activations, recover the set of directions whose sparse combinations produce them. Part XV §34 built the instrument (a sparse autoencoder, wider than the layer, with an $\ell_1$ penalty on the code) and derived why it must be both wide and sparse. In brief:

The sparse autoencoder, in one line

$$\min \;\; \lVert \mathbf{h} - W_{\text{dec}}\mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lambda \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert_1, \qquad \mathbf{a} = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\text{enc}}\mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b})$$

Wider than the layer, because §3 says there are more features than dimensions. Sparse, because §3 says sparsity is the condition that made the packing possible. Part VIII §6's sparse representations, put to a use they were not invented for.

What Part XV did not do, and what the rest of this movement needs, is score it. The construction is one thing; how much of the truth it recovers is another, and it is answerable only where the truth is known.

§6 What a dictionary actually recovers

Twelve of twenty is the number to carry out of this movement. It was obtained on a problem where the ground truth was known exactly, with a generously overcomplete dictionary, on a model small enough to have no complications. In every real application the ground truth is not known, and there is no corresponding way to notice the other eight.

Pitfall: an interpretable dictionary entry is not a verified one

A sparse autoencoder trained on a real model produces directions that, when you look at what activates them, often have clean human-readable descriptions. This is genuinely striking and it is the source of the method's reputation.

It is also not evidence that those directions are the model's features. Sparse decompositions of high-dimensional data tend to produce human-legible components: the same phenomenon that makes ICA on natural images produce edge detectors whether or not the generating process had any. The interpretation is a hypothesis. §10's intervention is what tests it.

The open problems, stated as they stand

Completeness. What fraction of the model's behaviour do the recovered features account for? Reconstruction error is the usual proxy and is not the same question.

Feature splitting. Widen the dictionary and a single entry often resolves into several finer ones. There is no principled place to stop, so "the model's features" may not be a well-defined set at all.

Verification. Nothing in the procedure checks that an entry is causally used. That is a separate measurement, and it is movement B's.

§7 Probing, revisited

Part XV §32 used probes as a diagnostic for representation quality and was careful about what they establish. That care was warranted, and this section is the measurement that shows why.

Why this had to come out this way

The hidden layer is a function of the input. The input contains the unused property. A sufficiently flexible layer therefore retains enough information to reconstruct it, whether or not anything downstream ever asks. Probing succeeds because information is present, and presence is nearly free.

So a probe measures the information content of a representation. It does not measure what the network does with it. Those are different claims, and only the second is what people usually mean when they say a model "represents" something.

The habit this should install

Whenever you read (or write) "the model encodes $X$", ask which measurement was made. If it was a probe, the claim is that $X$ is recoverable. If you want the claim that the model uses $X$, an intervention is required, and Figure 2's right panel is what one looks like.

This is not pedantry. Whole literatures have been built on probe results read as mechanism, and the correction has typically arrived only when somebody ran the ablation.

§8 What movement A establishes

Three things

The unit of analysis is a direction, not a neuron. Part XV measured why: networks store more features than they have dimensions whenever the data is sparse, which it is.

The directions can be partly recovered, and "partly" is load-bearing. Figure 1 scores the instrument for the first time in this book: 12 of 20 against PCA's 1, on a problem where the answer was known. The real cases are the ones where it is not, and there the eight are invisible.

Finding a feature is not finding a mechanism. Figure 2 reads a property out at 0.775 that the network demonstrably does not use. Every claim in this movement is correlational, and the repair is the subject of the next one.
