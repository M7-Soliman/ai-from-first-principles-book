§21 Bayes' rule

In 60 seconds

Posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior. You start with a belief, evidence arrives, you multiply your belief by how well each hypothesis predicted that evidence, then renormalise. The rule itself is one line of algebra; its importance is entirely in what the four pieces mean.

History: a minister, a posthumous paper, and a secret that lasted thirty years

Thomas Bayes was a Presbyterian minister with a mathematical hobby. He worked out the result now bearing his name and never published it. It was found among his papers after his death and read to the Royal Society in 1763 by his friend Richard Price, who wrote the introduction and may have contributed more than is usually credited.

Laplace, apparently unaware of the paper, derived the rule independently around 1774, stated it in the general form used today, and spent decades applying it to astronomy, demography and jurisprudence. Nearly everything now called Bayesian is closer to Laplace's work than to Bayes'.

Its most consequential application stayed classified for a generation. At Bletchley Park during the Second World War, Alan Turing built a sequential Bayesian procedure (he called it banburismus) for updating beliefs about German naval Enigma rotor settings as evidence accumulated, measuring the weight of evidence in units he named bans. It cut the search space enough to make the codebreaking tractable.

The work stayed secret until the 1970s. So through the decades when Fisher was arguing that Bayesian methods had no place in serious inference, one of the century's most consequential statistical successes was Bayesian, and nobody in the argument was permitted to know it.

Section 7 warned that $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ are different quantities. Often you can measure one but need the other. Bayes' rule is the conversion.

The derivation is three lines. The joint probability factors two ways:

$$P(x, y) = P(x \mid y)P(y) \qquad\text{and}\qquad P(x, y) = P(y \mid x)P(x)$$

Set them equal and divide by $P(x)$:

$$P(y \mid x) = \frac{P(x \mid y)\, P(y)}{P(x)}$$

That is the whole theorem. It is not deep; it is the definition of conditional probability rearranged. Its importance comes entirely from what the four pieces mean.

The four names
$P(y \mid x)$: the posterior
What you believe about $y$ after seeing the evidence $x$. This is what you want.
$P(x \mid y)$: the likelihood
How probable the evidence would be if $y$ were true. Usually the thing you can actually measure or model.
$P(y)$: the prior
What you believed about $y$ before any evidence arrived.
$P(x)$: the evidence, or marginal likelihood
How probable the evidence is overall. A normalising constant, computed by marginalising: $P(x) = \sum_y P(x \mid y)P(y)$.
Intuition: Bayes' rule is how beliefs should update

Read it as a process rather than a formula: $\textbf{posterior} \propto \textbf{likelihood} \times \textbf{prior}$.

You start with a belief. Evidence arrives. You multiply your belief by how well each hypothesis predicted that evidence, then renormalise so the result is a distribution again. Hypotheses that predicted the data well gain; those that predicted it poorly lose.

The denominator is doing exactly the renormalisation from §7; it is the sum of the numerator over all hypotheses. This is why you can frequently ignore it: if you only need to compare two hypotheses, the shared denominator cancels.

§22 The base rate trap

Go and read

Tversky & Kahneman (1974), Judgment under uncertainty: heuristics and biases. Not a mathematics paper, and among the most useful things you will read. It documents the fact that the error this section is about is not a lapse of arithmetic but a stable feature of how people reason, including trained clinicians, given their own data.

History: the discovery that people are predictably bad at this

The failure in this section is not carelessness. It is systematic, reproducible, and documenting it launched a field.

From the late 1960s, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky ran experiments showing that people do not reason about probability the way the mathematics prescribes. Instead they substitute simpler judgements: chiefly how representative an example seems, or how easily one comes to mind. Base rates, being abstract and unmemorable, get quietly discarded.

The medical version has been tested on doctors repeatedly since the 1970s. Given a rare disease, a highly accurate test, and a positive result, a large majority of practising physicians substantially overestimate the probability of illness, frequently by more than an order of magnitude, and in exactly the direction this section demonstrates.

Kahneman received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics in 2002 for this line of work. Tversky had died in 1996, and the prize is not awarded posthumously.

Worth holding onto: if the answer here surprised you, that is the expected result, and it is why the calculation is worth doing on paper rather than by instinct.

Here is the example that makes Bayes' rule feel necessary rather than academic. It is worth doing slowly, because nearly everyone gets it wrong on first encounter, including, in published studies, most practising physicians.

Worked example: the very accurate test

Setup. A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test for it is genuinely good: if you have the disease it comes back positive 99% of the time, and if you do not, it falsely comes back positive only 5% of the time.

You test positive. What is the probability you have the disease?

Write down your instinctive answer before reading on. Most people say something near 95%.

Naming the quantities. Let $D$ be "has the disease" and $+$ be "tests positive".

  • Prior: $P(D) = 0.001$
  • Likelihood (sensitivity): $P(+ \mid D) = 0.99$
  • False positive rate: $P(+ \mid \neg D) = 0.05$

The evidence term. There are two ways to test positive: being sick and correctly flagged, or being healthy and wrongly flagged. Marginalise over both:

$P(+) = P(+ \mid D)P(D) + P(+ \mid \neg D)P(\neg D) = (0.99)(0.001) + (0.05)(0.999) = 0.00099 + 0.04995 = 0.05094$

Apply Bayes.

$P(D \mid +) = \dfrac{P(+ \mid D)P(D)}{P(+)} = \dfrac{0.00099}{0.05094} \approx 0.0194$

About 1.9%. Despite a positive result from a 99%-accurate test, you almost certainly do not have the disease.

The result is so counterintuitive that it is worth seeing why it is nonetheless obviously correct. Forget the formula and just count people.

Pitfall: base rate neglect

The error is ignoring how rare the condition is to begin with. When a disease is rare, the healthy population is so much larger that even a small false-positive rate generates more false positives than there are true cases in total.

The lesson generalises well beyond medicine: a highly accurate test for a rare thing produces mostly false positives. This is why mass screening for rare conditions is genuinely difficult, why fraud detection systems drown in false alarms, and why a classifier reporting 99% accuracy on a dataset that is 99% one class has told you precisely nothing.

Why deep learning cares

Class imbalance is this problem wearing different clothes. Train a detector on data where 0.1% of examples are positive and you will get a model with wonderful accuracy and useless predictions. This is why practitioners report precision and recall rather than accuracy, and why techniques like resampling and class weighting exist; they are all ways of managing a base rate that would otherwise swamp the signal.

The Bayesian framing also underlies regularization, as §26 shows: a prior on the weights is literally the $P(y)$ term, and maximising the posterior instead of the likelihood is exactly what adding a penalty does.

Try it

Same test, but now applied to a high-risk group where the disease affects 1 in 20 rather than 1 in 1,000. Recompute $P(D \mid +)$. What changed?

Answer. Now $P(D) = 0.05$. The evidence term is $(0.99)(0.05) + (0.05)(0.95) = 0.0495 + 0.0475 = 0.097$, so $P(D \mid +) = 0.0495 / 0.097 \approx 0.51$.

The test did not change; the population did. Moving from a 0.1% to a 5% base rate took the posterior from 2% to 51%, a factor of 26. This is exactly why medical tests are administered to people with symptoms or risk factors rather than to everyone: choosing who to test raises the prior, and the prior is doing more work than the test's accuracy is.
