Before you start

Part 0 gave you the mathematics of change. This part gives you the objects that change is computed on, and they are the objects an intelligent system is literally made of.

§0.1 Why this part is long

Linear algebra is where most people quietly fall behind, and the reason is a mismatch of pace. The subject is usually compressed into a few dozen pages on the assumption that the reader has met it before; one major concept per paragraph, definitions arriving without motivation, almost no worked examples with actual numbers in them.

That compression is efficient for revision and disastrous for learning. Students read it, understand perhaps sixty percent, and tell themselves the rest will come later. It does not come later; it compounds. By the time neural networks arrive they are translating symbols rather than reading mathematics, and the wall they hit there was built here.

So this part moves slowly and deliberately. Every operation is given more than one reading, every claim is worked with numbers, and the two genuinely hard ideas (eigendecomposition and the singular value decomposition) are given the room they need.

§0.2 How to use it

Read one section in a sitting, and do not skip the "Try it" boxes; work them before reading on. Each section gives you the precise statement, the reason it is true, the picture, and a worked example with numbers you can check by hand, in that order and in one place.

Do not read this passively. Roughly every third page asks you to compute something. The asking is the point: linear algebra is a motor skill before it is a body of knowledge, and the gap between "I followed that" and "I can do that" is where the Part VI wall comes from.

You will meet these markers throughout:

Intuition

The picture in your head. If the formalism ever stops making sense, this is what you fall back to. Nothing here is a proof; it is the thing a proof is trying to make precise.

Watch the shapes

Dimension bookkeeping. Perhaps half of all real errors in machine learning code are shape errors, and essentially all of them are preventable by writing dimensions down. We write them down constantly.

Pitfall

A specific mistake that is easy to make and hard to spot. Usually a place where intuition from ordinary arithmetic is actively misleading.

Why deep learning cares

Where this concept shows up later in this book, and what breaks without it. If you are ever wondering why you are being made to learn something, look for one of these.

Try it

Stop reading. Compute. Answers are always given immediately after, so there is no risk of getting stranded, but look at yours first.

§0.3 What you need before page one

Genuinely not much. Be honest with yourself on these five, because everything after Section 1 assumes all of them.

  • Arithmetic with symbols. If $a(b+c) = ab + ac$ looks obvious, you are set.
  • Functions. $f(x) = 3x + 1$ takes an input and returns an output. We will be doing exactly this, with inputs that happen to be lists of numbers.
  • Derivatives, informally. You should know that a derivative measures how fast something changes. You will not need to compute any until Part III.
  • Summation notation. $\sum_{i=1}^{3} i^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14$. If that reads cleanly, you are fine. If not, read it as a for loop that accumulates, because that is precisely what it is.
  • Python. Loops, functions, lists. NumPy is taught here from scratch; you do not need it in advance.

Note what is not on that list: no prior linear algebra, no statistics, no proof-writing experience, no multivariable calculus. If you have had a linear algebra course, you will move quickly through Sections 1–8 and should slow down at Section 17.

§0.4 The one habit that matters

Before you compute anything with a matrix, write down its shape.

That is the entire habit. It sounds too small to bother with. It is the single highest-return thing in this part, and the thing that most separates people who find deep learning tractable from people who find it a fog. Shapes are the type system of machine learning: they catch a large fraction of your errors before you run anything, and they turn "I have no idea why this crashed" into a five-second fix.

We will write shapes in the form $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, meaning "$A$ is a grid of real numbers with $m$ rows and $n$ columns." Every single time.
