Part BMessage passing

Rather than choosing invariant features by hand, learn them: give every node a representation, let it exchange information with its neighbours, and repeat. Almost every graph architecture is that one loop with different choices inside it, and the loop has a failure mode built in.

§8 The loop

Go and read

Gilmer et al. (2017), Neural message passing for quantum chemistry. Read §2, which shows a dozen separately published architectures as the same loop below with different messages and aggregators, the single most clarifying page in this area.

One round

Each node collects from its neighbours, aggregates, and updates:

$$h_i^{(k+1)} = \psi\!\left(h_i^{(k)}, \; \bigoplus_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \phi\!\left(h_i^{(k)}, h_j^{(k)}\right)\right)$$

The aggregator $\bigoplus$ must be permutation-invariant (§2's sum, mean or max), which is what makes the whole layer equivariant. Everything else is an ordinary learned function.

After $K$ rounds a node's representation depends on its $K$-hop neighbourhood and nothing further. Depth is reach, which is a different quantity from what depth means anywhere else in this book.

§9 The graph convolutional network

Go and read

Kipf & Welling (2017), Semi-supervised classification with graph convolutional networks. The paper that made graph learning ordinary, and short enough to read in one sitting. Note what it does not say: the symmetric normalisation below is presented as a first-order approximation to a spectral filter, and the two-layer depth that everyone copied was a practical choice whose reason §13 measures.

The simplest useful choice

Take $\phi$ to be a shared linear map, aggregate by a normalised sum, and apply a nonlinearity:

$$H^{(k+1)} = \sigma\!\left(\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\tilde{A}\,\tilde{D}^{-1/2} H^{(k)} W^{(k)}\right)$$

with $\tilde{A} = A + I$ adding a self-loop. The normalisation keeps the scale stable across nodes of very different degree: without it, a hub's representation swamps everything downstream, which is Part IX §16's initialisation problem on an irregular graph.

Note what this layer is: a sparse matrix multiply and a dense one. All of Part I applies, and the sparsity is why it is affordable.

§10 Sampling, and why it was needed

The neighbourhood explosion

A node's $K$-hop neighbourhood grows exponentially in $K$. On a graph with average degree 20, three hops reach eight thousand nodes and four reach a hundred and sixty thousand, so a single training example can touch a large fraction of the graph, and minibatching stops meaning anything.

Neighbour sampling is the standard repair: at each layer, aggregate over a fixed-size random sample of neighbours rather than all of them. This bounds the computation per node, restores minibatching, and adds noise that acts as a regulariser. It also makes the aggregation stochastic, so the model must be evaluated with care: the same input gives different answers. GraphSAGE is the name attached to this combination: sampled neighbourhoods, a learned aggregator, and features that generalise to nodes never seen at training time.

§11 Attention on a graph

Learning the weights of the aggregation

A normalised sum weights neighbours by degree alone. Graph attention (the architecture usually called GAT) learns the weights instead: score each neighbour, softmax over the neighbourhood, and take the weighted sum.

This is Part XII's attention with the neighbourhood as the context window rather than the whole sequence, and the comparison is worth making precisely. A transformer attends to everything, which is a complete graph; a graph attention layer attends to the neighbours, which is the adjacency used as a mask. §22 returns to what happens when you remove the mask.

§12 What the aggregator decides

Three choices, three different blindnesses

Mean. Cannot count. Two nodes with three neighbours of a kind and thirty look identical, which matters whenever degree is informative.

Max. Sees only the extreme. Robust, and blind to multiplicity entirely.

Sum. Distinguishes multiset size, and is the only one of the three that can, which is why §16's expressivity result requires it.

The practical position: sum is the most expressive and the least stable on graphs with wildly varying degree. Normalising trades expressivity for conditioning, and knowing that is the trade, rather than that one is correct, is the point.

§13 Depth, which does not mean what it usually means

Over-smoothing

Over-smoothing is the convergence of node representations toward each other under repeated aggregation. Under mean aggregation (multiplication by a row-stochastic matrix), repeated application is a random walk, and a random walk on a connected graph converges to a stationary distribution that does not depend on where it started: every node's raw representation collapses to the same vector.

So the collapse is not an optimisation failure or a gradient problem. It is what the operator does, and it would happen with perfectly trained weights.

One refinement, because mean aggregation is not the operator Figure 2 runs, and the loose version of the claim above is wrong for the operator most often used in practice. The symmetric normalisation $D^{-1/2}(A+I)D^{-1/2}$ (the figure's operator) is not stochastic, and under it the rows do not go to a constant: they go to a common direction scaled by the square root of degree. Raw distances shrink by a factor of tens rather than to zero; what falls to Figure 2's plotted 0.000 is the distance between representations after normalising each to unit length, which measures alignment, not identity. Every pair becomes perfectly aligned, and the only surviving information is each node's degree. The same loss, two different operators, two different exact statements; drill C3 measures both.

§14 What is done about it

Four repairs, and what each concedes

Stay shallow. Two to four rounds, which is what most deployed models do. Concedes long-range dependence entirely.

Residual connections. Keep a path for the node's own representation, so the layer adds rather than replaces. Part X §23's ResNet, for the same reason.

Keep the initial features. Concatenate or re-inject $h^{(0)}$ at every layer, so no amount of diffusion erases what the node started with.

Normalise against collapse. Explicitly penalise or rescale to keep representations spread out.

All four treat the symptom. None removes the fact that the aggregation operator is a diffusion, and the honest summary is that depth in a graph network buys reach at a cost that the repairs reduce rather than eliminate.

§15 What movement B establishes

Three things

One loop covers the family. Collect from neighbours, aggregate invariantly, update. Convolutional, sampled and attentional variants differ in the aggregation and nothing else.

Depth is reach, not capacity. After $K$ rounds a node has seen $K$ hops, which is why the neighbourhood explodes and why sampling exists.

The operator is a diffusion, so it converges. Figure 2 measures representations collapsing to a mean pairwise distance of zero and the task following them down. That is the behaviour of the layer rather than a defect of the training.
