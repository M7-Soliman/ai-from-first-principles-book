Part AWhat generating means

§1 A different object

In 60 seconds

A discriminative model learns $p(y \mid \mathbf{x})$: given an input, produce an answer. A generative model learns $p(\mathbf{x})$ itself. The difference is not a matter of degree: you are now asked to account for everything about the data, including all the structure no particular task needed.

Three things you might want, and they are not the same

Sampling. Produce new $\mathbf{x}$ that could have come from the data. Generation in the everyday sense.

Density evaluation. Given an $\mathbf{x}$, report $p(\mathbf{x})$. What anomaly detection, compression and model comparison need.

Inference. Given an $\mathbf{x}$, recover the latent variables that explain it: Part XV's representation, arrived at from the other direction.

No single model family gives all three cheaply, and most of this part is about which two you are buying. A model that samples beautifully may be unable to tell you the probability of anything, and §41 is what that does to the practice of comparing models.

Why $p(\mathbf{x})$ is so much harder than $p(y \mid \mathbf{x})$

A classifier over 1,000 categories predicts a distribution over 1,000 outcomes. A generative model of $256\times256$ colour images predicts a distribution over $256^{196608}$ outcomes. Nothing about the discriminative apparatus scales to this by being made larger.

The manifold assumption (Part XV §5) is what makes it possible at all: almost all of that space has essentially zero probability, and the model only has to describe the vanishing region that does not. Generative modelling is the business of finding that region and putting a measure on it.

§2 The normalising constant

Most of the difficulty in this part traces to one place. It is worth isolating before any model appears.

Where $Z$ comes from

Suppose you write a model as an unnormalised score $\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x}; \theta)$: any positive function, as flexible as you like. To make it a distribution you must divide by

$$Z(\theta) = \int \tilde{p}(\mathbf{x}; \theta)\, d\mathbf{x}$$

and this integral is over the whole space. For images that is an integral in a hundred thousand dimensions, with no closed form, and it must be recomputed every time $\theta$ changes, which is every gradient step.

Why this single quantity organises the whole part

Every family in this part is a different answer to "what do we do about $Z$".

§3 Energy-based models

The form

$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{Z}\exp\big(-E(\mathbf{x})\big)$$

with $E$ any function you can compute (a neural network, typically). Low energy means high probability. The form is completely general: any strictly positive density can be written this way, by setting $E = -\log p$.

That generality is exactly why it is not free. All the difficulty has been moved into $Z$, and none of it has been removed.

Why the gradient needs samples from the model

Differentiate the log-likelihood of an energy-based model and it splits in two:

$$\nabla_\theta \log p(\mathbf{x}) = -\nabla_\theta E(\mathbf{x}) \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}' \sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta E(\mathbf{x}')\big]$$

The first term is the positive phase: push energy down at the data. The second is the negative phase: push energy up everywhere the model currently puts mass. Without it, the model would drive energy to $-\infty$ everywhere and learn nothing.

And the negative phase is an expectation under the model, so computing it requires sampling from the model (at every gradient step, from a model that is currently wrong). That is the loop this whole approach has never escaped, and §7 is why it does not work well.

§4 Which direction of KL

Part II §24 introduced the KL divergence and showed with a figure that its two directions give different answers. That difference now becomes a design decision with visible consequences.

Two objectives, two failure modes

Forward, $D_{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \parallel p_{\text{model}})$. This is what maximum likelihood minimises. It is infinite wherever the data has mass and the model has none, so the model is forced to cover everything. Where it cannot fit, it spreads: putting mass in places the data never visits.

Reverse, $D_{KL}(p_{\text{model}} \parallel p_{\text{data}})$. Infinite wherever the model has mass and the data has none, so the model is forced to stay inside the data. It may safely ignore whole regions, and it will.

Part II §24's figure is this, fitted numerically: forward KL stretches a single Gaussian across both humps of a bimodal target and puts mass in the empty valley; reverse KL locks onto one hump and abandons the other.

Why this predicts two famous complaints

Blurry samples are mode covering. A maximum-likelihood model that cannot represent the data exactly will hedge, and hedging between two sharp possibilities produces something between them that resembles neither. §22 is this, in the VAE.

Mode collapse is mode seeking. A model that is only penalised for producing implausible output, and never for failing to produce something, will find one thing it can do well and do only that. §36 is this, in the GAN (measured, and with a surprise about where it actually comes from).

The two failures are not bugs in two architectures. They are the two directions of one divergence, and knowing which one a method optimises tells you in advance which way it will fail.

§5 The taxonomy

Explicit tractable, explicit approximate, implicit

Explicit and tractable. The model defines a density you can evaluate exactly. Autoregressive models and normalising flows. You get likelihoods; you pay in architectural constraints.

Explicit and approximate. The model defines a density you cannot evaluate, so you optimise a bound or an alternative objective. VAEs, energy-based models, diffusion. You get an approximation whose looseness is generally unknown (§17).

Implicit. The model defines a sampling procedure and no density at all. GANs. You get samples and no way to score anything, including your own model.

Pitfall: the categories are not quality tiers

It is tempting to read this list as a ranking, with exact likelihood best. It is not. The family that dominated image generation for the last several years is in the middle category and cannot report a likelihood you would want to quote.

What the categories predict is which questions you will be able to answer. If you need anomaly detection, an implicit model is useless however good its samples. If you need samples, the exactness of a likelihood may buy you nothing at all; §42 is the measurement that shows those two goals coming apart.

§6 Ancestral sampling

The one sampling method that is easy

If a model factorises as a directed graph, each variable depends only on its parents, then sampling is trivial: order the variables so parents come first, and draw each from its conditional given the values already drawn.

One pass, no iteration, exact samples. Every generative model in this part that is pleasant to sample from is pleasant for this reason, and every one that is painful is painful because it is not a directed graph you can order.

Why undirected models cannot do this

An undirected model specifies compatibilities between variables, not a generative order. There is no "first" variable, so there is nothing to condition on, and the only general way to sample is to run a Markov chain until it forgets where it started.

This is the practical content of the directed/undirected distinction, and it is why the field moved decisively toward models that are directed by construction. Diffusion (§29) is an extreme case: a directed chain of a thousand steps, each one trivial.

§7 MCMC, and why it disappointed

The method

Construct a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the one you want, run it, and take late states as samples. Gibbs sampling does this by repeatedly resampling one variable from its conditional given all the others, which is cheap when the conditionals are simple.

The theory is unimprovable: under mild conditions the chain converges to the target from any starting point. The theory says nothing about when.

Why mixing is the problem, and why it is worst exactly where you need it

To move between two separated modes, a chain must pass through the low-probability region between them, and it is a chain that prefers high probability, so it does not want to. The time to cross grows exponentially with the depth of the valley.

So a Gibbs sampler on a well-trained model of realistic data gets stuck. It produces plausible samples that are all variations of one thing, and it does not report that it is stuck: the samples look fine.

Now recall §3: an energy-based model needs samples from itself at every gradient step, and the better it gets, the sharper its modes and the worse the mixing. The method's difficulty grows with its own success. That is the structural reason this family lost, and the reason Boltzmann machines are a history box (§39) rather than a movement.

Tempering methods, running parallel chains at different temperatures so mass can cross between modes at the hot end, help and do not solve it. The approach that actually worked was to stop needing samples from the model during training, which is what score matching (§23) achieves, and it is why the machinery in this movement leads where it does.
