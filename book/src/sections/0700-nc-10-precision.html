Part APrecision and its failures

§1 What a floating point number actually is

In 60 seconds

A float is scientific notation in binary: a sign, a fixed number of significant bits, and an exponent. The consequence that matters is that the significant bits are fixed in count, not in position, so the gap between representable numbers grows with magnitude. Near 1 the gap is about $10^{-16}$; near $10^{16}$ the gap is about 1.

Part 0 §26 said computers store approximations. Here is what the approximation actually is.

A 64-bit float divides its bits into three fields: one sign bit, 11 exponent bits, and 52 mantissa bits holding the significant digits. The value is

$$(-1)^{\text{sign}} \times 1.m \times 2^{e}$$

which is scientific notation with a base of 2 instead of 10. The mantissa holds the digits; the exponent says where the point goes.

Intuition: a fixed number of digits, sliding along the number line

Imagine a calculator that shows exactly 16 significant digits and an exponent. It can represent 1.234567890123456 and it can represent 1.234567890123456 × 1050. What it cannot do is represent a number needing 17 significant digits, at any magnitude.

So precision is relative, not absolute. You always get about 16 significant digits, but 16 digits of a number near $10^{20}$ means the smallest change you can express is around $10^{4}$. The absolute gap grows with the number.

Machine epsilon

The single most useful constant in numerical work:

Definition: machine epsilon

$\epsilon_{\text{mach}}$ is the gap between $1$ and the next representable number above it. For 64-bit floats, $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} = 2^{-52} \approx 2.22 \times 10^{-16}$.

Equivalently: adding anything smaller than about $10^{-16}$ to 1 leaves 1 unchanged. Not approximately unchanged, but exactly unchanged, because the result rounds back to the nearest representable value, which is 1.

Pitfall: accumulating small numbers into a large one

Summing a million small values into a running total is a common pattern and a common bug. Once the total is large relative to the individual terms, each addition rounds away part of what it was adding, and the error compounds.

Two defences. Sum in pairs rather than sequentially, so the running totals stay comparable in size; this is what np.sum does internally, which is one of several reasons it beats a Python loop. Or use compensated summation, which keeps a second variable tracking the accumulated rounding error and adds it back.

History: the standard that had to be fought for

Before 1985, every manufacturer did this differently. Word sizes differed, rounding rules differed, and whether a machine even had a way to signal that a result was invalid differed. The same program produced different answers on different hardware, and there was no way to reason about which was right.

The IEEE 754 standard fixed the representation, the rounding behaviour, and (crucially) the special values inf and nan that let a computation signal failure rather than silently continue with garbage. William Kahan drove it through a committee process against real commercial resistance, since every vendor's existing hardware was the incumbent and the standard obsoleted parts of it.

He received the Turing Award in 1989 for the work. It is worth appreciating what it bought: every numerical result in this book is reproducible on any machine you run it on, which was simply not true a decade before.

In the framework: why training runs in half precision on purpose

Everything in this section is a property of a dtype, and a deep learning library will happily hand you four of them. Their differences are not academic; choosing among them is one of the few decisions that changes both your results and your bill.

Read the last two rows against each other

Both are 16 bits, so both halve memory and roughly double throughput against float32. But they spend those sixteen bits differently, and the difference is the whole story of modern training.

float16 keeps more precision (three decimal digits against two) and pays for it with exponent bits. Its largest representable value is 65,504. Not $10^{38}$; sixty-five thousand. A single activation, gradient or intermediate sum crossing that becomes inf, and by §2 the run is then poisoned with nan. In a network of any depth this is not a remote possibility, it is a routine occurrence.

bfloat16 makes the opposite trade. It keeps float32's exponent field exactly (same $3.4\times10^{38}$ ceiling, same tiny floor) and throws away mantissa bits instead, leaving roughly two decimal digits. It is a truncated float32: anything float32 can represent in magnitude, bfloat16 can represent in magnitude, just coarsely.

And that trade turns out to be the right one, for a reason this whole part is about. Imprecision degrades a result; overflow destroys it. A gradient known to two digits is perfectly serviceable; §14 will show it is a noisy sample anyway, so a little more noise costs almost nothing. A gradient that became inf ends the run. So the format that cannot overflow wins, even though it is the less accurate one, and that is why bfloat16 became the default for large-scale training while float16 requires an elaborate apparatus of loss scaling to be usable at all.

What this means in practice

Mixed precision is the standard arrangement: keep a float32 copy of the weights, compute the forward and backward passes in 16 bits, and accumulate the updates back in 32. You get the speed where the work is and the precision where the accumulation happens, which is exactly the pitfall about summing small numbers into a large one, solved by putting the running total in a wider type.

with torch.autocast(device_type="cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
    loss = model(x)          # forward runs in 16 bits
loss.backward()              # gradients accumulate into float32 parameters

The habit that follows: 16 bits for the network, 32 or 64 for anything that looks like linear algebra. A covariance matrix, a solve, an eigendecomposition or a metric computed over a whole test set are all places where §5's condition number can be large and two decimal digits is not a budget you can spend.

§2 Overflow, underflow, and the operations that break

Three failure modes, each with a characteristic signature.

Overflow

The result exceeds the largest representable value, about $1.8 \times 10^{308}$, and becomes inf. The dangerous part is what happens next: $\texttt{inf} - \texttt{inf}$, $\texttt{inf}/\texttt{inf}$ and $0 \times \texttt{inf}$ all produce nan, and nan contaminates every subsequent operation. One overflow forty layers deep poisons the entire computation, and the traceback points nowhere near the cause.

Underflow

The result is smaller than the smallest representable positive value and becomes exactly zero. This is often harmless and occasionally catastrophic, specifically when the zero then appears in a denominator, or inside a logarithm. $\log(0) = -\infty$, and you are back to nan from the other direction.

Pitfall: nan is not equal to itself

By definition, nan != nan evaluates to True. This is deliberate and occasionally useful: it is how you detect one. x != x is True exactly when x is nan.

It also means nan silently breaks sorting, deduplication, and any equality check. If a data pipeline starts behaving impossibly, look for one.

§3 Catastrophic cancellation

In 60 seconds

Subtracting two nearly equal numbers destroys significant digits. The inputs each carry 16 good digits; if they agree in the first 15, the difference has one good digit and fifteen digits of noise. Nothing overflowed, nothing warned, and the answer is simply wrong. The fix is always to rearrange the algebra so the subtraction does not happen.

This is the subtlest of the failure modes and the one that catches experienced people, because unlike overflow it produces a perfectly ordinary-looking number.

Worked: where the digits go

Suppose two quantities are known to 16 significant digits:

$a = 1.2345678901234567$,   $b = 1.2345678901234000$

Each is accurate to about $10^{-16}$. Their difference is $5.67 \times 10^{-14}$, but only the last few digits of each input contributed to it, and those were exactly the digits carrying rounding error.

The result has perhaps two reliable digits, down from sixteen. Fourteen digits of accuracy vanished in a single subtraction, and nothing anywhere reported a problem.

Worked: the quadratic formula, done properly

The roots of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ are usually written

$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$

When $b$ is large and positive, $\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac} \approx b$. The root taking the $+$ sign then computes $-b + (\text{something almost exactly } b)$: catastrophic cancellation, and the answer loses most of its digits.

The fix uses the fact that the two roots multiply to $c/a$. Compute the safe root first (the one whose subtraction does not cancel) and get the other by division:

$$q = -\tfrac{1}{2}\left(b + \operatorname{sign}(b)\sqrt{b^2-4ac}\right), \qquad x_1 = \frac{q}{a}, \qquad x_2 = \frac{c}{q}$$

The $\operatorname{sign}(b)$ ensures the two terms in $q$ always add rather than subtract, so nothing cancels. Both roots then come out accurate. This reformulation is standard in every serious numerical library, and it is invisible to anyone who has only seen the textbook version.

Why this matters

Cancellation is why Part II §11's identity $\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$ is dangerous in code. When the mean is large and the variance small, those two terms are big and nearly equal, and the subtraction can return a small negative number, which is mathematically impossible for a variance.

It is also why numerical differentiation has a floor. The central difference $\big(f(x+h) - f(x-h)\big)/2h$ improves as $h$ shrinks, until cancellation in the numerator overwhelms the gain. Part 0's drill C15 observed this empirically; the cause is this section.

§4 Writing code that survives

The techniques are few and they recur. This is the whole catalogue.

TechniqueWhat it does
Work in log spaceProducts become sums; underflow becomes impossible. Every likelihood, everywhere.

Rearrange to avoid subtractionThe quadratic formula above; also log1p and expm1, which exist precisely for this.
Clamp defensivelyClip probabilities to $[\epsilon, 1-\epsilon]$ before taking a log; clamp near-zero negatives to 0 before a square root.

import numpy as np

# log1p and expm1 exist because of section 3
x = 1e-16
print(np.log(1 + x))     ### 0.0        -- the 1+x rounded to 1
print(np.log1p(x))     ### 1e-16      -- correct

# variance: the tidy identity versus the stable algorithm
a = np.array([1e8, 1e8 + 1, 1e8 + 2])
print((a**2).mean() - a.mean()**2)   ### 2.0    -- wrong by 3×, and silently so
print(np.var(a))                       ### 0.667 -- correct
Try it

You need $\log(1 + e^{x})$ for $x = 800$. The direct computation overflows. What is the answer, and how do you get it?

Answer. For large $x$, $e^x$ dwarfs the 1, so $\log(1+e^x) \approx \log(e^x) = x$. The answer is essentially exactly 800.

The general stable form, which is correct for every $x$ rather than just large ones:

$$\log(1+e^x) = \max(x, 0) + \log\!\left(1 + e^{-|x|}\right)$$

The exponent $-|x|$ is never positive, so it can never overflow, and the log1p form of the second term handles the small-$x$ case without cancellation. This is the softplus implementation from Part 0 §18, and now you know why it is written that way.
