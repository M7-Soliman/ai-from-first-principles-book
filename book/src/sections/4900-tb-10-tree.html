Part AOne tree

A decision tree is the only model family in this book whose capacity you can count, whose decisions you can read off, and whose bias is a geometric statement you can draw. It is also, alone, mediocre. This movement builds it and then establishes exactly what it cannot do, which is the specification for the two movements that follow.

§1 Why a table is not an image

What tabular data does not have

Part X earned convolution from a property of images: a pixel's neighbours are meaningful, and the same filter applies everywhere. Part XI earned recurrence from a property of sequences.

A table has neither. Column 7 is not adjacent to column 8 in any sense: reorder the columns and nothing about the problem changes. Columns are on unrelated scales and often unrelated kinds: an age, a country, a count, a boolean. There is no translation to be equivariant to and no locality to exploit.

So the architectural priors that made Parts X to XII work are unavailable, and the family that works instead is the one whose prior is axis-aligned thresholds, which is exactly what a table's columns are.

§2 The model

A decision tree

Repeatedly split the data on one feature at one threshold. Each leaf holds a constant: the mean of its training targets for regression, the class proportions for classification. Prediction is walking from the root to a leaf and reading the constant.

The function is piecewise constant on axis-aligned boxes. That is the entire hypothesis class, and everything the family does well or badly follows from it.

§3 Choosing a split

Greedy, by impurity reduction

For regression, choose the split minimising the sum of squared deviations within the two children. Writing $G$ for a node's sum of targets and $n$ for its count, the quantity to maximise is

$$\frac{G_L^2}{n_L} + \frac{G_R^2}{n_R} - \frac{G^2}{n}$$

For classification the same shape with Gini impurity or entropy in place of the squared error. In every case the choice is greedy: the best split now, with no lookahead.

Why greedy, and what it costs

Finding the optimal tree is NP-hard. Greedy growth is the concession, and it has a specific failure: a split that is worthless alone but essential in combination will not be taken. XOR is the canonical case: neither feature reduces impurity at the root, so a depth-1 search finds nothing, though a depth-2 tree represents it exactly.

In practice trees grow past that by luck and by noise, and the failure is real but rarely fatal. It is worth knowing because it explains why boosted shallow trees, which are strongly greedy, still capture interactions: the ensemble composes what one tree would not find.

§4 Finding the threshold

Exact, and the approximation everyone uses

Exact. Sort the feature, sweep the candidate thresholds, accumulate the sums. Every distinct value is a candidate, so the cost is a sort per feature per node.

Histogram. Bin each feature once, up front, into a fixed number of buckets at quantiles of the training data. A split search is then a $\mathrm{bincount}$ over bins rather than a sort over rows, and the histogram of a child can be obtained by subtracting its sibling's from its parent's, so only the smaller child needs computing.

This is the change that made gradient boosting practical at scale, and §22 measures what the approximation costs.

§5 Depth, and the curve you can point to

Why the tree shows the curve so cleanly

Part XXIII §23 measured a network fitting random labels and generalising anyway, which is why its capacity does not predict its behaviour. A tree has no such escape: each additional level doubles the boxes, each box holds fewer points, and each leaf's constant is estimated from less data. Capacity and variance move together with nothing intervening.

So a tree is where the classical theory is at its most useful, and it is worth noticing that the same theory told us almost nothing in Part XXIII.

§6 Stopping, and pruning

Three controls, and which one to reach for

Maximum depth. Blunt, effective, and the one to tune first.

Minimum samples per leaf. Adapts to density (deep where there is data, shallow where there is not), which is usually what you want and is why it is the better of the two.

Cost-complexity pruning. Grow fully, then remove the subtree whose removal costs least per leaf saved, generating a nested family indexed by a penalty. This is Part XXIII §20's structural risk minimisation, arrived at independently and decades earlier.

Once the tree is in an ensemble, all of this matters less than it looks. A boosted tree wants to be shallow because the ensemble supplies the capacity; a bagged tree wants to be deep because the averaging supplies the variance reduction.

§7 What movement A establishes

Three things

The prior is axis-aligned boxes, which is the right prior for a table and the wrong one for an image, the exact mirror of Part X's argument.

Capacity is countable and behaves classically. Figure 1's test error turns at depth 3 and rises by half again by depth 16, with training error at 0.071. The theory Part XXIII found vacuous for networks is informative here.

One tree is not enough. Greedy growth misses interactions, the boxes make every boundary a staircase, and the variance is large. The next two movements are two different repairs, and they repair different things.
