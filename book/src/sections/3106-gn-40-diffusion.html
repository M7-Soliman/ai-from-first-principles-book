Part DScore and diffusion

Everything so far has worked around the normalising constant. This movement learns an object that never contained it, and then discovers that the object is enough to generate from, which nobody expected when it was proposed.

§23 Score matching

Fit the gradient instead of the density

Part XV §12 defined the score, $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p(\mathbf{x})$. The reason to want it is one line: the normalising constant is a function of $\theta$ and not of $\mathbf{x}$, so

$$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p(\mathbf{x};\theta) = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log \tilde{p}(\mathbf{x};\theta) - \underbrace{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log Z(\theta)}_{= \; 0}$$

$Z$ disappears. Not approximated, not bounded: differentiated away. So fit the model's score to the data's:

$$J(\theta) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}} \big\| \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x};\theta) - \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}) \big\|^2$$

Why this is not circular, though it looks it

The objective mentions $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p_{\text{data}}$, which you do not know. The reason it is usable anyway is an integration by parts, and the result is worth stating because it is what makes the whole approach possible:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \left[ \frac{\partial^2 \log p_{\text{model}}}{\partial x_j^2} + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial \log p_{\text{model}}}{\partial x_j}\right)^{\!2} \right] \;+\; \text{const}$$

The unknown data score is gone, absorbed into a constant that does not depend on $\theta$. What remains involves only the model and expectations over the data, both computable.

And note what has been avoided: no partition function, no MCMC, no bound, and no samples from the model during training. §7's loop is broken.

Pitfall: the second derivative, and the cost that killed it

That objective needs $\partial^2 \log p / \partial x_j^2$ for every dimension: the diagonal of the Hessian with respect to the input. For a network with $n$ inputs that costs $O(n)$ backward passes per example.

At $n = 2$ this is free. At $n = 196{,}608$ it is fatal. Score matching in its original form is unusable at the scale anyone cares about, and this is why it sat in the literature for over a decade as a technically elegant method with no applications.

§25 is the fix, and the fix removes the second derivative entirely.

§24 Where the score has no signal

Why this is the more serious problem

Score matching's objective is an expectation over $p_{\text{data}}$. Gradient contributions arrive only where there is data. Away from the data the estimated score is whatever the network happened to extrapolate, constrained by nothing.

Now suppose you want to generate. The natural method is Langevin dynamics: start somewhere and follow the score uphill, with noise:

$$\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_t + \tfrac{\eta}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x}_t) + \sqrt{\eta}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$$

Started from noise, the very first steps are taken in the region the figure says received no training signal at all. The walk is guided by a field that was never fitted, and it does not find the data.

There is a second failure that the figure also explains. Even if the walk reaches the data, the score field between two well-separated modes is near zero, so the chain has no information about their relative weights. Langevin dynamics on an exact score recovers the modes and gets the mixing proportions wrong.

Both failures have the same cause and the same cure: the data is too concentrated. Blur it.

§25 Denoising score matching

Go and read

Vincent (2011), A connection between score matching and denoising autoencoders. The identity this entire movement turns on, in four pages. Read it before Part XVI's later sections rather than after: once you accept that reconstruction-minus-input is a score, diffusion stops being a recipe and becomes a consequence.

The identity, from Part XV

Corrupt the data with Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$ and the score of the smoothed density has an estimator with no second derivative and no unknown constant:

$$\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log p_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\mathbf{x} - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}}{\sigma^2} \;\Big|\; \tilde{\mathbf{x}}\right]$$

which is estimated by training a network to predict the clean point from the corrupted one: a plain regression. Part XV §12 measured this: mean cosine 0.9931 against the analytic score of the smoothed density.

The second-derivative cost is gone. The training signal now reaches wherever the corruption puts mass, which §24's figure says is most of the space. Both of §23's and §24's problems are solved by the same move.

Pitfall: one noise level cannot work

A single $\sigma$ forces a choice between two failures, and both are real.

Small $\sigma$: the target is nearly the true score, and coverage is nearly as bad as §24's, so the sampler still cannot start.

Large $\sigma$: coverage is excellent and the target is the score of a badly blurred distribution. Part XV §12 measured the distortion: against the unsmoothed score, the magnitude ratio falls to 0.609.

There is no setting that is right for both. The resolution is the one move that turns this collection of results into a working generative model, and it is simple enough to state in a sentence: use every noise level at once.

§26 Many noise levels

Go and read

Song & Ermon (2019), Generative modeling by estimating gradients of the data distribution. The paper that identifies exactly the two failures of §24 (no training signal away from the data, and no information about relative mode weights) and fixes both with a schedule of noise levels. Our Figure 3 is its argument, measured.

One network, conditioned on how noisy the input is

Choose a schedule $\sigma_1 < \sigma_2 < \cdots < \sigma_T$, spanning from almost nothing to larger than the data. Train a single network $s_\theta(\mathbf{x}, \sigma)$ on the denoising objective at all levels, sampling a level at random for each example:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\, \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\, \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \Big[ \lambda(\sigma) \big\| s_\theta(\mathbf{x} + \sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \sigma) + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}/\sigma \big\|^2 \Big]$$

At the largest $\sigma$ the smoothed distribution is nearly a wide Gaussian, so the score is known almost everywhere, which is exactly where a sampler starts. At the smallest, the score is nearly the true one, which is where it finishes.

Then generate by starting at the largest level and walking down: follow the score at $\sigma_T$, then at $\sigma_{T-1}$, and so on. Every step is taken in a region where that level's score was trained, which is the property §24 said was missing.

§27 The forward process

Corruption as a fixed Markov chain

The standard formulation writes the corruption as a chain that gradually destroys the data:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \; \beta_t I\big)$$

with a schedule $\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_T$. It has no parameters and nothing is learned about it.

Its useful property is that any number of steps composes in closed form. With $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$,

$$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$$

so training at step $t$ costs one draw, not $t$ of them. The scaling by $\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}$ keeps the variance bounded, so $\mathbf{x}_T$ approaches a standard Gaussian rather than diverging, which is what makes "start from noise" well defined.

§28 What the network predicts

Three equivalent parameterisations

The network can be asked for the noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, the clean point $\mathbf{x}_0$, or the score. They are related by identities and carry the same information:

$$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \log p_t(\mathbf{x}_t) = -\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}$$

Predicting $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ is the usual choice and it is not arbitrary: it makes the target unit-variance at every noise level, so one loss weighting works across the whole schedule. Predicting $\mathbf{x}_0$ has a target whose difficulty varies enormously with $t$, and the v-parameterisation interpolates between the two and behaves better at the extremes.

The training loop is now: draw a data point, draw $t$, draw noise, corrupt, predict the noise, take a squared-error step. There is no adversary, no bound, no sampling from the model and no partition function. It is regression.

§29 Sampling, and what it recovers

Three ways to walk back down

Ancestral (DDPM). Step through every $t$, sampling from the true reverse posterior: the distribution of $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$ given both $\mathbf{x}_t$ and $\mathbf{x}_0$, which is Gaussian, with a mean built from the network's noise prediction $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta$ (via §28's identity for $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$) and a variance fixed by the schedule alone:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\Big),\ \tilde{\beta}_t I\right), \qquad \tilde{\beta}_t = \beta_t\,\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}$$

"The correct amount of fresh noise" means drawing with variance $\tilde{\beta}_t$, not $\beta_t$ itself; the posterior is narrower than the forward step because conditioning on $\mathbf{x}_t$ already rules out most of what the forward step could have done. This is also where the classic Ho et al. (2020) derivation arrives from a different direction: minimising the ELBO's per-step term $D_{KL}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid\mathbf{x}_t)\big)$ reduces to matching this same mean, which is the identical $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$-regression §28 reached from denoising score matching. Bound and gradient-matching are two routes to one objective, not competing ones. Highest quality, $T$ network evaluations, and $T$ is often several hundred.

Deterministic (DDIM). Drop the injected noise and follow the implied ordinary differential equation. Far fewer steps for similar quality, and, because the map from noise to sample is now deterministic, a latent space you can interpolate in.

Higher-order solvers. The deterministic view says sampling is an ODE, so any ODE solver applies. This is where the step counts of 20–50 come from, and it was a change of perspective rather than of model. All three views (the discrete chain here, DDIM's ODE and Langevin's SDE of §24) are discretisations of one continuous-time process; Song et al. (2021) write down the SDE itself, with the forward corruption and the reverse sampler as its two time directions, and the ODE above as the special case with the noise term removed.

Why the weights being right is the claim worth making

A picture of samples that land on the modes establishes little; §36's GAN does that while omitting modes entirely, and §42 is about how badly visual inspection fails.

The mixing weights are the part a mode-seeking method gets wrong, and they are checkable here because the distribution was constructed. Recovering them to within the sampling noise of a real draw of the same size is the statement that the model learned the distribution and not merely its support.

Note also the cost structure, against §11. Sampling takes a fixed number of network evaluations set by the schedule, not by the dimension of what is being generated. A million-pixel image takes the same number of steps as a two-dimensional point. That is why this family displaced autoregressive models for images and did not for text.

§30 Conditioning and guidance

Go and read

Ho & Salimans (2022), Classifier-free diffusion guidance. One network, label dropout during training, and a weight at sampling time. The paper is explicit that guidance samples from a distribution proportional to $p(\mathbf{x})p(c \mid \mathbf{x})^w$ rather than from the conditional: a point routinely lost in retelling, and the one that explains Figure 5's collapse.

Classifier-free guidance

Train one network on both the conditional and unconditional problem, by dropping the condition on some fraction of examples. At sampling time combine them:

$$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing) + w\big[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, c) - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \varnothing)\big]$$

At $w=1$ this is the conditional model. Above 1 it extrapolates along the direction the condition points, amplifying whatever the condition contributed.

Pitfall: it is not a diversity-for-fidelity trade

The standard description is that guidance trades diversity for fidelity, monotonically. The measurement says something different and more useful.

Up to $w \approx 2$, both improve: adherence reaches 100%, spread stays near the truth's, and typicality rises. Past the peak, the samples do not become a tighter cluster around the mode; the spread grows while the density collapses by two orders of magnitude. They are being pushed off the distribution, outward past the mode, into a region containing no data.

So the failure at high guidance is not over-concentration. It is extrapolation along a direction that was only ever valid locally, and "reduce $w$ if samples look too similar" is advice for a symptom that does not occur. The number to watch is a density or a distance to the data, not a spread.

The honest reading of the sampled distribution: guidance does not sample from $p(\mathbf{x} \mid c)$ at all. It samples from something proportional to $p(\mathbf{x})\,p(c \mid \mathbf{x})^w$, which for $w \neq 1$ is a different distribution, one chosen because it looks better, which is §12's overruling of the model, again.

§31 The schedule

Why this is the hyperparameter that matters

The schedule decides how much of training is spent at each noise level, and the levels are not equally useful. Very high noise teaches almost nothing: the answer is nearly "predict the input". Very low noise teaches fine detail that barely affects the sample's structure. The informative band is in the middle and it is narrow.

A linear schedule spends too many steps at the destructive end. The cosine schedule, used for the figures here, holds more of the trajectory in the informative region, and it improved results across the board on a change that touches no architecture.

This is worth flagging as a general pattern rather than a detail. In this family the corruption process is part of the model, and the design decisions that most affect results are decisions about corruption: the schedule, the parameterisation of §28, the loss weighting $\lambda(\sigma)$. A field trained on architecture search found this counterintuitive for several years.

§32 Flow matching

The same destination, reached more directly

The deterministic sampler of §29 says generation is following an ODE from noise to data. Flow matching starts there: pick a path from noise to data, the straight line is the usual choice, and train a network to predict the velocity along it:

$$\mathbf{x}_t = (1-t)\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon} + t\,\mathbf{x}_0, \qquad \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\big\|v_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) - (\mathbf{x}_0 - \boldsymbol{\epsilon})\big\|^2$$

No forward Markov chain, no noise schedule, no variational bound. A regression onto a velocity field, with straight paths that a solver can follow in few steps.

The relationship to §26 is close: with particular choices flow matching and diffusion give the same objective. The value is in the framing: the path is now an explicit design choice rather than something inherited from a corruption process. The continuous-time view these paths live in, the reverse-time SDE and the probability-flow ODE that shares its marginals, is developed in Part XXVIII §§36–37.

§33 What this movement establishes

Why this is where the field arrived

The route ran: eliminate $Z$ by learning a gradient (§23); make that affordable and well-covered by adding noise (§25); repair the resulting bias by using every noise level (§26); and then notice that walking down the levels is a sampler (§29).

What arrives has properties no earlier family had together. Training is a stable regression: no adversary, no bound, no samples from the model. Sampling cost is set by a schedule you choose rather than by the dimension of the output. Conditioning is a single extra input. And the whole thing rests on an identity that was published in 2011 and sat unused for a decade because nobody needed it yet.

What it does not give you is a likelihood you would want to quote, which is the next movement's problem and the one after that's.
