Part DPolicy gradients

Stop learning values and learn the policy directly, by gradient ascent on the thing you actually want. It works, it has a beautiful derivation, and its practical history is almost entirely about controlling the variance of one estimator.

§24 Optimising the policy directly

The objective

Parameterise $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ and maximise the expected return $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[G(\tau)]$ by ascending $\nabla_\theta J$.

Four reasons to prefer this over value-based control. It handles continuous action spaces, where $\max_a Q$ is itself an optimisation problem. It learns stochastic policies, which are sometimes optimal: under partial observability, and in games. It changes the policy smoothly, where a small change in $Q$ can flip a greedy action discontinuously. And it optimises the objective you care about rather than a proxy for it.

§25 The policy gradient theorem

Differentiating through a distribution you sample from

The difficulty is that $\theta$ appears in the distribution being averaged over, not in the integrand. The identity that resolves it is one line:

$$\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x) = p_\theta(x)\,\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)$$

so that

$$\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\Big[\textstyle\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, G_t\Big]$$

An expectation you can sample. Note what is absent: any derivative of the environment. The transition kernel does not appear, so no model is needed and no differentiability of the world is assumed.

This is Part XVI §16's other estimator, the score-function or REINFORCE gradient, which that part set aside for its variance. Here it cannot be set aside, because the environment is not reparameterisable.

Reading the update

$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ is the direction that makes action $a$ more likely. Multiplying by $G_t$ scales it by how well things went. So the update is: make what you did more likely, in proportion to how good the outcome was.

Which immediately exposes the weakness. If all returns are large and positive, every action taken is reinforced, and the policy improves only through the differences between returns, differences that are swamped by the common part. §27 is the fix, and it is the single most important practical detail in this movement.

One smaller approximation, worth naming rather than leaving silent: the exact gradient of the discounted objective carries a $\gamma^t$ inside the sum, $\mathbb{E}[\sum_t \gamma^t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,G_t]$, down-weighting late time steps because they contribute less to $J$. The formula above, and every practical implementation including this part's, drops it and weights every step equally. That is the universal convention rather than an oversight, but it means the estimator in routine use targets the undiscounted step distribution, not quite the discounted objective it is presented as.

§26 REINFORCE

The algorithm, in full

Run an episode. For each step compute the return $G_t$. Take a gradient step on $\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) G_t$. Repeat.

Unbiased, model-free, and about six lines of code. Also close to unusable on anything substantial without the next three sections, because of its variance.

§27 Baselines

Subtract anything that does not depend on the action

$$\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\Big[\textstyle\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) \big(G_t - b(s_t)\big)\Big]$$

for any $b$ that does not depend on $a_t$. The expectation is unchanged, because

$$\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big] = \sum_a \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a\mid s) = \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s) = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0$$

The expected score function is zero. That three-step identity is why a baseline is free, and it is worth carrying because the same fact appears throughout statistics.

The best baseline is close to $V^\pi(s)$, which makes $G_t - b(s_t)$ an estimate of the advantage: how much better this action was than the state's average. Actions that are merely typical get no push.

§28 Actor–critic

Learn the baseline too

An actor $\pi_\theta$ and a critic $V_\phi$, trained together. The critic is fitted by TD (§16); the actor uses the critic as its baseline, and can also use the critic to bootstrap the return rather than waiting for the episode to end.

That second use is the more consequential: it converts the return from a full trajectory sum into a one-step or $n$-step quantity, which removes most of the remaining variance and allows updates on continuing tasks.

And it reintroduces bias, since the critic is wrong. So the whole movement's variance problem has been converted into movement C's bias problem, which is a better problem, because §17 says a transient bias beats a permanent variance.

§29 Advantage estimation

Choosing where on the spectrum to sit

The $n$-step advantage bootstraps after $n$ steps: small $n$ is low variance and high bias, large $n$ the reverse: §17's trade, in the actor's target.

Generalised advantage estimation takes an exponentially weighted average over all $n$, controlled by $\lambda$:

$$\hat{A}_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^k \delta_{t+k}, \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$$

$\lambda = 0$ gives the one-step advantage, $\lambda = 1$ gives the Monte Carlo advantage, and values around 0.95 are the usual choice. It is TD($\lambda$) applied to advantages, and it is computed in one backward pass over the trajectory.

§30 Why step size is the central difficulty

A bad step is not recoverable here

In supervised learning a step that is too large produces a worse model, and the next batch, drawn from the same fixed distribution, pulls it back.

Here a step that is too large produces a worse policy, which then collects worse data, from which you learn a worse policy. There is no fixed dataset to recover against, and a collapse can be permanent.

That asymmetry is why this movement's history is a sequence of increasingly careful ways to ask the same question: how far can the policy move before the data it collects stops being informative about where to go next?

§31 Trust regions, and then clipping

TRPO

Maximise a surrogate objective subject to a constraint on the KL divergence between the old and new policies:

$$\max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a\mid s)} \hat{A}\Big] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbb{E}\big[D_{KL}(\pi_{\text{old}} \,\|\, \pi_\theta)\big] \leq \delta$$

The constraint is in policy space, not parameter space, which is the right place: the same parameter change can barely move one policy and destroy another. It comes with a monotonic improvement guarantee, and it requires a constrained optimisation with conjugate gradients and a Fisher-vector product per step.

PPO

Replace the constraint with a clipped objective:

$$L = \mathbb{E}\Big[\min\big(\rho_t \hat{A}_t,\; \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\big)\Big], \qquad \rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}$$

The $\min$ is what makes it work, and it is worth reading carefully. When the advantage is positive, the objective stops improving once $\rho > 1+\epsilon$: no reward for pushing further. When the advantage is negative, it stops once $\rho < 1-\epsilon$. Either way the gradient vanishes outside the trust region rather than being penalised, so a step cannot be bought by accepting a penalty.

§32 Why PPO won

Go and read

Engstrom et al. (2020), Implementation matters in deep RL: a case study on PPO and TRPO. The replication that separated PPO's clipped objective from the code-level details shipped alongside it, and found the details doing much of the work. An uncomfortable paper and a model of how to check a method: Part XIV §21's ablation discipline applied to a whole subfield.

Not because it is better in theory

TRPO has the guarantee; PPO has none. What PPO has is first-order updates, ordinary minibatch SGD, several epochs of reuse per batch of experience, and about twenty lines of code. It is TRPO's insight with the machinery removed.

The honest qualification, established by careful reimplementation studies: a substantial part of PPO's reported advantage comes from implementation details that are not in the paper's equations: advantage normalisation, observation normalisation, value-function clipping, learning rate annealing, orthogonal initialisation, gradient clipping, and an entropy bonus added to the objective to slow the policy from collapsing to a point mass too early. Removing them costs more than removing the clipping does.

That is worth stating plainly rather than treating as gossip. It is Part XIV §21's ablation problem at the scale of a whole subfield: a method's reported benefit was partly the benefit of a well-tuned codebase, and it took deliberate replication work to find out.
