§40 When to reach for the frequency domain

Five situations where it pays, and one where it does not

You are resampling anything. Changing resolution, striding, decimating, or changing a sample rate. Filter first, always: §3's damage cannot be undone afterwards.

Your kernel is long. Above Figure 3's crossover the transform is not an optimisation but the difference between affordable and not.

The signal is periodic or nearly so. Vibration, power systems, seasonal data, audio pitch. The structure is a peak in one domain and a mess in the other.

You need to characterise noise. White, pink and periodic interference are trivially distinguishable by spectrum and hard to tell apart by eye.

Your domain has a Laplacian. Graphs, meshes, manifolds: §25's construction gives you a frequency decomposition with no signal in sight.

And where it does not: when the signal is transient and non-stationary and you care about individual events. A spectrum of a whole recording averages away what you were looking for, and §23's short-time version is a compromise with a resolution limit Figure 5 measures.

§41 The checks that catch a signals bug

Four, and the first two take a minute

Parseval. Sum the squared samples, sum the squared coefficients, divide. If §11's identity does not hold, your normalisation is wrong, and a wrong normalisation is the most common defect in hand-written spectral code, because it does not throw and does not look wrong.

Round-trip. Transform and invert. You should recover the input to machine precision, and the imaginary part of the recovered real signal should be at rounding level. If it is not, your spectrum is not conjugate-symmetric and something upstream is wrong.

The shift test. Shift the input by one sample and check the output moves the way it should. This is Part XXVI §1's permutation test in the translation group, it is three lines, and it is what produced Figure 1.

A known input. Feed a pure tone at a frequency that lands exactly on a bin. You should get one non-zero bin. Anything else is a windowing or normalisation error, and you have just measured it.

§42 What to report

Six things

The sampling rate and the record length. Together they fix both axes of every spectrum you show, and without them the plot is unreadable.

The window. Which function and how long. Figure 5 is why.

Whether you filtered before downsampling. If you did not, say so, because Figure 6's right panel is what your spectrum contains.

Whether the quantity is amplitude or power, and whether the axis is logarithmic. An exponent quoted without this is ambiguous by a factor of two.

What you did with phase. Discarding it is a decision, and §18 measures what it costs.

The estimator, checked. If you fit an exponent, fit it first to synthetic data whose exponent you set. Figure 6 does this on every run, and it is the reason the failure recorded against that figure is a failure of the prediction rather than of the measurement.

§44 What is unsolved

Four, distinguished by kind

Not yet measured. How much accuracy anti-aliased downsampling is worth across tasks. Figure 1 measures the inconsistency; what it costs at the metric, at scale, on tasks people care about, is a smaller literature than the phenomenon deserves.

Not currently measurable. Whether spectral bias helps or hurts a large model on real data. It is measurable on a sum of sinusoids, as Figure 7 does. On text there is no agreed way to say what the frequency content of the target even is.

Genuinely open. The right time–frequency representation for learning. Spectrograms are inherited from human hearing, learned front ends sometimes beat them and often do not, and nobody can say in advance which.

Not empirical. Whether a mel scale is appropriate for a task is a question about what the task is for, not about the data.

§45 What to carry forward

Five things

Every stride is a resampling, and resampling without filtering destroys information irrecoverably. Figure 1 measures a network's shift-inconsistency five orders of magnitude above the rounding error Part X was content with, growing with every stage.

Convolution is multiplication in frequency. This is the theorem the whole of Part X sits on, it explains why small kernels cannot be frequency-selective and why depth fixes that, and Figure 3 says when to compute it that way.

Phase carries the structure. Figure 4 measures +0.736 to the phase donor against −0.125 to the magnitude donor. Any pipeline that keeps magnitude and throws away phase has thrown away the content and will need a model to invent it back.

Time and frequency resolution trade exactly. Figure 5 measures the product constant while each factor moves sixteen-fold. Every spectrogram is a point on that curve.

Gradient descent is a low-pass filter on the target. Figure 7 measures 50 steps for the slowest component against never for the fastest, ordered by frequency and not by amplitude, which is most of what this book has been calling implicit regularisation.

Sixty seconds

Any signal is a sum of sinusoids, and writing it that way makes three things easy that are hard otherwise. Convolution becomes multiplication, which is why a filter has a one-line description and why the whole convolutional architecture works. Sampling becomes checkable: keep everything below half your sample rate or it comes back disguised as something else, which is a defect sitting inside every strided network in this book. And the order in which a network learns becomes visible: low frequencies first, always, which is why early stopping keeps the smooth explanation and why a coordinate network needs help to draw a fine detail. The one thing to hold on to if you hold on to nothing else: filter before you subsample.
