Part BOptimal control

Tuning gains by hand works and does not scale. Writing down a cost and solving for the controller that minimises it does, and for one important class the solution is closed-form, provably optimal, and computable in a few lines. What it cannot express is a constraint, which is where the rest of the movement goes.

§9 Stating the problem

Dynamics, cost, horizon

Given $x_{t+1} = f(x_t, u_t)$ and a cost $\sum_t \ell(x_t, u_t)$, find the policy minimising the total. Part XVIII wrote exactly this and called it a Markov decision process; the difference is that here $f$ is known and continuous, which turns a learning problem into an optimisation problem.

That is the whole distinction between this movement and reinforcement learning, and it is worth being blunt about: if you know the dynamics, do not learn a policy. Solve for one.

§10 The linear-quadratic case

Where the answer is closed-form

Linear dynamics $x_{t+1} = Ax_t + Bu_t$ and quadratic cost $x^\top Q x + u^\top R u$. The optimal policy is linear, $u = -Kx$, and $K$ comes from iterating

$$P \leftarrow Q + A^\top P (A - BK), \qquad K = (R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A$$

to convergence. Six lines of code, no tuning, and optimal by construction. The Kalman filter of Part XXVIII §13 is this same recursion for estimation rather than control, which is not a coincidence: they are dual problems.

§11 How much tuning leaves on the table

Why this is the argument for optimal control

Hand tuning is fine on a system with two states, which is why it survives: most introductory examples are double integrators and PD works beautifully on them.

It stops working fast. The intuition that got you a good gain on position and velocity gives you nothing on a system with five coupled states, and Figure 3 measures the cliff. That is the case for writing down a cost instead of a gain: not that it is more elegant, but that beyond a handful of states there is nothing to tune your way to.

§12 What LQR assumes

Four assumptions, and what each buys

Linear dynamics. Real systems are not. The standard response is to linearise about the current operating point and re-solve, which works while the excursions are small.

Quadratic cost. Convenient, and it says a large error is exactly four times as bad as one half its size, which is a claim about your preferences that is rarely examined.

Full state observation. Rarely true; combine with an estimator, which is §28.

No constraints. The one that matters most, and §13's subject.

§13 Constraints

Why clipping is not a constraint

Actuators saturate, states have limits, and an optimal controller that does not know this will ask for what it cannot have. Clipping the request is the obvious response and it is not the same thing: the controller keeps planning as though the limit were not there, so it never chooses the differently shaped trajectory that stays inside it.

The distinction matters most for state constraints. An input limit clipped is merely suboptimal; a state limit cannot be clipped at all, because by the time the state is out of bounds the decision that took it there was several steps ago.

§14 Model-predictive control

Optimise over a horizon, execute one step, repeat

At each instant, solve for the best input sequence over the next $H$ steps subject to the constraints, apply only the first, and re-solve at the next instant with the new measurement.

The re-solving is what makes it feedback rather than open-loop planning, and it is why MPC tolerates model error at all. Its costs are honest: an optimisation per control step, a horizon that must be long enough to see the constraint coming, and no stability guarantee unless one is engineered in.

§15 Nonlinear, and trajectory optimisation

Three ways past linearity

Iterative LQR. Linearise about the current trajectory, solve, roll out, repeat. Gets a locally optimal trajectory for a nonlinear system, cheaply.

Direct collocation. Treat the states and inputs at every knot point as decision variables with the dynamics as equality constraints, and hand it to a general solver. Handles constraints properly and scales badly.

Sampling. Perturb a nominal input sequence many times, roll out, and take a weighted average of what worked. Needs no derivatives, parallelises perfectly, and is the reason sampling-based MPC runs on hardware that could not solve a program.

§16 What movement B establishes

Three things

If you know the dynamics, solve rather than learn. The linear-quadratic case is closed-form and provably optimal, and it is six lines.

Hand tuning has a cliff. Figure 3 measures a tuned controller within 0.1% on two states, 78× worse on three, and unable to stabilise five at all.

A constraint is not a clip. Respecting a limit requires planning a different trajectory, which requires a planner that knows the limit exists.
