Part BHeuristics

A heuristic is a guess at how far you still have to go. Everything interesting about informed search follows from two questions about that guess: whether it is ever an overestimate, and whether it changes smoothly from one state to the next. The first buys optimality. The second buys the right to stop thinking about a state once you have settled it, and the two are routinely confused, at a cost this movement measures.

§7 Greedy best-first

Sort by the guess alone

Let $h(n)$ estimate the cost from $n$ to the nearest goal. Greedy best-first search expands the node with the smallest $h$, ignoring the cost already paid.

It is fast and it is wrong. Ignoring $g$ means it will happily follow a long path that ends up close to the goal over a short one that is momentarily further away, so it returns suboptimal solutions, and on a graph with cycles it need not terminate at all. It is worth a section only because it is one of the two endpoints of a scale that A* sits in the middle of.

§8 A*

Cost paid plus cost guessed

$$f(n) = g(n) + h(n)$$

$g(n)$ is the cheapest path cost found to $n$ so far; $h(n)$ is the estimate of what remains. Expand the node of least $f$. That is the entire algorithm.

Setting $h=0$ gives uniform-cost search; letting $h$ dominate gives greedy. A* is the interpolation, and the two properties in the next sections say when the interpolation is safe.

It is worth seeing why $f$ is the natural quantity. Any path through $n$ costs $g(n)$ to reach it and at least $h(n)$ to finish, if $h$ never overestimates. So $f(n)$ is a lower bound on the cost of any solution through $n$. Expanding the smallest such bound first means you are always working on the most promising possibility available, and it means that the moment a goal comes off the frontier, no unexpanded node could lead anywhere cheaper.

§9 Admissibility, and why it gives optimality

Never an overestimate

$h$ is admissible if $h(n)\le h^*(n)$ for every $n$, where $h^*$ is the true cost to the nearest goal. Optimism, in other words: the guess may be too hopeful, never too pessimistic.

The proof is three lines. Suppose A* is about to return a goal $G_2$ with $g(G_2) > C^*$, where $C^*$ is the optimal cost. Some node $n$ on an optimal path is on the frontier. Then $f(n)=g(n)+h(n)\le g(n)+h^*(n)=C^* \lt g(G_2)=f(G_2)$, so $n$ would have been expanded first. The contradiction is the theorem.

What the proof does not say

It says nothing about how much work A* does, and it assumes the frontier always contains a node on an optimal path, which is true for tree search and needs the extra condition in §12 for graph search. Both gaps are where the practical trouble lives, and both are measured below.

§10 What a heuristic actually buys

Two surprises, and one is exact

Half a perfect heuristic buys one per cent of the saving. That looks like a quirk of the domain and is not: it is forced. A* must expand every node with $f \lt C^*$. For a node on any optimal path, $g+\alpha h^* = C^*-(1-\alpha)h^*$, which is strictly below $C^*$ for every $\alpha\lt 1$. So a heuristic that is 90% of perfect still cannot prune the optimal corridor at all. The benefit arrives in the last step, not gradually.

And a perfect heuristic still expands a quarter of the grid. The saving from $\alpha=0$ to $\alpha=1$ is 4.0×, not the orders of magnitude the folklore promises. The reason is ties: on a grid with unit costs there are enormously many equally optimal paths, all with $f=C^*$ exactly, and knowing the distance to the goal does not tell you which to take. Tie-breaking, not heuristic strength, is what is left on the table, which is why real implementations break ties toward larger $g$, and why that one line is worth more than a cleverer $h$.

§11 Giving up optimality on purpose

Weighted A*

$$f(n) = g(n) + w \cdot h(n), \qquad w \ge 1$$

Inflating an admissible heuristic makes it inadmissible, so optimality is gone. What replaces it is a guarantee: the returned path costs at most $w$ times optimal. A bounded loss, chosen in advance, in exchange for less work.

A bound is a description of the worst case, not of behaviour

The gap in Figure 2 is the general lesson, and it is worth internalising because the same mistake is made constantly with generalisation bounds, robustness certificates and privacy budgets. A guarantee of 8× that delivers 1.25× is not a bad guarantee; it is a guarantee doing its job, which is to be true of every instance including the adversarial ones you will never see.

The practical consequence is that $w$ should be chosen by measuring, not by deciding how much suboptimality you can tolerate. If you need paths within 5% of optimal, do not set $w=1.05$; set it to 3 and check.

Anytime search

The natural use of the weight is not to fix it. Anytime weighted A* runs with a large $w$ to get some solution quickly, then decreases the weight and re-searches, reusing what it can, tightening the bound until the deadline. Robot planners and vehicle planners are built this way because they must return something within a fixed cycle time and would rather return a 1.3× path than nothing. The guarantee is what makes the early answer usable: it is not merely a path, it is a path with a known worst case.

§12 Consistency, and the cost of not having it

The triangle inequality for heuristics

$h$ is consistent (or monotone) if for every edge $n\to n'$ with cost $c$,

$$h(n) \le c(n,n') + h(n')$$

Consistency implies admissibility and is strictly stronger. It says $f$ never decreases along a path, so the first time A* expands a state it has already found the cheapest route to it, and closing a state is therefore safe.

The result reverses, and the reversal is the useful part

At 40% coverage and above the inconsistent heuristic is cheaper (0.50×, 0.37×, 0.40× the consistent one's work) because concentrated exact knowledge beats uniformly diluted knowledge once there is enough of it, and the re-expansion penalty collapses from 59% to 1.8%.

So "consistency matters more than tightness" is true only when the heuristic is sparse. That is the regime that matters for learned heuristics, which are exactly the sparse-and-inconsistent kind: accurate where the training data was dense, arbitrary elsewhere, with no triangle inequality anywhere. If you plug a learned $h$ into A* and close states, you have quietly given up optimality; if you allow re-expansion, you have quietly given up the running time. Knowing which of the two you chose is the whole of the engineering.

§13 Where heuristics come from

Four constructions, in increasing order of effort

Relaxation. Delete a constraint from the problem and solve the easier version exactly; its cost is an admissible heuristic for the original, because every real solution is also a solution to the relaxed problem. Manhattan distance is the 8-puzzle with the rule about tiles blocking each other removed. This is the single most reliable source, and it is mechanical.

Pattern databases. Project the state onto a subset of its features, enumerate that smaller space exhaustively backwards from the goal, and store the exact distances in a table. Lookup is $O(1)$ and the values are exact for the projection, hence admissible for the original.

Landmarks. Precompute exact distances from a handful of chosen vertices; the triangle inequality then gives $h(n)=\max_L|d(L,\text{goal})-d(L,n)|$, admissible by construction. This is what makes continental-scale road routing answer in milliseconds.

Learning. Fit a network to predict cost-to-go from states with known distances. Cheap and general, and it forfeits both admissibility and consistency: see §12 for what that costs, and §33 for the version that works anyway.

§14 Dominance, and combining

More is better, if it is still admissible

$h_2$ dominates $h_1$ if $h_2(n)\ge h_1(n)$ everywhere and both are admissible. A dominant heuristic never expands more nodes than the one it dominates, so between admissible heuristics, larger is strictly better.

That gives a free combination: $h(n)=\max\bigl(h_1(n),\dots,h_k(n)\bigr)$ is admissible whenever all the parts are, and dominates all of them. Taking the maximum of several admissible heuristics is almost always worth the extra evaluations.

Adding them is not free. $h_1+h_2$ is usually inadmissible, because the two may be accounting for the same moves. It is admissible when the heuristics are disjoint (when no single action contributes to both), which is exactly the condition that makes additive pattern databases work and the reason they are built with care rather than stacked.

Figure 1 puts an unwelcome ceiling on all of this. If even a perfect heuristic buys 4× on a grid, then heuristic engineering has a limit, and past that limit the way to search a big space faster is not a better guess but a different algorithm. That is the next movement.
