Part BCausal interpretability

Movement A ended on the gap between what a representation contains and what the network uses. Closing it needs an intervention rather than an observation. This movement builds the intervention, uses it to localise a computation exactly, and is then honest about how far the technique currently reaches.

§9 The intervention that makes a claim causal

The move, in one sentence

To show that a component matters, change it and see whether the output changes. Everything in this movement is a variation on that, and the variations differ only in what you change it to, which turns out to be the entire difficulty.

A network is unusually good territory for causal claims. In any observational science the counterfactual is unavailable; here you have the system in a file, you can run it twice, and you can set any internal quantity to any value you like. The obstacle is not access. It is that "change it" underdetermines the experiment. The formal apparatus for that word, intervention as a thing distinct from conditioning and what it licenses, is Part XXII; here the aim stays narrower and empirical.

What to replace an activation with, and what each choice asks

Zero. Asks: what happens when this component is silent? Cheap and the most common. Also the most misleading, because zero is usually far outside the distribution of values that component takes: you are measuring the response to an impossible input.

The mean over the dataset. Asks: what happens when this component carries no input-specific information? Better behaved, since the value is at least typical.

The value from a different input. Asks: does this component carry the information that distinguishes these two inputs? On-distribution by construction, and the most informative of the three. This is patching, and it is §10.

The three answer different questions. Reporting one and describing it as "the importance of the component" is where most confusion in this literature originates.

§10 Activation patching

The procedure

Take a clean input and a corrupted one differing in exactly the thing you care about. Run both, saving the clean activations. Now run the corrupted input again, and at one chosen site splice in the clean activation. Measure how much of the clean answer returns.

Report it as a fraction: 0 means the splice changed nothing, 1 means it fully restored the clean behaviour. Sweeping the site over layers and positions gives a map of where the information that distinguishes the two inputs is doing its work.

Reading the two columns that are zero

$c$ is zero everywhere, which is the control working: it is genuinely irrelevant and the method says so.

$b$ is also zero everywhere, and this one is worth pausing on. $b$ is essential to the computation (the answer depends on it) but it is identical in the clean and corrupted runs, so splicing it in changes nothing. Patching measures where the difference between two inputs is carried, not where the computation happens.

That is the technique's main limitation and its most common misreading. A component can be necessary and show zero patching effect. If you want $b$'s path, you need a corruption that changes $b$, which means your choice of corrupted input determines what the experiment can possibly find.

Try it

Take Figure 3's setup and corrupt $b$ instead. You should see a mirror-image map. Then corrupt both and watch the story become uninterpretable, because with two differences the restoration fractions no longer decompose. Minimal pairs are not a stylistic preference; they are what makes the arithmetic mean anything.

§11 Circuits

Circuit

A circuit is a subgraph of the network (specific heads, specific layers, specific directions) together with a claim about what each part contributes and how they compose, such that the subgraph accounts for a specific behaviour.

The claim is falsifiable in a way that a feature description is not: if the circuit is the mechanism, then ablating everything outside it should leave the behaviour intact, and ablating parts inside it should break it in the predicted way.

Part XII §37's induction heads are the canonical example, and they are worth revisiting with this movement's tools. The claim there was that two heads compose: one attends to the previous occurrence of the current token, the second copies what followed it. That is a circuit, and what makes it more than a story is that each half was verified by intervention rather than by inspection of attention patterns.

What a circuit claim owes you

Sufficiency. Keep only the circuit, ablate the rest, and the behaviour survives.

Necessity. Ablate the circuit, and the behaviour fails.

Faithfulness of the parts. Each component does what the story says: checked by intervening on that component specifically, not by its name.

Generality. It holds across inputs of the same kind, not just the ones the circuit was found on. This is the one most often skipped, and it is Part XIV §20's bias again: a circuit found by searching over many candidates on a small input set will look better on that set than it is.

§12 Ablation and its traps

Pitfall: ablation measures what the rest of the network does without a part, not what the part did

Networks are redundant. Remove a component and other components frequently compensate, so a small drop is consistent with either "unimportant" or "important but backed up". The two are distinguishable (ablate the backup as well and see whether the drop becomes large) but only if you thought to look.

The converse also happens: removing a component can push the network so far off-distribution that everything degrades, which reads as importance and is an artefact of the ablation value. §9's list of what to substitute exists for this reason, and Figure 2's random-direction control (0.003) is the calibration that makes its other numbers meaningful.

The control that should be automatic

Every ablation result needs a random-direction or random-component baseline run under identical conditions. Without it you cannot distinguish a real effect from the network's generic sensitivity to being perturbed. It costs one extra experiment and it is the difference between a measurement and an anecdote.

§13 Steering

If a direction in activation space encodes a property, adding a multiple of it during the forward pass should push the output towards that property. This is the most direct causal test available, and unlike patching it produces something usable.

The construction

Take paired inputs differing in the property (with and without, positive and negative) and let $\mathbf{v} = \bar{\mathbf{h}}_{+} - \bar{\mathbf{h}}_{-}$ be the difference in mean activation at some layer. At inference, run $\mathbf{h} \leftarrow \mathbf{h} + \alpha\mathbf{v}$.

The measurement that makes it a causal claim rather than a curiosity is the sweep over $\alpha$: a real direction produces a graded, monotone change in the property and leaves fluency intact until $\alpha$ is large. A spurious one produces degradation with no property change.

Pitfall: steering works better than it explains

A steering vector can reliably change behaviour without the direction meaning what you called it. It might be entangled with several properties that co-occur in your pairs, or it might work by degrading a competing behaviour rather than by promoting the target one.

The check is the one Part XIV would insist on: measure the properties you did not intend to change. A direction that moves only its target is a much stronger claim than one that moves the target most.

§14 Attribution, and why it disappointed

The oldest interpretability question (which parts of this input caused this output) has the longest list of proposed answers and the least to show for it. The history is worth knowing because the failure is instructive rather than merely embarrassing.

A method family that did not survive its own tests

Gradient saliency, deconvolution, guided backpropagation, layerwise relevance propagation, integrated gradients, and a dozen more, mostly 2013–2018. Each produced heatmaps over the input that looked plausible.

Adebayo et al. (2018) then ran the control nobody had: randomise the network's weights and re-run the method. Several methods produced essentially the same heatmap for a trained network and a random one, which means they were reporting properties of the input and the architecture, not of the learned function. A method that cannot tell a trained model from an untrained one is not explaining the model.

The lesson generalises past saliency. The sanity check that a method distinguishes systems it should distinguish is cheap, is rarely run, and has repeatedly been decisive.

Why plausibility was the trap

A saliency map over an image is evaluated by looking at it, and a human looking at a heatmap over a picture of a dog will find the parts on the dog convincing. That judgement measures agreement with the viewer's own prior, not correctness.

This is why the field moved towards interventions with numerical outcomes. Figure 3's map can be wrong in a way that a heatmap cannot: it makes a prediction about what happens when you splice, and the splice either restores the answer or does not.

Go and read

Adebayo et al. (2018), Sanity checks for saliency maps. Read it for the control, not the survey: randomising the weights and re-running the method is the whole paper, and it is the template for "does this method distinguish systems it should distinguish" that this section generalises past saliency.

§15 Evaluating interpretability itself

The circularity, and the three ways out

To check an explanation of a model you need to know what the model actually computes, which is the thing you lacked. Three escapes are in use, and each has a cost.

Build a system whose ground truth you know. Every figure in this movement and the last. The measurement is then exact, and the cost is that toy systems may not resemble the real ones: §41 states this as the movement's main open question, not a footnote.

Make a prediction and test it. The explanation says the model will fail on a specific novel input; construct it and check. Strong evidence, and the only method here that works at scale on systems you did not build.

Intervene as the explanation prescribes. If the direction means what you say, steering along it should do the specific thing you predicted. Weaker than the above but cheap enough to run routinely.

What is not a check: that the explanation is satisfying, that the visualisation looks clean, or that a language model asked to label a feature produces a fluent description. Those measure legibility.

§16 What movement B establishes

Three things

Interventions answer questions observations cannot. Figure 3 localises a computation to a layer and a position with numbers between 0.00 and 1.00, and nothing correlational could have produced that map.

The experiment's design bounds what it can find. Patching finds where a difference travels, so $b$, necessary to the computation, registers exactly 0.00. The corrupted input you chose decides what the method is capable of seeing.

Every technique here needs a control. The random-direction baseline in Figure 2, the irrelevant input in Figure 3, the randomised-weights check that ended saliency. In each case the control is what separates a measurement from a picture, and in each case it was available all along.
