BuildThe project

Twelve to sixteen hours. You write a gradient booster from the splitting rule upward, then use it to run the two comparisons this book has been citing without performing: trees against networks, and an honest split against a careless one.

P.1 What you are building

rowwise: a histogram-based gradient booster with second-order leaves, early stopping and subsampling; a bagged forest sharing the same tree; and a validation harness that knows about time and groups.

The rule: numpy only, and every comparison repeated. A single split gives one number with no error bar, and a ratio quoted off it is a ratio quoted off noise.

P.2 Stage 1: one tree

  1. Implement exact split search on one feature, refusing thresholds between equal values.
  2. Recurse with a depth limit and a minimum leaf size.
  3. Sweep the depth and plot training and test error, counting leaves.
  4. Build an XOR target and confirm a depth-1 search finds nothing.

Deliverable: the depth curve with the leaf count beside it, and the XOR result. Step four is what greedy growth costs, stated as a measurement rather than a caveat.

P.3 Stage 2: histograms

  1. Bin every feature once at quantiles. Rewrite the split search as a cumulative sum over a bincount.
  2. Check it agrees with the exact search when the binning is fine.
  3. Implement the sibling-subtraction trick and build only the smaller child.
  4. Sweep the bin count over several seeds and plot accuracy.

Deliverable: the agreement check and the bin sweep. Note where accuracy stops improving and whether it then declines, and say which of the two jobs binning is doing on your data.

P.4 Stage 3: averaging

  1. Bag your tree. Plot test error against the number of trees.
  2. Measure the mean pairwise correlation between the trees' predictions.
  3. Sweep the feature fraction, several repetitions each, and plot the ensemble error with error bars alongside the single-tree error and the correlation.
  4. Report the paired difference between your best fraction and using every feature, with its standard error.

Deliverable: the three curves with error bars, and the paired comparison. Whether the subsampling pays on your data is a question about your columns, and step four is how you answer it rather than inheriting a default.

P.5 Stage 4: boosting

  1. Implement the stagewise loop with the negative gradient, first for squared loss.
  2. Add the second-order leaf value and the split gain, with the L2 and per-leaf penalties.
  3. Swap in the logistic loss, changing only the gradient and curvature.
  4. Sweep the learning rate at a fixed budget and plot staged test error. Record which curves converged.
  5. Add early stopping on a validation split.

Deliverable: the staged curves, annotated with which reached their minimum inside the budget. Step three is the payoff of deriving it as a gradient method rather than as a recipe.

P.6 Stage 5: the two comparisons

  1. Write down your predictions first, in a file, before running anything.
  2. Build four generating processes: thresholds, smooth, linear, and a three-way interaction.
  3. Tune both your booster and a small network over the same grid on the same validation split.
  4. Repeat over at least eight paired draws and report means, spreads and paired differences.
  5. Score your predictions against the outcomes.

Deliverable: the comparison with error bars, and the scored predictions. If a comparison does not separate, say so: that is a result, and it is the one a single split would have concealed.

P.7 Stage 6: ordered rows

  1. Build a series with a trend, a season and a random walk. Construct lag features and assert no row uses information at or after its target.
  2. Fit under a random split and under a chronological one. Compare, and compare both against the last-value baseline.
  3. Implement rolling-origin validation with expanding and sliding windows.
  4. Forecast at several horizons, calibrate a band on a held-out slice at each, and measure its coverage.

Deliverable: the two split results with the baseline, and the coverage-against-horizon curve. Step four's number is the one to keep: measure it, and if you quote a nominal level instead, you will be quoting a promise the data does not support.

P.8 Stretch

  • Leaf-wise growth, compared with level-wise at matched leaf counts.
  • Ordered target statistics, with and without the out-of-fold repair.
  • Learned default directions for missing values, against imputation.
  • An ensemble across families, weighted on validation.
  • A reconciled hierarchy at three levels.
  • An audit of a tabular model you actually run.

P.9 Done when

  • Your tree reproduces a planted threshold and refuses to split between equal values.
  • Your histogram search agrees with the exact one at fine binning.
  • You have a depth curve where training error approaches zero and test error turns.
  • You have measured tree correlation and know your ensemble's floor.
  • Your booster works for squared and logistic loss with only the gradient changed.
  • You know which of your learning-rate curves converged inside the budget.
  • You wrote predictions down before the model comparison and scored them afterwards.
  • Every comparison you report has a spread beside it.
  • You have a random-split and a chronological-split number for the same series, and the baseline.
  • You have a measured coverage number for a forecast band, not a nominal one.
  • Your README names one preprocessing step you were fitting outside the fold.
Why this project

Gradient boosting is used more than any other model in this book and understood by fewer of its users, because it arrives as a library with forty hyperparameters. Written from the splitting rule up it has about six ideas in it, and each one is visible in the code.

Stage 5 and Stage 7 are the two the book owes itself. One is a comparison it has cited since Part VI without running; the other is the failure that costs the most in practice and passes every diagnostic in Part XIV on the way through.

The framework, and the prior

Dataset, model family, loss, optimiser; then Part XXI's claim about what the result establishes, Part XXII's estimand, and Part XXIV's interval. This part is about the second choice, and it says something the earlier parts left implicit: the model family is a prior about the shape of the function, and it should be chosen against what you believe that shape is.

Trees represent thresholds exactly and hyperplanes as staircases. Networks are the reverse. Convolution assumes locality; recurrence assumes an order that matters. None of these is a statement about a data format, and reading them as one ("tabular data wants trees") is how a choice that should be about the world becomes a choice about a file extension.
