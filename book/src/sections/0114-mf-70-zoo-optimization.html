§18 Differentiating the functions you will meet

A short list covers nearly everything. Learn these six derivatives and you can differentiate most of what appears in this book.

Two of the six will be unfamiliar, so it is worth saying what they are before differentiating them. The sigmoid $\sigma$ and the hyperbolic tangent $\tanh$ are squashing functions: each takes any real number, however large, and returns one confined to a fixed range: $(0,1)$ for the sigmoid, $(-1,1)$ for tanh. They are steep near zero and flatten out at both ends, so extreme inputs are compressed toward the boundaries and never escape them.

That behaviour is wanted for two different reasons, and the difference matters later. When a model must output a probability, squashing is necessary: a probability above 1 is not a number the rest of the mathematics can accept, so something has to enforce the range, and the sigmoid does. When a squashing function is used inside a network instead, the purpose is different: it is there to break linearity, because without some nonlinear step between layers the whole stack collapses into a single linear map and depth buys nothing at all. Part VII proves that collapse; for now, note only that the same function is doing two unrelated jobs, and that it turns out to be excellent at the first and quietly disastrous at the second.

Why disastrous is the point of everything below. Look at the third column of the table and then at the bottom row of Figure 7: what makes these functions useful (that they flatten out) is the same property that makes their derivatives collapse to nearly zero at both ends. A flat function has no slope, a stage with no slope has no gain, and §17's chain of amplifiers is only as strong as what survives it.

Deriving the sigmoid's derivative

This is the single most instructive chain rule exercise available, so it is worth doing in full.

Worked: $\sigma(x) = (1 + e^{-x})^{-1}$

Write it as a power to avoid the quotient rule: $\sigma(x) = (1+e^{-x})^{-1}$.

Outer: $\square^{-1}$, whose derivative is $-\square^{-2}$. Inner: $1 + e^{-x}$, whose derivative is $-e^{-x}$ (one more chain rule, for the $-x$).

$\sigma'(x) = -(1+e^{-x})^{-2} \cdot (-e^{-x}) = \dfrac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^2}$

Now the elegant part. Split the fraction:

$\dfrac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^2} = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-x}} \cdot \dfrac{e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}}$

The first factor is $\sigma(x)$. The second is $1 - \sigma(x)$, because $1 - \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}} = \frac{e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}}$. Therefore:

$$\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)\big(1 - \sigma(x)\big)$$

Why deep learning cares: two consequences

It is cheap. During the forward pass you already computed $\sigma(x)$. The gradient then costs one multiplication and no new function evaluation. For a network making billions of these calls, that matters.

It is bounded, and small. The product $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ is maximised at $\sigma = 0.5$, where it equals $0.25$, and approaches 0 at both ends. So every sigmoid layer multiplies the gradient by at most 0.25. Stack ten of them and the gradient is scaled by at most $0.25^{10} \approx 10^{-6}$: it has effectively vanished. This is the vanishing gradient problem of §17, now with a specific number attached, and it is why deep sigmoid networks were nearly untrainable before ReLU.

Pitfall: ReLU is not differentiable at zero

$\max(0,x)$ has a corner at $x=0$: the slope is 0 on the left and 1 on the right, so no single tangent exists. Strictly, the derivative is undefined there.

In practice every framework simply defines it as 0 and moves on. This is mathematically improper and entirely harmless: floating point hits exactly zero essentially never, and the optimiser is robust to one arbitrary value at one point. Worth knowing so that you are not confused when a textbook and a codebase disagree.

§19 Critical points and the second derivative

History: finding maxima before calculus existed

Around 1636, some thirty years before Newton and Leibniz, Pierre de Fermat described a method for locating maxima and minima. His idea was to compare the function's value at a point with its value a small distance away, set the two roughly equal, simplify, and then let the small distance go to zero.

That is the derivative, arrived at without the concept of a derivative, and Fermat had no way to justify the final step of discarding the quantity he had just divided by. It is the same soft spot Berkeley would attack a century later.

Descartes disputed the method vigorously and was wrong to. Lagrange later held that Fermat should be considered the true inventor of the calculus, which is a defensible reading: he had the technique, if not the theory.

Training a model means minimising a function. So: how do you find a minimum?

The idea that makes this tractable is almost embarrassingly simple, and it is the reason the derivative was worth all that trouble. Stand at the lowest point of a valley and consider the ground beneath your feet. It is level. It has to be: if it sloped downward in some direction, you could step that way and get lower, and you would not have been at the lowest point after all. So a minimum has a signature you can check for locally, without knowing anything about the rest of the landscape: the slope there is zero.

That is a genuinely powerful trade. Finding the lowest point of a surface sounds like a global search, in which you would have to inspect everywhere. Instead we have converted it into a local test that can be applied at a single point using a single number. Everything about optimisation, in this book and in the field, is built on that conversion.

At the bottom of a valley the ground is level: the tangent is horizontal, the slope is zero. That gives the first condition.

Definition: critical point

A point where $f'(x) = 0$ (or where the derivative does not exist). Every local minimum and maximum is a critical point.

But the converse fails, and that failure is the point of this section. Level ground is necessary for a minimum and nowhere near sufficient: the ground is equally level at the top of a hill, and equally level at a mountain pass where you would descend in one direction and climb in the other. The zero-slope test finds every minimum, and also finds a number of things that are emphatically not minima, and by itself cannot tell you which is which.

The second derivative

Differentiate twice. $f''(x)$ measures how the slope is changing, that is, the curvature.

Taking a derivative of a derivative sounds like an abstraction stacked on an abstraction, so make it concrete first. If $f$ is your position, $f'$ is your speed and $f''$ is your acceleration, the rate at which the speed itself is changing. Everyone has a physical sense of the difference: speed is what the needle reads, acceleration is what you feel pushing you into the seat. Nothing about the second derivative is stranger than that.

Applied to a shape rather than a motion, the same quantity reports which way the curve bends. Walk left to right along a bowl and your slope goes from steeply negative, through zero at the bottom, to steeply positive: the slope is increasing throughout, so $f''$ is positive. Walk along a dome and the reverse happens: the slope decreases from positive to negative, so $f''$ is negative. The sign of the second derivative is the answer to "does this curve hold water or shed it", which is exactly the question you needed the first derivative to be unable to answer.

That is why the test works and why it is not a trick to be memorised. Zero slope says you are at a flat spot. Positive curvature says the flat spot is the bottom of a bowl. Together they say: minimum.

This is the second derivative test, and it is exactly the idea Part I §18 generalises to many dimensions: there, "curving upward in every direction" becomes "positive definite Hessian".

Worked: classify the critical points of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$

$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3$. Setting this to zero: $3x^2 = 3$, so $x = \pm 1$.

$f''(x) = 6x$.

At $x = 1$: $f''(1) = 6 > 0$, so a local minimum.

At $x = -1$: $f''(-1) = -6 < 0$, so a local maximum.

Neither is global: this cubic runs off to $\pm\infty$ in both directions, so it has no global extremum at all.

Pitfall: local is not global

A local minimum is lower than its immediate neighbourhood. A global minimum is the lowest point anywhere. Calculus finds local ones easily and global ones only with extra argument.

This is the fear that hangs over neural network training: gradient descent finds a local minimum, and there may be a far better one elsewhere. The reassuring modern answer, developed in Part IX, is that in very high dimensions the problem is mostly saddle points rather than bad local minima, since for a critical point to be a true minimum, the curvature must be positive in every one of millions of directions, which is vanishingly unlikely.

§20 Convexity

In 60 seconds

A convex function curves upward everywhere, so it has at most one minimum and no bad local ones. That guarantee is why classical machine learning invested so heavily in convex methods. Neural network losses are emphatically not convex; the field traded the guarantee for expressive power, and it turned out to work anyway for reasons still not fully understood.

Definition: convex function

A function is convex if it curves upward everywhere: $f''(x) \geq 0$ for all $x$. Equivalently, the line segment between any two points on the graph never dips below the graph.

The second half of that definition is the one to picture, because it needs no calculus at all. Mark any two points on the graph and stretch a straight string between them. If the string never dips below the curve (if the curve always sags underneath the string, or touches it), the function is convex. Try it on a bowl and the string is always above. Try it on a dome and the string is immediately below. Try it on a curve with two separate valleys and a hill between them, and the string stretched across the two valley floors passes straight through the hill: not convex, and the hill is the proof.

Convexity is the property that makes optimisation easy, for one reason:

Intuition: convex means no bad local minima

A convex function has at most one minimum, and if it has one it is global. There is nowhere to get stuck. Roll a ball anywhere on the surface and it reaches the bottom.

This is why classical machine learning invested so heavily in convex methods: linear and logistic regression, SVMs. You get a guarantee: find a point where the gradient vanishes and you are provably done.

Examples: $x^2$, $e^x$, $|x|$, and $-\ln x$ are all convex. Sums of convex functions are convex, which is why adding an $L^2$ penalty to a convex loss keeps it convex.

It is worth being explicit about what the guarantee is actually worth, because it is larger than it first appears. On a convex function, a point where the gradient vanishes is not merely a candidate: it is the answer, provably, with no further checking. You can stop. You never have to wonder whether restarting from a different initial point would have found something better, because there is nothing better to find. Two different people running two different algorithms on the same convex problem arrive at the same solution, and the result is reproducible in the strong sense that it does not depend on where anyone started.

Now consider what it means that neural networks give this up. There is no certificate of optimality, no reason to believe a second run would not do better, and no way to tell a good solution from a merely adequate one except by measuring. That is not a small concession, and the field made it knowingly, in exchange for models that can represent things convex ones cannot. Keeping the size of that trade in view is what makes the honest parts of the later chapters legible: when Part IX says that in high dimensions the practical obstacle is saddle points rather than bad local minima, it is telling you why the concession turned out to cost less than everyone feared.

Why deep learning cares, and why it gave up on this

Neural network loss surfaces are not convex. Not remotely. They have vast numbers of critical points and no guarantee that gradient descent finds the best one.

The field accepted this trade knowingly: convex models are reliably optimisable but limited in what they can represent, while deep networks are far more expressive and come with no guarantees. It turned out empirically that good-enough solutions are easy to find in practice, for reasons still not fully understood. Part IX discusses this honestly, and knowing what convexity would have bought you is what makes that discussion legible.

Try it

For $f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$: find all critical points and classify each.

Answer. $f'(x) = 4x^3 - 4x = 4x(x^2-1) = 4x(x-1)(x+1)$, which is zero at $x = 0, 1, -1$.

$f''(x) = 12x^2 - 4$. At $x = 0$: $f'' = -4 < 0$, a local maximum. At $x = \pm 1$: $f'' = 8 > 0$, local minima.

Both minima have $f(\pm 1) = 1 - 2 = -1$, so they are tied and both global. Note this function is not convex: a convex function cannot have two separate minima, and the local max at the origin is the proof.
