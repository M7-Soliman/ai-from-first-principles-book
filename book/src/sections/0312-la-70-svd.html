§19 Singular value decomposition

In 60 seconds

Every matrix (any shape, any rank, no conditions) factors as $A = U\Sigma V^\top$: rotate, stretch along axes, rotate again. That is all a linear transformation can ever do. Keeping only the largest few stretch factors gives the provably best low-rank approximation, which is the engine behind PCA, compression, the pseudoinverse and LoRA.

Go and read

Eckart & Young (1936). Two pages establishing that truncating the SVD gives the provably best low-rank approximation: not a good one, the best one. Everything this section claims about compression rests on it. Pair it with Golub & Kahan (1965), the first numerically stable way to compute the thing, which is why a theorem from 1873 became technology in the 1960s.

History: discovered five times, by people who had not read each other

Some results are found once. The singular value decomposition was found over and over, which is the strongest evidence that an idea is natural rather than invented.

Eugenio Beltrami published it in 1873, in an Italian journal, framed as a problem about bilinear forms. Camille Jordan arrived at it independently in 1874, by a different route. Sylvester found it again in 1889 and evidently did not know of either. Autonne extended it to complex matrices in 1915. Eckart and Young proved the optimality theorem in 1936: the result that makes it useful for approximation, and the one this section is really about.

It then waited for computers. A numerically stable algorithm did not exist until Golub and Kahan produced one in 1965, and it is that algorithm, refined, that numpy.linalg.svd calls today. A theorem from 1873 became practical technology ninety years later, and a workhorse of machine learning a century after that.

Eigendecomposition is powerful and fragile. It requires a square matrix, it requires enough independent eigenvectors, and outside the symmetric case it can produce complex numbers and non-perpendicular axes. Real data matrices are rarely square and never guaranteed to cooperate.

The singular value decomposition removes every one of those restrictions. It is the most generally useful factorisation in the chapter, and arguably in applied mathematics.

Definition: the SVD

Every matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ (any shape, any rank, no conditions whatsoever) can be written $$A = U \Sigma V^\top$$ where $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ is orthogonal, $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is orthogonal, and $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is diagonal with non-negative entries $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq 0$ called the singular values.

The word "every" is doing enormous work there. No hypotheses, no exceptions.

Intuition: why this works when eigendecomposition does not

The two decompositions look similar enough that the difference in their power seems mysterious. It comes down to a single concession.

Eigendecomposition insists that a vector come out pointing the way it went in: $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$, same direction, only the length changed. That demand is what forces the restrictions. A matrix that maps $\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^5$ cannot possibly satisfy it: input and output do not even live in the same space, so "same direction" is not a sentence that means anything. And even for square matrices it can be impossible: a rotation of the plane by 30° turns every direction, so it has no real eigenvector at all.

The SVD gives up that demand. It asks for something weaker and achievable: a set of perpendicular directions in the input space that land on a set of perpendicular directions in the output space, with a stretch factor for each pairing. In symbols, $A\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i$, where $\mathbf{u}_i$ is allowed to be a completely different vector from $\mathbf{v}_i$.

Two bases instead of one. That is the entire difference, and it is why the SVD has no hypotheses. There is always a way to line up perpendicular directions on one side with perpendicular directions on the other; there is not always a way to make them the same directions.

What it says geometrically

Read the three factors right to left, as a sequence of actions:

  1. $V^\top$: rotate. Orthogonal, so it turns space without deforming it.
  2. $\Sigma$: stretch along the axes. Diagonal, so it scales each coordinate independently by $\sigma_i$.
  3. $U$: rotate again. Orthogonal, another rigid turn.

Which yields a genuinely surprising claim: every linear transformation, no matter how complicated it looks, is a rotation, then an axis-aligned stretch, then another rotation. There is nothing else a matrix can do.

This also explains the promise made back in Figure 3: that a matrix always maps the unit circle to an ellipse. A circle is unchanged by the first rotation, becomes an axis-aligned ellipse under $\Sigma$, and is merely turned by the second rotation. The singular values are the semi-axis lengths of that ellipse: $\sigma_1$ is the largest stretch the matrix ever applies, and $\sigma_n$ the smallest.

How it relates to eigendecomposition

The two are close relatives. For any $A$, the matrices $A^\top A$ and $AA^\top$ are symmetric and positive semidefinite, so §17 applies to them cleanly. It turns out:

  • $V$ holds the eigenvectors of $A^\top A$: the right singular vectors.
  • $U$ holds the eigenvectors of $AA^\top$: the left singular vectors.
  • The singular values are the square roots of the shared non-zero eigenvalues: $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$.

So the SVD is what you get by pushing eigendecomposition through a symmetrisation trick. That is also how you can reason about it when the geometry gets slippery, and it is why the SVD inherits the good behaviour of the symmetric case rather than the pathologies of the general one.

Watch the shapes

For $A$ that is $4 \times 3$: $U$ is $4 \times 4$, $\Sigma$ is $4 \times 3$, and $V^\top$ is $3 \times 3$. Chaining: $(4\times4)(4\times3)(3\times3) \to 4 \times 3$. ✓. Note $\Sigma$ is rectangular here; "diagonal" for a non-square matrix means non-zero only where the row and column index coincide, with the leftover rows or columns entirely zero. In practice you usually want the thin SVD, which drops those structural zeros: np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False).

Why it matters: low-rank approximation

Here is the property that makes the SVD indispensable. Expand the product as a sum of simple pieces:

$$A = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i \, \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^\top$$

Each term is an outer product (a full matrix of rank 1, from §6), scaled by its singular value. Since the $\sigma_i$ are sorted in decreasing order, the first terms carry the most of the matrix. Keep the top $k$ and discard the rest:

Before the formula, it is worth being clear about what that sum is doing, because "expand the product as a sum of rank-1 pieces" is a sentence that can pass by without depositing anything.

Think of it as decomposing a signal into ingredients ordered by importance. The first term is the single best rank-1 description of the whole matrix: the one pattern that, if you were allowed to keep only one, explains the most. The second term is the best correction to what the first missed. The third corrects what the first two missed, and so on down. Every term is perpendicular to the ones before it, so none of them repeats work already done, and the singular value in front says how much that particular ingredient contributes.

Because they arrive in order of importance, you can stop early and know exactly what you gave up. This is the same bargain as compressing an image: keep the broad shapes and discard the fine texture, and the result is smaller and still recognisable. The difference is that here the ordering is not a heuristic someone chose. It is provably the right one, which is what Eckart–Young says.

$$A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i \, \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^\top$$

The Eckart–Young theorem then says something remarkably strong: $A_k$ is the best possible rank-$k$ approximation to $A$, measured in Frobenius norm. Not a good heuristic: provably optimal. No cleverer rank-$k$ matrix exists.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
A = rng.normal(size=(80, 60))
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)

# rebuild using only the top k components
for k in [1, 5, 20, 60]:
    Ak = U[:, :k] @ np.diag(s[:k]) @ Vt[:k, :]
    err = np.linalg.norm(A - Ak) / np.linalg.norm(A)
    stored = k * (80 + 60 + 1)
    print(f"k={k:3d}   relative error {err:.3f}   numbers stored {stored:6d} / 4800")

### k=  1   relative error 0.972   numbers stored    141 / 4800
### k=  5   relative error 0.869   numbers stored    705 / 4800
### k= 20   relative error 0.553   numbers stored   2820 / 4800
### k= 60   relative error 0.000   numbers stored   8460 / 4800

This particular matrix is pure noise, so its singular values decay slowly and compression buys little, which is itself the lesson. Low-rank approximation works precisely to the extent that your data has structure. Run the same code on a photograph or a real dataset and the error at $k=20$ collapses, because real data is enormously redundant. Noise is incompressible; structure is not.

In the framework: the decompositions, and why they are worth calling

Everything in Part F is one call, in every library, and the names are close to identical because they all wrap the same LAPACK routines underneath:

import torch

U, s, Vh = torch.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)  # the thin SVD -- s is DESCENDING
w, V     = torch.linalg.eigh(S)        # symmetric only -- w is ASCENDING
torch.linalg.pinv(A)                   # the pseudoinverse of section 20
torch.linalg.lstsq(A, b).solution      # least squares, done properly
torch.linalg.cond(A)                   # = s[0] / s[-1], no separate computation
torch.linalg.matrix_rank(A)            # counts singular values above a tolerance

Note the two orderings, and note that they disagree. The SVD returns singular values largest first; eigh returns eigenvalues smallest first. Slicing [:k] therefore takes the most important components from one and the least important from the other, which is exactly the silent PCA bug in §23's pitfall list, and the reason that section recommends the SVD route.

Truncation is slicing, which is the whole point

k = 20
A_k = U[:, :k] @ torch.diag(s[:k]) @ Vh[:k, :]   # best rank-20 approximation

Because Eckart–Young guarantees the top $k$ terms are optimal, and because the library hands them to you already sorted, the best rank-$k$ approximation to any matrix is three array slices. No search, no optimisation, no training loop. That is an unusually good deal and it is why the SVD sits underneath so much: PCA, model compression, LoRA, the pseudoinverse and the condition number are all this operation with different framing.

Why you should reach for lstsq and not the formula

Given $A$ and $\mathbf{b}$ it is tempting to write the normal equations from Part I's drill S3: torch.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ b). It is one line, it is algebraically correct, and Part III §20 measures how badly it fails: forming $A^\top A$ squares the condition number and therefore doubles the digits you lose. lstsq never forms that product. Same answer in exact arithmetic, several orders of magnitude better in floating point.

The general habit is worth stating once, because it recurs: when a library provides a named function for something you could assemble from primitives, the name usually encodes a numerical decision. lstsq over normal equations, logsumexp over log-of-sum-of-exp, cross_entropy over log-of-softmax. In each case the assembled version is the formula and the provided version is the algorithm, and Part III is about the gap between them.

TensorFlow mirrors all of this under tf.linalg: tf.linalg.svd, tf.linalg.eigh, tf.linalg.lstsq, with one caveat worth knowing: its svd returns $V$ rather than $V^\top$, so a formula ported directly between the two libraries will be silently transposed.

Why deep learning cares

The SVD is the machinery behind an unusual number of things you will meet: PCA is the SVD of centred data, as §23 derives from scratch. The pseudoinverse in §20 is defined through it. Model compression replaces a big weight matrix with a low-rank factorisation to shrink a network. LoRA fine-tunes enormous language models by constraining the update to be low-rank, training a few million parameters instead of a few billion. And the ratio $\sigma_1 / \sigma_n$ is exactly the condition number from §13; the SVD is where that quantity comes from.

§20 The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse

Section 12 left us with a genuine problem. When $A$ is not square (the normal case in machine learning) there is no inverse, so $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$ is not available. We still want the best available answer.

Definition: pseudoinverse

The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of $A$, written $A^+$, is defined via the SVD: $$A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^\top$$ where $\Sigma^+$ is formed by taking the reciprocal of each non-zero entry of $\Sigma$ and then transposing.

Notice the construction is exactly "undo the SVD": reverse the rotations by transposing them, and invert the stretches by reciprocating them. Where a stretch was zero (a direction the matrix annihilated) there is nothing to undo, so we leave a zero and accept the loss. That single choice is what makes the pseudoinverse well-defined for every matrix.

When $A$ has full column rank, $A^+$ also has the closed forms $(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$ (overdetermined, $m > n$) or $A^\top(AA^\top)^{-1}$ (underdetermined, $m < n$), which is where the normal equations of drill S3 come from. They agree with the SVD definition above whenever both are defined; the SVD route is the one that still works when $A$ does not have full rank, so it is the definition this section leads with.

What $\mathbf{x} = A^+\mathbf{b}$ gives you depends on which case from §12 you are in, and it does the sensible thing in each:

CaseWhat $A^+\mathbf{b}$ returns
Overdetermined
no exact solution		 The $\mathbf{x}$ minimising $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2$: the least squares solution, the closest miss available.
Underdetermined
infinitely many solutions		 Among all exact solutions, the one with smallest $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2$: the minimum norm solution.
Square, invertible Exactly $A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$. The pseudoinverse is a strict generalisation of the inverse.
Intuition: "closest" means perpendicular

Why must the best approximation have a perpendicular residual? Suppose it did not. Then the error would have some component lying within the plane, and you could slide your answer along the plane to cancel that component, getting closer. Only when nothing remains to cancel, meaning the residual is entirely perpendicular, is improvement impossible. That argument is the whole of least squares, and it is why fitting a model is a projection.

Why deep learning cares

Linear regression is the pseudoinverse. Given a data matrix $X$ and targets $\mathbf{y}$, the fitted weights are $\mathbf{w} = X^+\mathbf{y}$; this is not an analogy, it is the same computation. Part VI §12 develops exactly this and builds outward. The underdetermined row of the table is quietly important too: modern overparameterized networks have infinitely many perfect fits available, and the minimum-norm preference is a first model of why the one gradient descent finds tends to generalise rather than merely memorise.

import numpy as np

# fit a line through noisy points: 10 equations, 2 unknowns -> overdetermined
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = np.linspace(0, 10, 10)
y = 2.5 * x + 1.0 + rng.normal(0, 1.5, size=10)   # true slope 2.5, intercept 1

A = np.column_stack([x, np.ones_like(x)])          # shape (10, 2)
w = np.linalg.pinv(A) @ y                          # least squares via pseudoinverse
print(f"slope {w[0]:.3f}, intercept {w[1]:.3f}")
### slope 2.495, intercept 1.361   -- recovered despite the noise

# in production, prefer lstsq: same answer, better conditioned
w2, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)
print(np.allclose(w, w2))     ### True

§21 Trace

A short section: the trace is simple, and its value is in the identities it enables.

$$\tr(A) = \sum_i A_{i,i}$$

Just the sum of the diagonal. Three facts justify its existence:

It rewrites the Frobenius norm. From §14:

$$\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\tr(A^\top A)}$$

which converts a double sum over every entry into a single trace, and lets norm manipulations happen inside the matrix algebra rather than outside it.

It is invariant under cyclic permutation. Provided the shapes work out:

$$\tr(ABC) = \tr(BCA) = \tr(CAB)$$

You may rotate factors around the cycle, though not reorder them arbitrarily. This is a workhorse identity in derivations, because it lets you move an awkward factor to where it is convenient. Strikingly, $\tr(AB) = \tr(BA)$ holds even when $AB$ and $BA$ are different sizes, as long as both products are defined.

It equals the sum of the eigenvalues.

$$\tr(A) = \sum_i \lambda_i$$

Which is a genuinely useful shortcut: you can total the eigenvalues by adding diagonal entries, without computing a single eigenvector. In §23 this gives us total variance for free.

§22 Determinant

History: determinants came first, and got demoted

Determinants predate matrices by roughly a hundred and seventy years, which explains why §2's naming story runs the way it does.

In 1683 the Japanese mathematician Seki Takakazu wrote down determinants for systems of equations. In the same decade (and with no possible contact, Japan being closed to most foreign exchange) Leibniz described them in correspondence, using them to decide when a system has a solution. Two independent discoveries, half a world apart, within a few years.

For the next two centuries determinants were the prestigious object. Enormous effort went into their theory. Sylvester named the matrix, as §2 recounts, purely as the thing determinants were born from.

Then the hierarchy inverted. Matrices turned out to be the richer structure, and determinants turned out to be numerically treacherous at any real scale: they overflow, they underflow, and they answer a question that rank and the singular values answer more robustly. Today the determinant is a superb tool for thinking and a poor one for computing, which is precisely the pitfall this section closes with.

The determinant, written $\det(A)$ or $|A|$, is a single number summarising a square matrix. The formula is awkward; the meaning is not.

Intuition: the determinant is a volume scale factor

Feed the unit square (or cube, or its $n$-dimensional analogue) through $A$ and it comes out a parallelogram. $|\det(A)|$ is how much the area or volume changed. A determinant of 3 triples volumes; a determinant of $0.5$ halves them.

The sign carries extra information: a negative determinant means the transformation flipped orientation, turning the space over, like a mirror.

The right-hand panel is the important one, and it connects everything in Part C:

The determinant test

$\det(A) = 0$ $\iff$ $A$ is singular $\iff$ $A$ has a zero eigenvalue $\iff$ $A$ is rank-deficient $\iff$ $A$ is not invertible $\iff$ $A$ collapses space onto a lower-dimensional set.

Six statements, one condition. Every one of them says information was destroyed.

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix the formula is worth memorising, and it is the only one you will compute by hand with any regularity:

$$\det\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = ad - bc$$

Two further identities that connect the determinant to the rest of the chapter:

$$\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B), \qquad \det(A) = \prod_i \lambda_i$$

The first is exactly what the volume reading predicts: apply two transformations in sequence and the volume scalings multiply. The second pairs with §21 to give a tidy mnemonic: the trace adds the eigenvalues, the determinant multiplies them. And the product form makes the determinant test obvious, since a product is zero exactly when one of its factors is.

Pitfall: determinants are numerically unreliable

Do not test invertibility in code by checking det(A) == 0. Floating point arithmetic essentially never produces exactly zero, and determinants of large matrices overflow or underflow readily; the determinant of a $1000\times1000$ matrix with modest entries can easily exceed what a float can represent. Use np.linalg.matrix_rank, or inspect the singular values from the SVD, or check np.linalg.cond. The determinant is a superb conceptual tool and a poor numerical one, and knowing the difference is part of maturing as a practitioner.

Try it

Compute $\det\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. What does the answer tell you about invertibility, and can you see it directly in the columns?

Answer. $\det = 4(1) - 2(2) = 0$. So the matrix is singular and has no inverse. Looking at the columns, $(4,2)$ and $(2,1)$: the first is exactly twice the second. They are linearly dependent, the rank is 1, and the whole plane gets flattened onto the single line through $(2,1)$. Every one of the six equivalent statements in the determinant test is visible in this one small example.
