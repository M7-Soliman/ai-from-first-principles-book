§29 A run that will not train: the ladder

Everything in this part assembles into one procedure. The ordering is by information gained per unit of effort, and the first six rungs together cost less than a single training run.

The rule that governs the whole thing: measure before you change anything. Almost every hour lost to a failing training run is lost to sweeping hyperparameters that were never the problem, and the reason people sweep is that sweeping feels like progress while measuring feels like delay. It is the other way round.

The diagnostic ladder

Why the ladder is in this order and not the usual one

The conventional order is roughly the reverse: try Adam, try a lower learning rate, try a bigger model, then start reading. Three things make that expensive.

Rungs 0 to 5 are nearly free and each eliminates a whole family of causes. Rung 4 alone rules out every critical-point explanation, which is where most of the folklore lives. Nothing above rung 6 eliminates anything: a failed run with a different optimiser tells you that optimiser did not work, and nothing else.

The cheap rungs catch the common faults. Bad initialisation scale, dead units, an untuned output bias and accumulated regularisation are between them a large share of real failures, and none is an optimiser problem.

And the expensive rungs are ordered by §12. If the run is failing because the local direction does not lead anywhere good, then rungs 8 and 9 (changing the model, changing where you start) are the only interventions that address it, and rung 10 provably does not.

§30 The theoretical limits, and what they are worth

There are results showing that no optimisation algorithm can do well on neural networks in general. They are correct and they matter less than they sound, and it is worth knowing why, because dismissing them for the wrong reason is as bad as being intimidated by them.

Three reasons a hardness result may not apply to you

The model may not be the one analysed. Several results apply only to networks whose units output discrete values. Almost every unit in use outputs a smoothly increasing value, which is what makes local search feasible at all, and, per §27, is a design choice made partly for that reason.

You may not be in the hard class. Some results establish that certain problem classes are intractable. They do not give you a way to decide whether the problem in front of you is in one, and typically it is very difficult to tell.

You are allowed to change the question. Other results show that finding a solution for a network of a given size is intractable. In practice you use a larger network, for which many more parameter settings are acceptable, which is a different problem, and empirically a much easier one.

Underneath all three is a mismatch that this part opened with. These results concern finding the minimum of a function. You do not want the minimum of a function. §1: you want a parameter setting whose generalisation error is low enough, which by §4 you will stop short of any minimum to obtain, and which by §3 you may reach by optimising something else entirely.

Analysing whether an algorithm can accomplish that is extremely difficult, and realistic bounds on it remain an open goal. So the honest position is not that the hardness results are wrong or irrelevant; it is that they answer a question adjacent to the one you are asking, and the adjacent question is the tractable one.

History: a field that proceeded without permission

It is worth sitting with how often this pattern has now appeared.

Adam's convergence proof was wrong and Adam remained the default (Part III §16). Batch normalisation's stated explanation was disputed and the method kept working (§23). The classical bias–variance account fails to describe modern networks and the networks work anyway (Part V §20). Local minima were believed for thirty years to be the central obstacle and appear not to be (§7). Hardness results said this could not be done and it was done.

Two readings are available and both are correct. One is that this field's practice runs ahead of its theory, and that empirical evidence has repeatedly been the stronger signal. The other is that a great deal of confident explanation in this subject has turned out to be wrong while the underlying methods were fine, so the thing to hold loosely is the explanation, not the measurement.

Which is the argument for the parts of this book that are derived rather than reported. §6's step-size ceiling, §20's conjugacy, §22's expansion of the product, §16's variance bookkeeping: these are theorems. They will be true when the current explanations of batch normalisation have been replaced twice more.

§31 What optimisation cannot do

Three limits, each a place where more or better optimisation is the wrong response.

It cannot close the generalisation gap
Every method here reduces training loss. Part VI §4's gap between training and test error is untouched by all of them, and §13's Cramér–Rao argument goes further: an optimiser that converges faster than $O(1/k)$ is spending the extra speed on overfitting, because generalisation is not permitted to improve that fast. When the gap is your problem, this part has nothing for you and Part VIII has everything.
It cannot rescue a model that is in the wrong place
§12: local descent follows local information. If the region you started in is not connected to a good solution by a path local descent can follow, no estimate of the local direction (however exact, however well preconditioned) will get you there. The remedies are a different starting point or a different model, and this is the difficulty the field has made least progress on.
It cannot tell you when to stop
§4: training halts on a criterion the optimiser cannot evaluate. An optimiser knows when its gradient is small and by §12 that moment may never arrive, may arrive at the wrong place, or may not exist. The stopping signal comes from held-out data, which means the quality of your validation set bounds the quality of every decision in this part.
The one thing to carry forward

The optimiser is not solving your problem, and that is the arrangement rather than a defect.

It minimises a surrogate, on a sample, from a starting point you chose, until a criterion it cannot see tells it to stop. Every difficulty in this part is a consequence of that arrangement, and so is every technique: initialisation, pretraining and curricula choose the starting point; normalisation and architecture change the surrogate's shape; clipping and schedules bound the step; early stopping supplies the halt.

Which is why "which optimiser should I use" is the wrong first question and stays wrong for a long time. §18's honest answer (no consensus, pick one and learn it properly) is not a gap in the literature. It is a signal that the choice matters less than the four things around it.
