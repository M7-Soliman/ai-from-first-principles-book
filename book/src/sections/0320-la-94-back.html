Pitfall: the 1-D versus column-vector trap

NumPy distinguishes shape (n,) from (n, 1), and mathematics does not. A (n,) array is neither a row nor a column; it adapts to context, which is convenient right up until it silently broadcasts into something unintended. If x.shape is (3,) and y.shape is (3, 1), then x + y is a (3, 3) matrix rather than the vector you expected. When a result has more axes than you planned, this is the first thing to check. Use x.reshape(-1, 1) to be explicit when you need a genuine column.

C Error messages, decoded

The four errors you will actually hit, and what each is really telling you.

ValueError: matmul: Input operand 1 has a mismatch in its core dimension 0

The inner dimensions do not agree. Print both shapes and check the chaining rule from §5: $(m \times \underline{n})(\underline{n} \times p)$. Nine times out of ten a transpose is missing, or two operands are in the wrong order.

ValueError: operands could not be broadcast together with shapes (3,4) (5,4)

An elementwise operation on incompatible shapes. Broadcasting aligns axes from the right and requires each pair to be equal or to contain a 1. Here $3$ against $5$ in the leading axis satisfies neither.

LinAlgError: Singular matrix

You asked to invert or solve with a rank-deficient matrix: §12's information-loss case. Do not reach for a workaround immediately; ask why it is singular. Duplicate features? A constant column? More unknowns than equations? The fix is usually lstsq, but the diagnosis matters more than the fix.

No error at all, and the numbers are wrong

The dangerous one. Ranked by how often it is the culprit:

  1. * used where @ was meant, silent whenever shapes happen to be compatible.
  2. Broadcasting that produced an extra axis you did not notice.
  3. eigh output used without sorting descending.
  4. Data not centred before PCA.
  5. A missing transpose, in code where the shapes happen to work both ways.

The defence is the habit from §0.4: assert your shapes. A single assert H.shape == (batch, hidden) in the right place catches most of these instantly, and costs nothing.

E If you want more

Three genuinely different resources, listed in the order most people benefit from them:

3Blue1Brown: Essence of Linear Algebra
A short video series built entirely on the function view of §4. If the geometric picture is the part not clicking, watch this first. Free on YouTube.
Gilbert Strang: Introduction to Linear Algebra, and MIT 18.06
The standard full course, lectures free on MIT OpenCourseWare. Strang emphasises the column view more than most texts, which aligns well with how this part is organised.
Trefethen & Bau: Numerical Linear Algebra
For when §13 and §19 leave you wanting the real story about conditioning and stability. Harder, and worth it if you end up doing numerical work.
