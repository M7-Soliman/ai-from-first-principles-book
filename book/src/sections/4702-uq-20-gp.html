Part BDistributions over functions

A Gaussian process is the case where all of this is exact: a posterior over functions in closed form, with intervals that widen where the data stops and a marginal likelihood that selects the model without a validation set. It does not scale, and it is the standard everything in movement C is approximating.

§8 A prior over functions

The object

A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions such that any finite set of function values is jointly Gaussian. It is specified by a mean function, usually zero, and a covariance function $k(x, x')$ saying how correlated the values at two inputs are.

The shift in viewpoint is the whole idea. Parts VI to XX put a distribution over parameters and derived predictions from it. Here the distribution is over functions directly, and no parameters appear.

What the kernel encodes

The kernel is the assumption. A squared-exponential kernel says nearby inputs have similar outputs and the function is very smooth. A Matérn kernel says the same with a controlled amount of roughness. A periodic kernel says the function repeats.

The length-scale says how far "nearby" reaches, and it is the single most consequential number: too short and the posterior reverts to the prior between every pair of points, too long and the model cannot represent the variation it needs to.

Part VI §15's kernels appeared as a computational trick for making a linear method non-linear. This is the same object read as a statement about which functions are plausible, and it is the more useful reading.

§9 The posterior, in closed form

Go and read

Rasmussen & Williams (2006), Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning. Free online, and chapter 2 derives exactly the posterior below, worked through with more patience than any second-hand treatment manages, including the numerically stable Cholesky route this section only asserts. Chapter 5 is where §11's marginal likelihood comes from: read it for the log-determinant term earned as a complexity penalty rather than stated as one.

Conditioning a joint Gaussian

With observations $\mathbf{y}$ at inputs $X$ and noise $\sigma^2$, write $K = k(X,X)$ for the training covariances, $K_* = k(X_*,X)$ for the covariance between the new points and the training points, and $K_{**} = k(X_*,X_*)$ for the covariance among the new points themselves. The posterior at $X_*$ is then Gaussian with

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_* = K_{*}\left(K + \sigma^2 I\right)^{-1}\mathbf{y}, \qquad \Sigma_* = K_{**} - K_{*}\left(K + \sigma^2 I\right)^{-1}K_{*}^{\top}$$

Two things are worth noticing. The variance $\Sigma_*$ does not depend on $\mathbf{y}$ at all: only on where you observed, not what you saw. And the whole thing is Part I's linear algebra: a Cholesky factorisation and two triangular solves, which is also why the cost is cubic in the number of observations.

§10 What the intervals do

The property no method in movement C reproduces cheaply

Growing uncertainty away from the data is what people want from uncertainty quantification and what most cheap approximations fail to deliver. Here it falls out of the algebra: $\Sigma_*$ reverts to the prior covariance $K_{**}$ as the correlation with observed points goes to zero.

Note also the over-coverage. The measured 0.979 against a nominal 0.95 is not a flaw: it is the model being honest about an assumption it holds and the truth does not exactly satisfy. A method that came in at 0.93 would be worse in a way that matters more.

§11 Choosing the kernel by marginal likelihood

Model selection without a validation set

The marginal likelihood integrates over all functions the prior allows:

$$\log p(\mathbf{y} \mid X, \theta) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^{\top}K_\theta^{-1}\mathbf{y} - \tfrac{1}{2}\log|K_\theta| - \tfrac{n}{2}\log 2\pi$$

Maximise it over the kernel's hyperparameters. The first term rewards fitting the data; the second, the log determinant, penalises a kernel that could have explained many datasets. It is Occam's razor arriving as a determinant rather than as a preference.

This is genuinely different from Part VI §10's cross-validation: no data is held out, and the complexity penalty is derived rather than tuned. It is also less robust, because it trusts the model class entirely: if the kernel is wrong, the marginal likelihood optimises within a wrong family and reports no distress.

§12 The cost, and what is done about it

Cubic, and the standard escapes

Inverting an $n \times n$ matrix is $O(n^3)$ time and $O(n^2)$ memory. At $n = 10^4$ that is uncomfortable; at $10^6$ it is out of the question.

Inducing points. Summarise the data by $m \ll n$ pseudo-inputs whose locations are learned, giving $O(nm^2)$. The variational form makes this a proper approximation with a bound rather than a heuristic.

Structure. On a grid, or with a separable kernel, the algebra factorises.

Random features. Approximate the kernel by an explicit finite feature map, turning the process back into Bayesian linear regression, which is Part XXIII §25's random-features model, arriving from the other direction.

§13 Bayesian optimisation

Using uncertainty to decide where to look

To optimise an expensive function, fit a Gaussian process to what you have and choose the next point by an acquisition function trading the posterior mean against its variance: expected improvement, upper confidence bound, or the probability of improvement.

This is the formal version of Part XIV §18's model-based hyperparameter search, and it is the clearest case in the book of an uncertainty estimate being used rather than reported: the variance is not decoration, it is the term that decides where to sample.

Pitfall: it is only as good as the kernel

Bayesian optimisation with a badly chosen length-scale explores nearly at random or fixates on one region. Its reputation for sample efficiency is earned in low dimension with a well-matched kernel, and Part XIV §16's finding that random search is a strong baseline is not a contradiction: it is what happens when those conditions do not hold.

§14 Where this belongs

When a Gaussian process is the right instrument

Few observations, expensive to obtain. Experimental design, hyperparameter search, materials and drug screening. Hundreds of points, not millions.

Smoothness is a real assumption, not a convenience.

The uncertainty is load-bearing: you will act on it, as in §13.

And when it is not: high-dimensional structured inputs, large $n$, or problems where the function is genuinely discontinuous. The failure there is not slowness but a kernel that cannot represent the truth, at which point the intervals are confidently wrong.

§15 What movement B establishes

Three things

Exact Bayesian uncertainty over functions is available, in closed form, and it does the thing people want: Figure 2's posterior sd is 0.181 where there is data and 0.862 where there is none.

The posterior variance does not depend on the observed values, only on where you observed. Uncertainty is a statement about coverage of the input space, which is why it grows away from the data and why no amount of agreement among your labels reduces it there.

The cost is cubic and the assumption is the kernel. Everything in movement C trades exactness for scale, and the question to ask of each is which of Figure 2's properties survives.
