PracticeDrills

Thirty-one problems. A large share are measurements, because this part's central claims (that noise is a penalty, that early stopping tracks weight decay, that averaging cuts variance by $1/k$) are all checkable in a few lines, and checking them is far more convincing than the derivations.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · What weight decay actually does

Weight decay of 10−4 at a learning rate of 0.1, with a weight receiving no gradient at all. Write down how many steps it takes for that weight to halve.

Answer. About 69,000: ln2 / (lr × λ). Which is worth internalising: weight decay is a very slow force, so in a run of a few thousand steps it is barely acting, and in a run of a million it dominates any weight the data does not defend. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

WarmW1 · The definition

A change reduces both training and test error. Is it regularization?

Answer. No. Regularization reduces generalisation error but not training error; the trade is the definition. A change that improves both is simply a better model.
WarmW2 · Which fraction survives

Ridge with $\alpha = 5$. A direction has curvature $\lambda = 500$; another has $\lambda = 0.05$. What fraction of each is kept?

Answer. $500/505 = 0.990$ and $0.05/5.05 = 0.0099$. The sharp direction is untouched; the flat one is erased. $L^2$ deletes what the data did not determine.
WarmW3 · Why $L^1$ reaches zero

In one sentence, why does $L^1$ produce exact zeros and $L^2$ not?

Answer. $L^2$'s gradient contribution $\alpha w$ vanishes as $w \to 0$, so the push weakens exactly when it would need to finish; $L^1$'s is the constant $\alpha\,\text{sign}(w)$, which does not.
WarmW4 · Noise as penalty

You add input noise $\sigma = 0.2$ on $n = 500$ examples. What $L^2$ penalty is that equivalent to?

Answer. $\alpha = n\sigma^2 = 500 \times 0.04 = 20$. Substantial, and easy to apply without realising you have regularized at all.
WarmW5 · Early stopping strength

You stop after $\tau = 200$ steps with $\epsilon = 0.01$. Roughly what $\alpha$?

Answer. $\alpha \approx 1/(\tau\epsilon) = 1/2 = 0.5$. Train longer and the effective penalty falls.
WarmW6 · A flip too far

You augment MNIST with horizontal flips. What goes wrong?

Answer. A flipped 2 is not a 2. The transformation does not preserve the label, so you are teaching an invariance the task does not have. The model trains fine and is quietly worse.
WarmW7 · The rescaling

Dropout keeps a unit with probability $1-p$. Why divide the survivors by $1-p$?

Answer. So the expected input to the next layer is the same in training and at test time. With inverted dropout, evaluation needs no adjustment: it simply switches dropout off.
WarmW8 · When bagging does nothing

You average 50 copies of an underfitting model. What happens?

Answer. Nothing, at fifty times the cost. Every member is wrong in the same direction, so the errors are perfectly correlated and the ensemble equals a single member. Bagging removes variance and bias is what you had.
WarmW9 · Sharing versus penalising

How is parameter sharing a stronger statement than a tying penalty?

Answer. A penalty says two weights should be similar; sharing says they are one weight. Sharing removes parameters outright, which is why a convolutional filter has nine parameters where a dense layer would have millions.
WarmW10 · Diagnose first

Training error 24%, test error 25%. Would you add dropout?

Answer. No. The gap is one point; this is bias, not variance, and every technique in this part treats variance. Adding dropout makes it worse. Add capacity or better features.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Derive the shrinkage

From $\tilde{\mathbf{w}} = (H+\alpha I)^{-1}H\mathbf{w}^*$, show that in the eigenbasis each coordinate is scaled by $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\alpha)$.

Answer. $H = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with $Q$ orthogonal, so $H + \alpha I = Q(\Lambda + \alpha I)Q^\top$ and its inverse is $Q(\Lambda+\alpha I)^{-1}Q^\top$. The product telescopes to $Q(\Lambda+\alpha I)^{-1}\Lambda Q^\top$, and both middle factors are diagonal, so in coordinates $\mathbf{y} = Q^\top\mathbf{w}$ the system decouples entirely into $\tilde{y}_i = \lambda_i y_i^*/(\lambda_i+\alpha)$.
CoreC2 · Measure it

Fit ridge at several $\alpha$ on an ill-conditioned design. Divide each solution by the unregularized one in the eigenbasis and plot against $\lambda$. Does it land on the predicted curve?

Answer. It does: that is Figure 1's left panel, where the points are measured and the curve is predicted. If yours does not, suspect that you compared in the wrong basis: the decoupling only happens after rotating by $Q^\top$.
CoreC3 · The U in $\alpha$

Sweep $\alpha$ over eight orders of magnitude on a problem with $n$ barely exceeding $d$. How much does the best $\alpha$ beat $\alpha \to 0$?

Answer. On the setup behind Figure 1, by a factor of 6.1. The optimum is strictly interior, which is the whole claim: neither extreme is right.
CoreC4 · Lasso by proximal gradient

Implement $L^1$ by taking a gradient step then soft-thresholding at $\epsilon\alpha$. Confirm coefficients reach exactly zero, not merely small values.

Answer. Soft-thresholding is $\text{sign}(w)\max(|w|-\epsilon\alpha, 0)$, and the $\max$ is what produces exact zeros: a plain gradient step on $|w|$ oscillates around zero instead. Figure 2 measured 5 of 8 coefficients at exactly zero.
CoreC5 · Paths

Plot coefficient paths for $L^1$ and $L^2$ against $\alpha$ on data where most features are noise. What is qualitatively different?

Answer. $L^2$'s paths approach zero asymptotically and never arrive; $L^1$'s hit zero at a finite $\alpha$ and stay. That is feature selection, and it is why $L^1$ is used when you want to know which inputs matter.
CoreC6 · Penalty equals constraint

For a given $\alpha$, find the $c$ such that constraining $\|\mathbf{w}\|_2 \leq c$ gives the same solution. Verify numerically.

Answer. $c = \|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_\alpha\|_2$: solve the penalised problem and read off the norm you landed at. The correspondence is monotone but not linear: shrinking $c$ raises $\alpha$ faster than proportionally, because room you are short of is worth more (Part III §19).
CoreC7 · Noise is a penalty

Fit a linear model on inputs corrupted with noise $\sigma$, averaged over many copies. Compare against ridge with $\alpha = n\sigma^2$ on clean data.

Answer. They agree to about 1% across the range (Figure 4). The cross-term vanishes because the noise has mean zero and is independent of the data, and what survives is $\sigma^2\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$.
CoreC8 · The kept-fraction of early stopping

Show that gradient descent from zero on a quadratic covers a fraction $1-(1-\epsilon\lambda)^\tau$ of each eigen-direction after $\tau$ steps.

Answer. In the eigenbasis each coordinate obeys $y_{t+1} = y_t - \epsilon\lambda(y_t - y^*)$, so $y^* - y_{t+1} = (1-\epsilon\lambda)(y^*-y_t)$. Starting from $y_0 = 0$ the remaining distance is $(1-\epsilon\lambda)^\tau y^*$, so the covered fraction is $1-(1-\epsilon\lambda)^\tau$.
CoreC9 · How close is the equivalence

Plot $1-(1-\epsilon\lambda)^\tau$ against $\lambda/(\lambda+1/(\tau\epsilon))$ across several decades of $\lambda$. Where do they differ most?

Answer. They agree closely at both ends and differ by up to about 0.23 in the middle, where a direction is half-learned (measured in Figure 5). The correspondence is one of shape and ordering, not identity, and it assumes a quadratic loss besides.
CoreC10 · Bagging algebra

Derive $\frac1k v + \frac{k-1}{k}c$ for the ensemble's expected squared error, and read off both extremes.

Answer. Expand $\mathbb{E}[(\frac1k\sum\epsilon_i)^2]$ into $k$ variance terms and $k(k-1)$ covariance terms, each divided by $k^2$. At $c = v$ it is $v$: no benefit. At $c = 0$ it is $v/k$. The benefit is the disagreement, and $c$ is a floor no ensemble size can beat.
CoreC11 · Measure the $1/k$

Average $k$ models fitted to independent samples and measure bias² and variance separately against $k$.

Answer. Variance falls close to $1/k$ (Figure 6 measured a factor of 39 over $k = 1$ to $50$), while bias² stays flat. If your variance explodes instead, your members are numerically unstable: a high-degree polynomial on few points blows up outside the data, which is how this figure was got wrong the first time.
CoreC12 · Dropout by hand

Implement inverted dropout in a network you wrote yourself. Confirm the same mask is used in the forward and backward pass.

Answer. The mask must be stored and reapplied on the way back: a unit that was deleted contributed nothing, so it receives nothing. Using a fresh mask in the backward pass computes the gradient of a different network than the one you evaluated, and a gradient check catches it immediately.
CoreC13 · The dropout U

Sweep $p$ from 0 to 0.8 on a model with a real generalisation gap. Report training and test error.

Answer. Training error rises monotonically with $p$; test error falls then rises. Figure 7 measured the optimum near $p = 0.4$ on that problem. Average several seeds, as a single run's curve is too noisy to locate a minimum.
CoreC14 · Build an adversarial example

Take a trained classifier, compute the gradient with respect to the input, and step along its sign. Compare against a random perturbation of the same magnitude.

Answer. The gradient direction destroys accuracy at a perturbation size where random noise does essentially nothing (Figure 8 measured chance accuracy at $\epsilon = 0.10$ where random noise left 98.5%). The model is fragile in one direction out of many, and it is not fragile in general.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · The whole equivalence, end to end

On one quadratic problem, produce four solutions: ridge at $\alpha$, gradient descent stopped at $\tau = 1/(\alpha\epsilon)$, a fit on inputs with noise $\sigma = \sqrt{\alpha/n}$, and a constrained fit at $c = \|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_\alpha\|$. Report the pairwise distances.

Answer. Ridge, noise and the constraint agree to within a percent or so; early stopping agrees in ordering and drifts in the transition region. Producing all four from one script is the most convincing form this part's argument can take.
StretchS2 · Regularisers add

Apply input noise and weight decay together and measure the effective total. Does the solution match ridge at $\alpha_{\text{noise}} + \alpha_{\text{explicit}}$?

Answer. For a linear model, yes: the two penalties are the same quadratic form and their coefficients add. This is why stacking mild-looking regularisers produces severe underfitting, and why sweeping one while another dominates looks like the swept one having no effect.
StretchS3 · Dropout as an ensemble, measured

Train with dropout. At test time, instead of switching it off, average predictions over many random masks. How close is that to the deterministic answer?

Answer. Close, and it converges as you average more masks, which is the evidence for the ensemble reading. The deterministic pass is an approximation to the ensemble average that costs one forward pass instead of hundreds, and its accuracy is the reason nobody does the sampling in practice.
StretchS4 · Augmentation that is false

Deliberately augment with a transformation that does not preserve labels, such as vertical flips on digits. Measure the damage against no augmentation.

Answer. Test accuracy falls, and the loss curve looks entirely healthy while it does. That combination (trains fine, quietly worse) is the signature of a false invariance, and the only reliable check is to look at the augmented examples and ask whether the label still holds.
StretchS5 · Adversarial training

Generate adversarial examples during training and include them. Measure robust accuracy, clean accuracy, and wall-clock cost.

Answer. Robustness improves substantially, clean accuracy usually falls a little, and each step costs roughly twice as much because building the attack needs its own forward and backward pass. All three numbers belong in any claim about adversarial training; reporting only the first is the standard way to overstate it.
StretchS6 · Where the classical story breaks

Reproduce Part V §20's double descent, then apply weight decay at each width. What does regularization do to the interpolation peak?

Answer. It flattens it: enough regularization removes the peak entirely, which is why the phenomenon went unnoticed for so long: the default settings of most solvers regularize it away. That is worth sitting with, because it means the classical U-curve and double descent are not rival accounts of one experiment; they are what you see at two different regularization strengths.
