§21 One idea, six costumes

The one thing to carry forward

Every regulariser is a statement about which functions you would rather have, made before you know which ones fit.

That is why they can be equivalent despite looking nothing alike: two procedures expressing the same preference must produce the same solutions, whatever route they take to it. Input noise and weight decay are not similar in form and are identical in effect, because both say the output should not swing wildly.

And it is why none of them is universally good. A preference is an assumption, Part VI §7 guarantees no assumption wins everywhere, and the useful question is never "how much regularization" but "which preference is true here, and how strongly".

§22 Choosing, in practice

The honest ordering, and it is not the order these are usually taught in.

What to reach for, in order
  1. Diagnose first. Part VI §6's learning curves. If both errors are high and close, everything below will make it worse: you have a bias problem and regularization treats variance.
  2. More data, if it can be had. It cuts variance and buys no bias, which nothing else here offers.
  3. Augmentation, if you know a true invariance. The only regulariser carrying actual knowledge of your problem.
  4. Early stopping. Nearly free, self-tuning, composes with everything.
  5. Weight decay, swept on a validation set. The reliable generic.
  6. Dropout, for large models with a real gap.
  7. Ensembling, when the compute is worth the last point or two.
Pitfall: the total, not the terms

The single most common regularization error in practice is not picking the wrong one. It is not noticing how many are running at once.

A typical modern configuration has augmentation, dropout, weight decay, early stopping, a finite learning rate, and small-batch gradient noise: six preferences, each defensible, and their strengths add. §12 showed input noise alone contributing an effective $\alpha$ twenty times the explicit one; §14 showed early stopping contributing $2.5$ where someone was about to sweep $\alpha$ over $[0.1, 1]$.

The symptom is underfitting that looks like a broken model: training error will not come down, and adding capacity does not help because capacity is not the constraint. The diagnostic is to turn them all off and confirm the model can overfit (Part VII §20's ten-example test), and then add back one at a time.

§23 What regularization cannot do

Three limits worth stating plainly, because each is a place where more regularization is the wrong response.

It cannot manufacture information
Part V §20's irreducible error $\sigma^2$ is a property of the world. A model already at the noise floor is finished, and every technique here will only move it away. Knowing $\sigma^2$ (or having a human baseline that estimates it) is what tells you whether you are underfitting or done.
It cannot fix a wrong model class
If the true function is not in your family, no preference among the functions that are will reach it. Regularization chooses within a class; it cannot enlarge one. A straight line penalised more gently is still a straight line.
It cannot survive distribution shift
Every guarantee in Parts V and VI rests on the i.i.d. assumption of Part VI §5. A regulariser tuned on a validation set drawn from the training distribution tells you nothing about a deployment distribution that differs. This is the most common way a well-regularized model fails in production, and no amount of $\alpha$ addresses it.
Why the honest version of this part is short

Regularization has an unusual place in the field: it is where practice ran furthest ahead of theory, and the gap has not closed.

Dropout was proposed with an ensemble argument that is suggestive rather than rigorous. Batch normalisation's stated explanation has since been disputed while the method keeps working. The classical bias–variance account that motivates the whole subject fails to describe modern networks, as Part V §20's double-descent figure showed, and the question of why enormously overparameterized models generalise at all remains open.

What survives that uncertainty is the part of this chapter that is derived rather than observed: the eigen-shrinkage of §4, the noise-equals-penalty identity of §12, the constrained reading of §7, the variance algebra of §18. Those are theorems and will still be true when the folklore around them has been replaced.

Which is the argument for having done them properly. When a field's practice is unsettled, the derivations are the part you can stand on.

Try it: the last one

A model gets 3% training error and 22% test error. You add dropout 0.5, weight decay $\alpha = 0.1$, and heavy augmentation, all at once. Training error rises to 19% and test error to 24%. What happened, and what is the next step?

Answer. You overshot, and by a lot. The original diagnosis was right (a 19-point gap is variance, and regularization was the correct family of remedy), but three regularisers applied simultaneously totalled far more than the problem needed, and the model is now underfitting. Training error at 19% is the tell: it is close to test error, which is Part VI §6's high-bias signature, and it means the model can no longer represent the data it has.

The next step is not to reduce all three proportionally. You do not know which is doing the damage, and their strengths are not comparable in any units you have. Turn all three off, confirm the original 3%/22% reproduces, then add one back and sweep it alone on the validation set. Then the next, holding the first at its chosen value.

That is slower and it is the only procedure that produces a number you can defend. It is also Part V §14's warning in a new setting: three knobs tuned jointly by eye is a search, and the configuration you land on is partly selected by luck.
