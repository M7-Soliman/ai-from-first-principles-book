Part AThe objects

§1 Why linear algebra, in one honest paragraph

Here is the entire justification, and it is worth more than a page of hedging: a neural network is a stack of matrix multiplications with a simple nonlinear function sprinkled between them. That is not a simplification for beginners. That is literally what the object is. When you train a network on a GPU, upwards of ninety percent of the electricity is spent multiplying matrices. Linear algebra is not background material for deep learning in the way that, say, chemistry is background material for cooking. It is the material.

So a specific, useful way to read Part I: you are learning to read the source code of the thing you want to build.

To make that concrete before we have any tools: here is a complete two-layer neural network, the kind that classifies handwritten digits:

$$\hat{\mathbf{y}} = f\big(W_2 \, g(W_1 \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2\big)$$

Right now that is probably noise. By the end of Section 8 you will read it the way you read a sentence: $\mathbf{x}$ is the image, $W_1$ and $W_2$ are the things we learn, $g$ and $f$ are simple fixed functions, and the whole expression is a recipe that turns pixels into a guess. Every symbol in it is defined in this chapter. That is the climb.

Why deep learning cares

The specific reason matrices dominate is hardware. A GPU is, in essence, a machine built to multiply matrices very fast in parallel. Expressing a computation as matrix operations is what makes it fast enough to be worth doing. A model that cannot be written in this language cannot be trained at scale, which means the mathematics here is not just descriptive, it actively shapes which ideas in the field succeed.

§2 Scalars, vectors, matrices, tensors

There are four objects, and they form a ladder where each rung adds one index. That is the whole classification: it is genuinely no deeper than counting indices.

Scalar: zero indices

A single number. $s = 3.7$. That is it. We write scalars in ordinary italic lowercase, and say what kind they are: "let $n \in \mathbb{N}$ be the number of layers", or "let $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ be the learning rate."

Read $\in$ as "is an element of" or just "is a", and $\mathbb{R}$ as "the real numbers", meaning ordinary numbers with decimal points, as opposed to integers or complex numbers. So $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ is the mathematician's way to write alpha: float.

Vector: one index

An ordered list of numbers. We write vectors in bold lowercase, and identify a single entry with a subscript:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$$

The notation $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ says: three real numbers, in a fixed order. Order matters: $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(3, 2, 1)$ are different vectors, exactly as they would be different Python lists.

The subscript picks one out: $x_2$ is the second entry, a scalar. Note it is not bold, because it is a scalar rather than a vector; the boldness is doing real work in this notation, and it repays attention.

Pitfall: indexing from one

Mathematics indexes from 1; Python indexes from 0. The first entry of $\mathbf{x}$ is $x_1$ here and x[0] in your editor. This causes a genuinely large number of off-by-one bugs, and there is no fix other than staying awake. When translating a formula to code, convert indices deliberately rather than by reflex.

Matrix: two indices

History: the word "matrix" means womb

Rectangular arrays of numbers were used for centuries before anyone named them. The name arrived in 1850, from James Joseph Sylvester, and his reasoning is worth knowing because it inverts what you would expect.

In Sylvester's time determinants were the object of study: the important quantity, the thing theorems were about. The grid of numbers was merely the thing you extracted determinants from. So he called it a matrix, borrowing the Latin word for womb: the thing that gives birth to determinants.

His friend Arthur Cayley then spent the 1850s and 60s showing that the container was more interesting than the thing it contained, defining matrix multiplication, the identity, the inverse, and establishing that matrices form an algebra in their own right.

The hierarchy has since completely reversed. Matrices are central to modern mathematics and computation; determinants, as §22 will note, are a fine conceptual tool and a poor numerical one. The subordinate object outgrew the one it was named after.

A rectangular grid. Bold capital, two subscripts, row first, then column:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} A_{1,1} & A_{1,2} \\ A_{2,1} & A_{2,2} \\ A_{3,1} & A_{3,2} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}$$

So $A \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}$ is three rows, two columns. $A_{2,1}$ sits in row 2, column 1. Rows before columns, always, everywhere, without exception: this convention is worth burning in now, because a surprising amount of later confusion is just row/column order slipping.

Two pieces of shorthand you will meet constantly:

  • $A_{i,:}$: the whole of row $i$, as a vector.
  • $A_{:,j}$: the whole of column $j$, as a vector. The colon means "all of them".

Column notation matters more than it looks, because Section 4 will show that the columns of a matrix are the key to understanding what it does.

Tensor: three or more indices

Same idea, more axes. A colour image is naturally a tensor with three indices (height, width, and colour channel), so $\mathsf{A}_{i,j,k}$ is the intensity of channel $k$ at pixel $(i,j)$.

Pitfall: "tensor" means two different things

In physics and differential geometry, a tensor is an object with specific transformation properties under change of coordinates, and the definition has real content. In deep learning, "tensor" means an array with any number of axes, and carries no additional meaning whatsoever. PyTorch and TensorFlow use the second sense. If you have taken a physics course, deliberately set that meaning aside, as it will only slow you down here.

The whole ladder in one table

Notice the third row. A batch of vectors is a matrix: one row per example. This is how essentially all machine learning code is organised, and it is why matrices matter even when your conceptual object is a single data point.

Watch the shapes

From here on, every object gets its shape written down at first mention. When you read $W \in \mathbb{R}^{10 \times 784}$, say to yourself: ten rows, seven hundred eighty-four columns. When you read $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{784}$: a list of 784 numbers. Doing this out loud for the first few days feels excessive and works extremely well.

Grounding it in code

Everything above, in NumPy. Run this, actually run it, the muscle memory is the point:

# the four objects, and the only property that distinguishes them
import numpy as np

s = np.array(3.7)                      # scalar
x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])            # vector
A = np.array([[1., 2.], [3., 4.], [5., 6.]])  # matrix, 3 rows x 2 cols
T = np.zeros((2, 3, 4))                # tensor, 3 axes

for name, obj in [("s", s), ("x", x), ("A", A), ("T", T)]:
    print(f"{name}:  shape={obj.shape}   ndim={obj.ndim}")

### s:  shape=()          ndim=0
### x:  shape=(3,)        ndim=1
### A:  shape=(3, 2)      ndim=2
### T:  shape=(2, 3, 4)   ndim=3

# row first, then column -- always
print(A[1, 0])      # row 1, col 0 (0-indexed) -> the math A_{2,1}
print(A[1, :])      # all of row 1      -> [3. 4.]
print(A[:, 0])      # all of column 0   -> [1. 3. 5.]

ndim is exactly the index count from our ladder. That is the whole classification, expressed as one attribute.

In the framework: why everything is called a tensor

The same four objects in PyTorch, which is the library the rest of this book will use when it uses one at all:

import torch

s = torch.tensor(3.7)                          # shape ()        ndim 0
x = torch.tensor([1., 2., 3.])                  # shape (3,)      ndim 1
A = torch.tensor([[1.,2.],[3.,4.],[5.,6.]])      # shape (3, 2)    ndim 2
T = torch.zeros(2, 3, 4)                     # shape (2, 3, 4) ndim 3

A[1, 0]     # 3.0        -- row first, then column, exactly as in the maths
A[1]        # [3., 4.]   -- all of row 1
A[:, 0]     # [1., 3., 5.] -- all of column 0

The naming is the thing to understand. A scalar, a vector and a matrix are all torch.Tensor. The library has no separate types for them, and this is deliberate rather than lazy: it is the ladder of §2 taken seriously. If the only difference between the four objects is how many indices they carry, then one type with a shape attribute expresses that exactly, and a function written for one rank frequently works unchanged for another.

Recall the pitfall above: "tensor" here carries none of its meaning from physics. The library is simply using the word for "array with any number of axes", and ndim is the count.

What differs from NumPy, and why. Two things, both of which will matter later:

  • The default type is 32-bit, where NumPy's is 64-bit. torch.tensor([1.0]).dtype is float32. This is not a detail: it halves memory and roughly doubles throughput on a GPU, and it costs you half your significant digits. Part III §1 is about what that buys and what it costs.
  • A tensor knows where it lives. x.device is cpu or cuda:0, and operations require their operands to be in the same place. This is the single most common error message a beginner meets, and it exists because moving data between processor and accelerator is expensive enough that the library refuses to do it silently.

TensorFlow uses the identical model: tf.constant([1., 2., 3.]), with .shape and .dtype. Everything in this part about shapes transfers between the two without change, because it is a fact about the mathematics rather than about either library.

Try it

Without running anything: what is the shape of a batch of 16 greyscale images, each 28 by 28 pixels? And how many numbers is that in total?

Answer. Shape (16, 28, 28), a 3-axis tensor. Total count $16 \times 28 \times 28 = 12{,}544$ numbers. The shape is the whole story: multiply its entries and you get the memory footprint. (If the images were colour, you would have (16, 3, 28, 28) and three times the numbers.)

§3 Two ways to see a vector

A vector is a list of numbers. But there are two mental pictures for that list, and fluent people switch between them constantly without noticing. Learning to switch deliberately is worth doing early.

View 1: a vector is an arrow

An instruction for movement: go 3 units right, 2 units up. The arrow has a direction and a length, and crucially it does not care where it starts. This picture makes addition obvious: to add two vectors, walk the first, then walk the second from wherever you landed. It makes scaling obvious too: $2\mathbf{v}$ is the same direction, twice as far, and $-\mathbf{v}$ is the same distance backwards.

View 2: a vector is a point

A location: the place you get to from the origin. This picture is the right one for data. A data point with 784 pixel values is a single point in a 784-dimensional space, and a dataset of 60,000 such images is a cloud of 60,000 points in that space.

The point view is what makes machine learning geometric. Similar images sit near each other; classification becomes the problem of carving the space into regions; PCA in Section 23 becomes the question of which direction the cloud is longest along. None of that language is available under the arrow view alone.

Intuition: when to use which

Use arrows when you are thinking about operations: adding, scaling, transforming, or asking what a matrix does. Use points when you are thinking about data: distances, clusters, neighbourhoods, or spread. When a passage stops making sense, a genuinely reliable debugging move is to try the other picture.

The dimension problem, and how to survive it

Both pictures are drawable in two and three dimensions. Deep learning routinely uses 784, or 4096, or several million. Nobody visualises that. Not your professor, not the authors of this book.

What experienced people actually do is reason in two or three dimensions and then carry the conclusion upward, because almost every fact in this chapter is dimension-independent. "The set of points reachable by combining two independent vectors is a plane" is true in $\mathbb{R}^3$ and equally true in $\mathbb{R}^{784}$, it is just a two-dimensional plane sitting inside a very large room.

So: draw the 2-D picture without guilt. It is not a childish version of the real thing; it is the real thing with the irrelevant dimensions omitted.

Pitfall: where low-dimensional intuition genuinely fails

A few facts do break in high dimensions, and it is worth knowing they exist so you are not blindsided later. In high-dimensional space, randomly chosen vectors are almost always nearly perpendicular to each other; nearly all the volume of a ball sits close to its surface; and the ratio between the nearest and farthest points in a random cloud approaches one, which is why distance-based methods degrade as dimension grows. None of these matter for Part I, and all of them are worth remembering when you meet the "curse of dimensionality" in Part VI §21.

Vector addition and scaling, concretely

Both operations work entry by entry, which is the simplest possible rule:

$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad 2.5\,\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix}$$

Addition requires both vectors to have the same length; you cannot add a vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$ to one in $\mathbb{R}^4$, and NumPy will tell you so in a way we decode in Appendix C. Scaling by a scalar touches every entry.

Putting these two operations together gives the single most important construction in the chapter, which we will name properly in Section 11 and use constantly before then: a linear combination, meaning scale some vectors and add the results:

$$c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k \mathbf{v}_k$$

Almost everything ahead (matrix multiplication, span, rank, eigenvectors, PCA) is a question about linear combinations wearing a different hat.
