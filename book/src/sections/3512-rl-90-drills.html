PracticeDrills

Thirty-seven problems. Small MDPs can be solved exactly, so most of these have a right answer to check against rather than a plausible one.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · What more episodes buy

You estimate a policy gradient from 100 episodes and the estimate is too noisy. Write down how much the standard error falls if you go to 400.

Answer. By a factor of two, not four: the error goes as 1/√n. Each halving of noise costs four times the data, which is the arithmetic behind Part XVIII §27's baselines: reducing variance at the source beats buying it with samples, and it is the only thing that scales. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

WarmW1 · What changes

Name the four difficulties that follow from the learner choosing its own data.

Answer. Credit assignment, exploration, non-stationarity of the state distribution, and delayed sparse reward. Every method in the part is a response to one.
WarmW2 · Three reasons for $\gamma$

Give them, and say which is the load-bearing one.

Answer. It makes the return finite, it expresses a preference for sooner, and it makes the Bellman operator a contraction. The third is load-bearing: every convergence result in the part rests on it.
WarmW3 · Why $Q$ rather than $V$

Why does value-based control learn $Q$?

Answer. Given $Q$ you can act greedily by comparing actions with no model. Given only $V$ you need $p(s'\mid s,a)$ to know where each action leads.
WarmW4 · One symbol

Write the SARSA and Q-learning targets and say what the difference means.

Answer. $Q(s',a')$ for the action actually taken, against $\max_{a'}Q(s',a')$. On-policy against off-policy: SARSA learns the value of the policy it is running, exploration included; Q-learning learns the optimal policy's values whatever it does.
WarmW5 · The triad

Name the three ingredients and say what happens when all three are present.

Answer. Function approximation, bootstrapping, off-policy sampling. The iteration can have no fixed point, so the weights diverge, and removing any one of the three restores convergence.
WarmW6 · Why a baseline is free

Give the three-line identity.

Answer. $\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[\nabla\log\pi(a\mid s)] = \sum_a \nabla\pi(a\mid s) = \nabla\sum_a \pi(a\mid s) = \nabla 1 = 0$. The expected score function is zero, so subtracting anything independent of the action leaves the expectation unchanged.
WarmW7 · Why the step size matters more here

Why is a too-large step worse in RL than in supervised learning?

Answer. A worse policy collects worse data, from which you learn a worse policy. There is no fixed dataset to recover against, so a collapse can be permanent.
WarmW8 · Linear regret

Why does $\epsilon$-greedy's regret grow linearly, and what do UCB and Thompson do differently?

Answer. It explores at a fixed rate forever and uniformly at random, so it keeps paying $\epsilon$ times the average gap. UCB and Thompson explore in proportion to uncertainty, so exploration is spent where information is and the per-step regret falls to zero.
WarmW9 · Reward hacking

A policy earns a high reward and does the wrong thing. What went wrong?

Answer. Nothing, in the agent. The reward was a specification and the agent found what it literally said. The gap between the specification and the intention is what the optimiser is being paid to exploit.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Value iteration, and its uniqueness

Implement it on a random MDP. Then run it from three very different starting points (all zeros, all $+500$, all $-500$) and confirm they reach the same fixed point.

Answer. They do, to machine precision. That is the contraction's uniqueness claim, and it is the property that makes the initialisation irrelevant.
CoreC2 · The rate is exactly $\gamma$

Measure the per-sweep ratio of the max-norm error against the converged answer, for several $\gamma$. Compare to theory.

Answer. They agree to several decimal places. Then compute how many sweeps six decimal places needs at $\gamma = 0.9$ and at $0.99$ (132 against 1,375), and note that long horizons are expensive in a way you can predict.
CoreC3 · Policy iteration terminates exactly

Implement it with exact evaluation. Count iterations and compare to value iteration's sweep count for the same accuracy.

Answer. A handful of iterations against hundreds of sweeps, because each one is a full solve. The termination is exact rather than asymptotic: finitely many deterministic policies, each strictly better than the last.
CoreC4 · Evaluation is a linear solve

Compute $V^\pi$ by solving $(I-\gamma P_\pi)V = r_\pi$ and by iterating. Confirm they agree, and compare the cost as the state space grows.

Answer. The solve is $O(S^3)$ and exact; the iteration is $O(S^2)$ per sweep and converges geometrically. The iteration wins as soon as $S$ is at all large.
CoreC5 · TD against Monte Carlo

On a chain whose true values you can solve for, run both across a range of episode budgets. Report error against the exact values and the spread across seeds.

Answer. Monte Carlo's spread is several times TD's. Note also that both plateau with a constant step size: that floor is the step size, not the method, and decaying it removes it.
CoreC6 · $n$-step, swept

Implement the $n$-step return and sweep $n$ from 1 to the episode length. Plot error against $n$.

Answer. A U-shape: the best $n$ is neither extreme. TD and Monte Carlo are the two ends of one family and the interior is usually better than both.
CoreC7 · The cliff

Build a gridworld with a shortest path running alongside a catastrophic drop. Train SARSA and Q-learning with $\epsilon$-greedy. Compare the paths learned and the rewards actually collected.

Answer. Q-learning learns the optimal path along the edge and falls off regularly; SARSA learns a safer path and collects more. Q-learning's policy is better and SARSA's behaviour is better, which is a real distinction rather than a curiosity.
CoreC8 · The maximisation bias, isolated

Every action has true value zero. Estimate each from a few samples, take the max, and average over trials. Sweep the number of actions.

Answer. Positive and growing. This is Jensen's inequality, and it is Part V §14's multiple comparisons arriving inside a training loop where it feeds back into itself.
CoreC9 · Double estimation

Repeat C8 selecting with one estimator and evaluating with another. Then build the two-state MDP where the bias makes Q-learning prefer a genuinely worse action, and run both.

Answer. The bias vanishes, and in the MDP the difference is large and persistent. The selection is still noisy; the evaluation is independent of the noise that drove it.
CoreC10 · Baird's counterexample

Implement it. Run four conditions: all three ingredients, no bootstrapping, on-policy, and tabular. Plot $\|\mathbf{w}\|$.

Answer. Only the first diverges. Be careful with the on-policy condition: it means the state distribution is the target policy's too, not merely that the importance ratio is one. Then halve the step size and confirm it delays divergence rather than preventing it.
CoreC11 · What a target network does

Add one to a diverging configuration and vary the update interval from 1 to 10,000.

Answer. Long intervals stabilise it by making each phase closer to ordinary regression against a fixed target. Note what this is: damping the feedback loop, not removing the reason it exists.
CoreC12 · The score function is mean-zero

For a softmax policy, verify $\sum_a \pi(a)\nabla\log\pi(a) = 0$ numerically, then confirm that a baseline leaves the sampled gradient's expectation unchanged.

Answer. Zero to machine precision. That identity is the entire justification for baselines, advantages and every variance reduction in the movement.
CoreC13 · What a baseline buys

On a problem where the exact gradient is computable, measure the sampled estimator's bias and variance with and without the mean baseline, across sample counts.

Answer. Variance falls by an order of magnitude or more; bias stays at zero. Then shift all rewards by $+100$ and repeat: the no-baseline variance explodes and the baselined version is unchanged, which is the mechanism.
CoreC14 · GAE, both ends

Implement it in one backward pass. Confirm $\lambda = 0$ gives the one-step advantage and $\lambda = 1$ the Monte Carlo one, then sweep $\lambda$ and measure bias and variance.

Answer. The same trade as C6, in the actor's target. Around 0.95 is the usual choice and it is worth confirming rather than inheriting.
CoreC15 · Why the min in PPO

Plot the clipped objective against the ratio for a positive and a negative advantage. Find where the gradient is zero.

Answer. Outside the trust region in the improving direction. The $\min$ means a large step buys nothing rather than being priced: you cannot pay a penalty to take it.
CoreC16 · REINFORCE end to end

Train a softmax policy on a small MDP with REINFORCE, then add a baseline, then a learned critic. Plot learning curves over ten seeds each.

Answer. Each step reduces the variance and the spread across seeds. Report the spread, not just the mean: §43 says it is the number that gets omitted.
CoreC17 · Four bandit strategies

Implement greedy, $\epsilon$-greedy, UCB and Thompson sampling. Run to a long horizon and plot cumulative regret and regret per step.

Answer. Run long enough: at short horizons UCB has not yet repaid its exploration and can look worse than $\epsilon$-greedy. The distinguishing property is the slope at the end, not the total.
CoreC18 · Where random exploration fails

Build a chain of $n$ states where reward sits at one end and only a specific $n$-step sequence reaches it. Run $\epsilon$-greedy for increasing $n$ and record when it stops finding the reward.

Answer. Very quickly. The probability of a specific length-$n$ sequence falls exponentially, which is why the bandit apparatus applied per step does not solve exploration in sequential problems.
CoreC19 · Potential-based shaping

Add $\gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$ for a large random $\Phi$ and confirm the optimal policy is unchanged. Then add an arbitrary action-dependent bonus and confirm it is not.

Answer. Compare the values the two policies achieve, not their action indices: ties are broken arbitrarily. Note also that the values shift by exactly $-\Phi$, which is the telescoping made visible.
CoreC20 · Build a reward hack

Write a gridworld with a goal and a repeatable small bonus. Solve exactly for the proxy and for the true objective. Report both returns under both policies.

Answer. With discounting, a repeatable small reward can beat a single terminal one. The proxy policy scores far higher on the proxy and may never reach the goal, and the reward curve rises throughout, which is why looking at the behaviour is not optional.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · DQN, ablated

Implement it on a small task, then remove each component in turn (replay, target network, double estimation) and measure the cost of each.

Answer. Both leave-one-out and add-one-in, per Part XIV §21, since the components interact. Removing replay usually hurts most.
StretchS2 · PPO's implementation details

Implement PPO, then ablate advantage normalisation, observation normalisation, value clipping, learning-rate annealing, orthogonal initialisation and the entropy bonus; and separately ablate the clipping itself.

Answer. The details often cost more than the clipping does. That is §32's uncomfortable finding, and reproducing it once changes how you read a method paper.
StretchS3 · Offline RL and extrapolation

Collect a dataset from a mediocre policy, then run Q-learning on it with no further interaction. Track the learned $Q$ values against the true returns.

Answer. They grow without bound. The max ranges over actions the data does not cover, the network extrapolates optimistically, and nothing corrects it. Then add a penalty for straying from the behaviour policy and watch it stop.
StretchS4 · A reward model, over-optimised

Generate preference pairs from a known ground-truth utility, fit a reward model, and optimise a policy against it. Plot reward-model score and true utility on the same axes.

Answer. They separate: the model's score keeps rising while the true utility peaks and falls. Then add a KL penalty to the initial policy and find the coefficient that keeps them together: that is the knob, and this is how it is tuned.
StretchS5 · Seeds, honestly

Take any algorithm here and run it with thirty seeds. Plot the full distribution of final performance, then report what three seeds would have told you.

Answer. Often a different answer, and sometimes the opposite one. §43 is why this literature has a reproducibility problem, and this drill is the cheapest way to believe it.
StretchS6 · Imitation first

On a task you can demonstrate, compare behaviour cloning against RL from scratch at matched compute.

Answer. Cloning usually wins by a wide margin, because it is supervised learning. §44's first case, measured, and worth doing before any RL project rather than after.
StretchS7 · The noisy TV

Add a curiosity bonus based on prediction error, then put an unpredictable stimulus in the environment. Watch what the agent does.

Answer. It stares at it. Infinite prediction error is infinite bonus, and the agent is correct: the bonus said so. §39's failure, arriving in the exploration bonus rather than the task reward.
