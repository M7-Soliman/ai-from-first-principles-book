Whether you have a graph problem at all, what to check before reaching for an architecture, and what a result on a graph owes its reader.

§39 Do you have a graph problem

Three questions, in order

Is the structure informative? Fit a model on the node features alone, ignoring every edge. If it matches the graph model, the edges are decoration and you have a tabular problem (Part XXV's) with an expensive wrapper.

Is the graph homophilous? Measure the fraction of edges joining nodes with the same label. Message passing assumes neighbours are alike, and on a heterophilous graph the aggregation is averaging away the signal. Below about half, standard architectures underperform a feature-only baseline.

How far does the information have to travel? If the answer is one or two hops, keep the network shallow and neither §13's collapse nor §20's dilution has room to bite. If it is far, expect both and read §23.

These three take an afternoon and decide more than the choice of architecture does.

§40 The baselines that must be reported

Four, and the first two are usually missing

Features only. A tabular model on the node features, no graph. This is the baseline the graph has to beat and it is left out of most papers.

Structure only. Degree, spectral coordinates, subgraph counts, no features. Tells you which of the two sources carries the signal.

Label propagation. Spread known labels along edges with no learning at all. On a homophilous graph it is remarkably hard to beat, and it costs nothing.

A shallow message-passing network. Two layers, standard. Most of the reported gains from elaborate architectures are within noise of this.

§41 The reproducibility problem in this area

Go and read

Shchur et al. (2018), Pitfalls of graph neural network evaluation. The re-evaluation that found a well-tuned simple model matching most of what was published to beat it. Read it as a method paper about comparison, not as a result about graphs.

Why published comparisons on graphs are unusually unreliable

Several causes compound. The standard node-classification benchmarks are small, so the spread between seeds is comparable to the differences between methods. Splits vary between papers and are often not the published ones. Baselines are frequently untuned while the proposed method is not: Part XXV §27's requirement, ignored. And §27's leakage is easy and silent.

Careful re-evaluations have repeatedly found that a well-tuned two-layer network matches most of what was reported to beat it. Read a graph result by asking which of these four applies before asking what the architecture does.

§42 What to report

Six things

The split, and whether it is transductive or inductive. They are different problems.

The feature-only baseline. Without it the graph's contribution is unmeasured.

Homophily. One number, and it predicts whether the architecture suits the graph.

The spread over seeds. On these benchmarks it is often larger than the effect.

How negatives were sampled, for link prediction, since the metric depends on it.

Which symmetry the architecture assumes, and whether the problem has it.

§44 What is unsolved

Four, distinguished by kind

Not yet measured. Whether higher-order equivariant networks are worth their cost across tasks. The reported gains are real and the comparisons are usually against weaker baselines than §40 requires.

Not currently measurable. Whether a large model trained on enough data learns a symmetry well enough that building it in stops paying. Figure 5 measures the gap narrowing; where it lands at scales nobody has run is an extrapolation.

Genuinely open. Long-range dependence on graphs, given that over-smoothing and over-squashing pull opposite ways. Rewiring and transformers each trade something away and neither is a solution.

Not empirical. Whether a rewired graph is still the object you were asked about. That is a question about the problem, not about the model.

§45 What to carry forward

Five things

An architecture is a declaration about a symmetry group. Convolution declares translation, message passing declares permutation, a molecular network declares both permutation and rigid motion. Getting the group wrong in either direction is a modelling error.

Building a symmetry in is exact; learning it is approximate. Figure 1 measures 1.000 against 0.947 on relabelled graphs with augmentation, and Figure 5 measures a gap that rises from 0.004 to 0.073 as both models leave the weak-data regime, then narrows to 0.039, and does not close.

Depth on a graph is reach, and the operator is a diffusion. Figure 2's representations collapse to a mean pairwise distance of zero, taking the task with them. More layers is not more capacity.

The family has an exact expressive ceiling. Figure 3's two graphs receive identical representations to exactly zero at every depth, because message passing is colour refinement with a learned hash. This is a proof, not a tendency.

Check whether you have a graph problem first. A feature-only baseline, a homophily number and the distance the information must travel decide more than the architecture, and all three are an afternoon's work.

Sixty seconds

A graph has no node ordering and a molecule has no orientation, so a model reading either should be built not to notice. That is what an architecture is: a claim about which transformations leave the answer alone, and the claim buys you the samples you would otherwise spend learning it. Message passing is the standard way to make the claim for permutations, and it comes with two structural limits (representations collapse as you add rounds, and distant information is diluted before it arrives), plus an exact ceiling on what it can distinguish at all. Before any of that, check whether the edges are carrying anything: fit the features alone, measure how often neighbours agree, and ask how far the signal has to go.
