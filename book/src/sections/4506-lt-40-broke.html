Part DWhere the theory broke

Everything so far is correct and, at the sizes anyone uses, numerically worthless. This movement measures how worthless, establishes that the failure is structural rather than a loose constant, and goes through what has been proposed instead, with the current state of the evidence for each, which is weaker than the volume of writing suggests.

§23 The bound against the gap it bounds

Vacuous

A bound is vacuous when it permits values the quantity cannot take. A generalisation bound above 1 is satisfied by every possible classifier including a random one, so it excludes nothing and carries no information.

The bounds on the right of Figure 3 are not merely loose. They are not statements about the model at all.

Why this is not fixable by sharpening the constants

Modern networks routinely have more parameters than training samples, so $d/n > 1$ and the bound is at least $\sqrt{d/n} > 1$ before any constant is chosen. No improvement to the proof helps.

Worse, the argument cannot be rescued by observing that the effective capacity is smaller than the parameter count, because the capacity is demonstrably maximal: a standard network trained on randomly labelled data reaches zero training error. It can fit any labelling of the training set, so its VC dimension is at least $n$, so uniform convergence over that class provably gives nothing; §13's lower bound applies.

And yet the same network trained on the real labels generalises well. Both facts hold at once, which means the explanation cannot be a property of the hypothesis class. It has to involve the data, the algorithm, or both.

Go and read

Zhang et al. (2017), Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization. The randomised-label experiment, run cleanly, with the consequences stated plainly. Short, and the argument is entirely available to a reader of movement B: the paper's contribution is noticing that an obvious experiment settles a question the field had assumed was open.

§24 Interpolation is not overfitting

The classical picture, and where it came from

The U-shaped curve, error falling then rising as capacity grows, is real and is a statement about a regime. It describes what happens up to the point where the model can fit the training data exactly, and the classical theory was built when that was where practice lived.

Past that point there are many exact fits, and which one you get is no longer determined by the objective. The curve does not continue upward, because the question has changed from "how well can it fit" to "which of the perfect fits does the algorithm choose".

§25 Double descent

Why the peak is where it is

At $p = n$ there is exactly one interpolating solution and no freedom to choose. The design matrix is square and typically ill-conditioned (Part III §5's condition number, arriving as a statistical phenomenon), so the unique exact fit has enormous norm and is wildly sensitive to the noise.

With $p \gg n$ the system is underdetermined and there are infinitely many exact fits, most of them badly behaved. The minimum-norm one is not, and that is what the solver returns. The second descent is entirely a statement about which solution gets picked, which is §27.

Pitfall: this does not license unlimited capacity

Double descent is real in this setup and in others, and it is not a general licence. The peak is a genuine hazard: a model sized near $n$ can be far worse than one either smaller or larger, so capacity chosen by a coarse sweep can land in the worst place available.

And the second descent depends on the implicit bias being a good one for the problem. Nothing guarantees that in general, which is the honest limit of the result.

Go and read

Belkin, Hsu, Ma & Mandal (2019), Reconciling modern machine-learning practice and the classical bias–variance trade-off. Already read at Part V §20; it belongs here too, as the curve this section explains. It named the interpolation threshold and drew the second descent past it on real models rather than a constructed example. Read it for the picture; §25's job is the mechanism the picture leaves open, which §27 then closes.

§26 Benign overfitting

Go and read

Bartlett, Long, Lugosi & Tsigler (2020), Benign overfitting in linear regression. The sharp characterisation for linear models: exactly which covariance spectra allow interpolation of noise without harm. The one place in this movement where the theory is complete.

Fitting the noise without being hurt by it

In high dimension an interpolating estimator can absorb the noise into directions that carry little of the signal and contribute little to the test error. The fit is exact on the training points and the damage is spread thinly enough not to matter.

This is now understood precisely for linear regression: the condition involves how the covariance spectrum decays, and there are sharp characterisations of when interpolation is benign. For deep networks it remains an analogy rather than a theorem.

§27 What the optimiser chooses

Implicit regularization

When the objective has many minimisers, the optimisation procedure selects among them. That selection is implicit regularization: a bias with the effect of a penalty that was never written into the loss.

The clean cases are known. Gradient descent from zero on underdetermined least squares converges to the minimum $\ell_2$-norm solution; Figure 5 measures it to fifteen digits. On separable data with logistic loss, it converges in direction to the maximum-margin separator, which is §18's bound arriving without anyone asking for it.

Why this is the most promising direction, and what it still lacks

It has the right shape. The bound must depend on the algorithm, because the class provably cannot supply one, and implicit regularization is a statement about the algorithm.

What is missing is the deep case. The results above are for linear models and specific losses. For a network trained with Adam, momentum, weight decay, batch normalisation and augmentation, there is no characterisation of what is implicitly preferred, and each of those components changes it. This is the honest state: the correct form of an explanation, without the explanation.

§28 Flatness, and the trouble with it

A proposal, and three objections

The claim: solutions in flat regions of the loss surface generalise better than those in sharp ones, on the intuition that flatness means insensitivity to perturbation.

It is not reparameterisation invariant. Rescaling one layer up and the next down leaves the function unchanged and can make a flat minimum sharp. Any measure of sharpness must handle this, and the naive ones do not.

The correlation is not causation. Flat solutions do tend to generalise better; whether flatness is the mechanism or a symptom of one is not established: the same gap between a favourable number and what it is evidence for as this part's own §36, where a no-regret guarantee does not establish that the comparator it beat was any good, and Part XXII's whole subject.

Optimising for it works anyway. Sharpness-aware minimisation improves test accuracy reliably, which is evidence the quantity is doing something, and is not evidence that the explanation is right. Part XVI's mode collapse is the precedent: a real effect with a wrong standard account.

§29 Norm-based bounds, and the ones that are not vacuous

Go and read

Dziugaite & Roy (2017), Computing nonvacuous generalization bounds for deep networks. PAC-Bayes bounds that come in under 1 on a real network, the first ones that did. Note carefully which object is bounded: a stochastic network, not the deterministic one you would deploy.

Progress, honestly stated

Replacing the parameter count with a norm of the weights gives bounds that are sometimes non-vacuous. The strongest results come from PAC-Bayes: bound the risk of a distribution over hypotheses in terms of its divergence from a prior, and compress the network so the posterior is cheap to describe. These have produced genuinely non-vacuous numbers on real networks and real data.

Two caveats to keep. The bounds are non-vacuous, around 0.2 to 0.5 where the true error is a few percent, rather than tight, so they exclude something but not much. And they typically bound a compressed or stochastic version of the network rather than the one you deployed.

This is real progress and it is not a solution. It is worth stating in that order.

§30 What movement D establishes

Three things

The classical bounds are vacuous at real sizes, provably. Figure 3 measures 1.1 to 10.0 where an error rate cannot exceed 1, and the randomised-label experiment shows the capacity really is maximal, so §13's lower bound forecloses any distribution-free repair.

Capacity past the interpolation threshold can help. Figure 4's second descent reaches 0.224 against a classical best of 0.541, because past that point the question stops being how well the class can fit and becomes which exact fit the algorithm picks.

The answer must involve the algorithm. Figure 5 shows four solutions with identical training error and test errors from 6.2 to 109.5; gradient descent finds the best of them to fifteen digits. That is the right shape for an explanation, and for deep networks it is a shape without contents.
