BuildThe project

Probability is a subject where simulation beats argument. If you are not sure whether a claim is true, generate a million samples and count. This project builds the tools to do that, then uses them to check every major result in the chapter.

P.1 What you are building

tinyprob: a small probability toolkit written from scratch, then used as an experimental instrument.

The rule: you may use a raw uniform random number generator and nothing else. rng.random() gives you a float in $[0,1)$. Everything (Bernoulli, categorical, Gaussian, mixtures) gets built from that. No rng.normal, no scipy.stats, until the final verification step.

P.2 Stage 1: samplers

Deliverable: for each sampler, draw 100,000 values and confirm the empirical mean and variance match the theoretical ones to three decimal places.

P.3 Stage 2: estimators

  • empirical_pmf(samples) and histogram_pdf(samples, bins): recover a distribution from samples.
  • entropy(p), kl(p, q), cross_entropy(p, q): handle zeros correctly, as drill C5 describes.
  • mutual_information(joint): from a joint table.

Deliverable: verify $H(P,Q) = H(P) + D_{KL}(P \parallel Q)$ numerically on random distributions. This identity is the hinge of §25, and confirming it yourself is worth more than reading the derivation twice.

P.4 Stage 3: verify the chapter

History: Monte Carlo was named after a casino, and invented during a card game

The technique of answering a hard mathematical question by generating random samples and counting has an origin story that is unusually well documented.

In 1946 Stanisław Ulam was recovering from illness and playing solitaire. He wondered what fraction of layouts could be won, tried to compute it combinatorially, found it hopeless, and realised it would be far easier to deal a large number of hands and count. He took the idea to von Neumann, who saw immediately that the new machines at Los Alamos could do exactly this for neutron diffusion problems that resisted analysis.

The work was classified and needed a codename. Nicholas Metropolis proposed Monte Carlo, after the casino in Monaco where Ulam's uncle was said to have borrowed money to gamble.

The method underwrites this project: when an integral or an expectation has no closed form (the usual situation in probabilistic modelling), you sample and average. Its error falls as $1/\sqrt{n}$ regardless of dimension, which is why it survives in spaces where every deterministic method collapses.

Now use the toolkit as an instrument. Each of these is a claim from the notes; your job is to test it rather than believe it.

  1. Central limit theorem. Average $n$ samples from three very different distributions. Show convergence to a Gaussian, and measure how $n$ must grow with skewness.
  2. Base rate trap. Simulate the §22 medical test with a million people. Confirm ~1.9%. Then sweep the prevalence from 0.01% to 50% and plot the posterior; the shape of that curve is the whole lesson.
  3. Zero covariance ≠ independence. Generate the ring from drill C9. Show covariance ≈ 0 and mutual information clearly positive. One statistic sees the dependence, the other is blind.
  4. KL asymmetry. Reproduce Figure 15 by direct optimisation.
  5. Maximum likelihood. Generate data from a known Gaussian, then recover $\mu$ and $\sigma$ by maximising log-likelihood numerically. Plot the estimate's error against sample size and confirm the $1/\sqrt{n}$ rate.

Deliverable: one figure per claim, each captioned with what it demonstrates and how it could have come out otherwise.

P.5 Stretch goals

  • Rejection sampling: sample from an arbitrary density using only uniforms. Measure the acceptance rate and watch it collapse in higher dimensions.
  • A tiny Naive Bayes classifier on real text, built entirely from your own estimators. It will work better than the independence assumption deserves; think about why.
  • Calibration curve for a small classifier you train, as in drill C15.
  • Estimate $\pi$ by Monte Carlo, and confirm the $1/\sqrt{n}$ error rate on log axes. A satisfying five-line program.

P.6 How to know you are done

  • Every sampler matches its theoretical mean and variance to three decimals.
  • The cross-entropy identity is verified numerically, not just quoted.
  • All five chapter claims have a figure and a caption.
  • Your KL implementation handles $P(x)=0$ and explains $Q(x)=0$.
  • Your README names one result that surprised you before you simulated it.
Why this project

Part I's project made abstract operations concrete. This one builds a different and equally important skill: using simulation to check whether you have understood something. Probability intuition is famously unreliable (the base rate result in §22 fools most people, including experts), and the ability to settle a question with fifty lines of sampling code is a genuine professional advantage. It is also exactly how researchers debug probabilistic models in practice.

Pitfall: $\sigma$ is overloaded

The same letter means standard deviation and the logistic sigmoid, sometimes on the same page. There is no fixing this; the convention is universal. Read the context: if it has an argument, $\sigma(x)$, it is the function. If it appears alone or squared, it is the standard deviation.
