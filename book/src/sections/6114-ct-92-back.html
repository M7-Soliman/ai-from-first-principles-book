BuildThe project

Fourteen to eighteen hours. You build a control stack on plants you wrote down (feedback, optimal control, an estimator, a planner) and then compute what it would take to claim the result is safe.

P.1 What you are building

actuate: PID with lag and delay, a Riccati solver, receding-horizon control with constraints, rigid transforms and a two-link arm, a sampling planner, and the validation arithmetic of movement F.

The rule: numpy only, and every plant written down. Knowing the dynamics means the optimal controller is computable, so each controller you build can be compared against the best one rather than against your last attempt.

P.2 Stage 1: feedback

  1. Simulate a first-order plant open loop with a mis-estimated gain. Confirm the error tracks the model error.
  2. Close the loop and sweep the gain. Verify the residual offset against $1/(1+k_p g)$ rather than assuming it.
  3. Find the gain below which feedback is worse than open loop, for a given model error.
  4. Add integral action and confirm the offset goes to zero.
  5. Add an actuator lag, then a measurement delay, and find the gain at which each destabilises.

Deliverable: the crossing from step three and the two stability thresholds. Then convert your own perception latency into loop delay and read off what gain you are allowed.

P.3 Stage 2: optimal control

  1. Implement the Riccati recursion. Check the closed-loop poles are inside the unit circle.
  2. Perturb the LQR gain randomly and confirm nothing costs less.
  3. Search hand-tuned PD gains on chains of two to five integrators and record the ratio.
  4. Then tune along the LQR direction instead, and notice the comparison becomes vacuous.
  5. Write the Kalman recursion beside the LQR one.

Deliverable: the ratio table and the two recursions side by side. Step four is a trap worth falling into once: a baseline that can recover the thing it is compared against measures nothing.

P.4 Stage 3: constraints

  1. Implement receding-horizon control: optimise over a horizon, apply one step, re-solve.
  2. Fix the plan instead of re-solving, and measure what the model error costs.
  3. Add an input limit and compare against clipping an unconstrained controller.
  4. Add a state limit and find a plant where clipping cannot respect it.
  5. Shorten the horizon until the controller commits to a trajectory it cannot exit.

Deliverable: the horizon at which step five happens, related to how fast your plant can stop. Step two is where you find out that the feedback is in the re-solving.

P.5 Stage 4: a body

  1. Implement rigid transforms and their inverses. Confirm the inverse is not the transpose.
  2. Convert between Euler angles, matrices and quaternions, and find gimbal lock.
  3. Build a two-link arm, compute its Jacobian, and command a motion at full extension.
  4. Add damping and measure the trade between bounded joint velocity and tracking error.
  5. Add gravity compensation and compare the gains you need with and without it.

Deliverable: the singularity plot and the two gain sets. Step five is the highest-value single term in the dynamics and takes ten lines.

P.6 Stage 5: state and planning

  1. Integrate a biased, noisy velocity and plot error against time. Separate the $t$ and $\sqrt{t}$ components.
  2. Fuse two sensors in a filter and watch the covariance when one drops out.
  3. Count grid cells against degrees of freedom.
  4. Implement a sampling planner and measure samples to solution against dimension.
  5. Look at the path it returns, and smooth it.

Deliverable: the two scaling curves and a before-and-after path. Step five is where you learn that feasibility and quality are separate problems.

P.7 Stage 6: learning, and its horizon

  1. Write down your predictions first, in a file, before running anything.
  2. Clone a policy from an expert's states and roll it out. Measure deviation against horizon.
  3. Relabel the states the learner reached and retrain. Measure again.
  4. Add the same volume of extra expert data instead, and compare: the improvement should come from the new states, not the new data.
  5. Perturb a known linear system's gain and measure absolute rollout divergence on stable, marginal and unstable plants.
  6. Score your predictions.

Deliverable: the three divergence curves and the scored predictions. Step five must use absolute divergence: the relative version is scale-free and puts all three curves on top of each other.

P.8 Stage 7: the claim

  1. Derive the rule of three.
  2. Compute the evidence needed to bound a rate you choose, and to estimate it.
  3. Express both as vehicle-years for a fleet size you choose.
  4. Write a one-page claim: the domain, the failure, the rate, the confidence, and the assumption each piece of evidence rests on.

Deliverable: the one-page claim. It is the only deliverable in this book that is a piece of writing rather than a measurement, and it is the one a deployment turns on.

P.9 Stretch

  • Iterative LQR on a pendulum swing-up, with the local optima it finds.
  • Sampling-based MPC, with the weighting temperature swept.
  • A shield: a verified fallback and the region it recovers from.
  • Self-play, evaluated against frozen ancestors.
  • A pose graph, with a false loop closure introduced deliberately.
  • An audit of something you deploy that takes actions.

P.10 Done when

  • Your measured offset matches 1/(1+kpg).
  • You have found a gain at which feedback is worse than none.
  • You have two stability thresholds, one from lag and one from delay.
  • Your LQR's closed-loop poles are inside the unit circle.
  • No perturbation of your LQR gain costs less than it does.
  • You know the ratio a hand-tuned PD achieves at two states and at five.
  • You have seen a baseline that can recover the thing it is compared against.
  • You have measured what happens when the plan is not re-solved.
  • You have a state limit that clipping cannot respect.
  • Your transform inverse is not the transpose, and you have checked.
  • You have found gimbal lock.
  • You have the joint velocity at a singularity, damped and undamped.
  • You have separated the t and √t components of dead-reckoning error.
  • Your rollout divergence is absolute, and the three plants separate.
  • You wrote predictions down before Stage 6 and scored them afterwards.
  • You have a one-page safety claim with its assumptions named.
Why this project

Every other project in this book ends with a measurement. This one ends with an argument, because a system that acts is not deployed on a number; it is deployed on a case, and the case has parts a metric cannot supply.

Stage 7 step three is the one that changes people's estimates. Converting a target failure rate into vehicle-years makes the arithmetic of movement F concrete in a way the miles alone do not, and it is usually the first time the number feels real.

The framework, and the loop

Dataset, model family, loss, optimiser. Every part until now has fitted a function to data that existed before the model did. This part breaks that: the policy determines the data, so there is no fixed distribution to fit and no test set that means what it usually means.

What replaces it is older than the framework. Feedback substitutes for knowledge, so the model matters less than the loop it sits in. The plant's stability, not the model's accuracy, sets how far ahead anything can be trusted. And the evidence a deployment turns on is not a metric at all but an argument, with named assumptions and an arithmetic that is unforgiving: a million clean miles bounds a failure rate only below one in three hundred thousand. Building the system is the smaller half of the work.
