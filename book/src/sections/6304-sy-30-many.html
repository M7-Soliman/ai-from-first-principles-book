Part CMany machines

Part XVII chose which axis to split a model along. This movement is the layer below: what the machines actually say to each other, how long that takes, and why one collective algorithm is used everywhere.

§17 The operations that exist

Four, and almost all traffic is the first

All-reduce. Every worker contributes a buffer, every worker ends with the sum. This is gradient averaging, and it is the overwhelming majority of what a training job sends.

All-gather. Every worker ends with everyone's buffer concatenated, used to reassemble sharded parameters.

Reduce-scatter. The sum, but each worker keeps only its slice. Half of an all-reduce.

All-to-all. Every worker sends a different piece to every other: what mixture-of-experts routing needs, and the most expensive of the four.

§18 Why the ring

Go and read

Thakur, Rabenseifner & Gropp (2005), Optimization of collective communication operations in MPICH. Why a library switches algorithm by message size, worked through for each collective. The volume-against-latency trade that Figure 4 leaves implicit is explicit here.

What the ring gives up

Volume is optimal and latency is not: a ring takes $2(N-1)$ sequential steps, so for a small buffer across many workers the time is dominated by per-hop latency and a tree is better. Which is why real libraries switch algorithm by message size, and why a benchmark at one size tells you little about another.

§19 The network is not uniform

Bandwidth by distance

Accelerators within a machine talk over a dedicated interconnect at hundreds of gigabytes a second; machines within a rack talk over a network at tens; racks talk at less. The ratios are large enough that which workers are adjacent changes the wall clock.

So the assignment of parallelism axes to hardware is a real decision: put the axis with the most traffic (tensor parallelism, which communicates every layer) inside a machine, and the axis with the least (data parallelism, which communicates once a step) across the slow links. Getting that backwards can cost more than any kernel optimisation recovers.

§20 Overlap

The optimisation that makes the rest affordable

Communication and computation use different hardware, so they can happen at once. Gradients for the last layer are ready before the backward pass reaches the first, so their all-reduce can start immediately and finish while the rest of the backward pass runs.

Done well this hides communication almost entirely, and it is why data-parallel training scales at all. Done badly (one all-reduce at the end of the step), the communication is fully exposed and the same job is much slower with nothing in the profile to point at except a gap.

§21 What actually goes wrong at scale

Four failures, in the order they cost the most

Stragglers. A synchronous step runs at the speed of the slowest worker, so one machine with a thermal problem sets the pace for the whole job. The distribution's tail matters, not its mean.

Failures. At thousands of machines and hours of running, something failing is the expected case. This is why checkpointing frequency is a real decision and not hygiene (Part XVII §28).

Non-determinism. Reduction order varies between runs, so results are not bitwise reproducible, and a bug that appears once may not appear again. Deterministic collectives exist and cost throughput.

Silent corruption. At enough machine-hours, a chip occasionally computes the wrong answer without saying so. Large operators check for it; it is the failure that most resembles a software bug and is not one.

§22 Where the memory goes

Four consumers, and only one is the model

Parameters, gradients, optimiser state (two moments for Adam, so roughly four times the parameters in total), and activations, which depend on batch size and depth and are frequently the largest.

Which is why the sharding strategies of Part XVII §18 exist and why activation recomputation is a lever: trading arithmetic for memory is favourable exactly when arithmetic is cheap, which §1 says it is.

§23 Serving is a different problem

Training is throughput, serving is latency under a distribution

A training job optimises total time and can batch as it likes. A serving system faces requests arriving at random with a latency target on each, so the quantity that matters is a tail percentile rather than a mean, and utilisation trades directly against it: a system run near saturation has a long tail by queueing theory alone, whatever the model does.

Part XIX's techniques all apply and the constraint is different, which is why the same model needs different engineering in the two settings.

§24 What movement C establishes

Three things

The ring is optimal in volume and not in latency. Figure 4 measures 2 buffers per worker against a linear growth, a factor of 32 at 64 workers, and libraries still switch algorithm by message size.

Topology is a decision. Put the chattiest parallelism axis on the fastest links, or lose more than any kernel optimisation recovers.

At scale the failures are statistical. Stragglers, hardware failures, non-determinism and silent corruption are all expected rather than exceptional.
