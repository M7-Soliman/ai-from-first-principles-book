Part DThe spectra of operators and of data

Frequency is not only for signals. Any symmetric operator has an eigenbasis, and on a graph or a mesh that basis behaves like frequency does on a line. Then two measurements: what natural data looks like in that basis, and the order in which a network learns it.

§25 Eigenvalues are frequencies

The generalisation

Sinusoids are the eigenvectors of the second-difference operator on a line. That is the whole reason they are the natural basis there: not a fact about waves, but about which operator you care about.

Take a different domain with a different notion of "neighbouring" and the same construction gives a different basis: the eigenvectors of that domain's Laplacian, ordered by eigenvalue. Small eigenvalue means the eigenvector varies slowly across neighbours; large means it alternates. That ordering is frequency, defined intrinsically.

§26 The graph Laplacian

What Part XXVI was leaning on

For a graph with adjacency $A$ and degree matrix $D$, the Laplacian is $L = D - A$, and $x^\top L x = \sum_{(i,j) \in E} (x_i - x_j)^2$, the total disagreement across edges. Minimising it over unit vectors gives the smoothest non-trivial assignment, the second eigenvector, and the rest follow in order of roughness.

Part XXVI §3 used the adjacency spectrum as a permutation-invariant summary and did not say why it works. It works because relabelling nodes conjugates $L$ by a permutation matrix, which leaves eigenvalues alone. The spectrum is the classical invariant, and Part XXVI §13's over-smoothing is the low-pass filter this basis makes visible.

Where else the same construction appears

Spectral clustering partitions using the low eigenvectors, the graph's low frequencies.

Positional encodings on graphs (Part XXVI §22) use those eigenvectors as coordinates, which is the direct analogue of the sinusoids of §33.

Meshes use the same Laplacian with geometric weights, giving a frequency decomposition of a surface. This is the machinery Part XXIX will need for shape.

§27 The spectrum of natural data

Reading the exponent honestly

The canonical figure quoted for natural scenes is an exponent near 2, and this sample measures 2.91. The three sources here are smoother than the outdoor scenes that number comes from, and the per-tile spread is wide: 1.28 to 3.58, standard deviation 0.56. So the exponent is a property of this sample and is reported as one.

What is not in doubt is the shape, and the shape is what the rest of this movement uses: natural data has almost all of its energy at low frequency, and white noise has it spread evenly. That is the difference between the two curves and it is not subtle.

§28 What that licenses

Three consequences of one measurement

Compression works. JPEG discards high-frequency coefficients because there is little there to discard. The measurement is the justification.

Downsampling is usually acceptable, provided you filter first. Figure 6's right panel is what happens when you do not, and it is Figure 1's defect seen in the other domain.

Smoothness priors are not arbitrary. Part VIII's penalties, Part XV §5's manifold assumption and Part XXVI's message passing all bet on the target being smooth. Figure 6 is the measurement that bet is placed on.

§29 Spectral bias

Go and read

Rahaman et al. (2019), On the spectral bias of neural networks. The phenomenon named and measured. Read §4 for the argument from the eigenvalues, then read it again beside Part XXIII §47, where the same argument becomes a derivation instead of an observation.

Spectral bias

Spectral bias is the tendency of a network trained by gradient descent to fit low-frequency components of its target before high-frequency ones, and to fit them faster.

It is not a regulariser anyone added. It is a property of the optimisation, and it is why networks generalise as often as they do: gradient descent finds the smooth explanation first, and early stopping keeps it.

§30 Why it happens

Two ways to see it, and they agree

Through the eigenvalues. Analysed at fixed weights the training dynamics are governed by the eigenvalues of a kernel that depends on the architecture, and error along an eigendirection decays at a rate set by its eigenvalue. For standard networks on coordinate inputs the large eigenvalues belong to smooth functions, so those directions converge first. Part XXIII's new movement supplies the kernel machinery this account depends on (the neural tangent kernel and its eigenvalue-governed convergence) though it is argued there for width and the lazy/rich distinction generally, not stated in terms of frequency.

Through the parameterisation. A high-frequency function needs large weights in the first layer to make the preactivation vary quickly across a small input range. Initialisation puts those weights near zero and gradient descent moves them slowly, so high frequencies are far from where optimisation starts.

The two accounts are the same fact from either side, and the second is the one that suggests the fix in §36.

§31 What follows for training

Four things this explains

Early stopping is a low-pass filter. Stop early and you keep the components fitted first, which are the smooth ones. Part VIII §13 and §14 showed early stopping behaving like a penalty on the weights; this says which functions the penalty prefers.

Learning curves are not one curve. The loss is a sum over components converging at different rates, which is why it plateaus and then drops again, a slower component finally arriving, not the optimiser escaping anything.

High-frequency targets need help. A coordinate network cannot represent fine detail in reasonable time without §36's remedy. Figure 7 measures it failing at f = 16 on a target of five terms.

Noise is high-frequency. Label noise is uncorrelated between neighbouring inputs, so it lives at the top of the spectrum, and spectral bias means the network fits it last. That is the mechanism behind Part XIII §9's finding that networks absorb noise late.

§32 What movement D establishes

Three things

Frequency is an eigenbasis, and every domain with a Laplacian has one. Graphs and meshes included, which is what Part XXVI §3's spectrum was.

Natural data is low-frequency and white noise is not. Figure 6 measures a power law over more than a decade against a flat spectrum, and that difference is what every smoothness prior in this book is betting on.

Gradient descent fits low frequencies first. Figure 7 measures 50 steps against never, and shows the ordering is set by frequency rather than by amplitude. Much of what this book calls implicit regularisation is this one fact.
