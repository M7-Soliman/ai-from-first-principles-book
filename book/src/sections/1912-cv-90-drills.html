PracticeDrills

Thirty-two problems. This part has more exact results than any other: the matrix equivalence, the separable ratio, the checkerboard condition and the complex-cell invariance are all things you can verify to machine precision rather than to a tolerance, and a large share of these ask you to.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · The cost of one conv layer

A 3×3 convolution mapping 256 channels to 256, applied to a 56×56 feature map. Write down the parameter count and the multiply–adds, before computing either.

Answer. 589,824 parameters and 1.85×109 multiply–adds. The ratio is the whole point of convolution: 3,136 operations per parameter, against one per parameter for a dense layer. Sharing does not save compute: it saves parameters, and spends them harder. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

WarmW1 · The count

A dense layer maps a $128\times128$ colour image to 64 feature maps of the same size. How many parameters, and how many would a $3\times3$ convolution need?

Answer. Dense: $(128^2\times3)\times(128^2\times64) = 5.15\times10^{10}$. Convolution: $64\times3\times3\times3 = 1{,}728$. Halving the image side divides the dense count by sixteen and leaves the convolution untouched.
WarmW2 · Output size

Input $32\times32$, kernel 5, stride 2, padding 2, dilation 1. What comes out?

Answer. $\lfloor(32 + 4 - 5)/2\rfloor + 1 = 16$. So $16\times16$, and note the floor discarded nothing here, which is luck rather than design.
WarmW3 · Two constraints

Name the two independent constraints convolution places on §4's matrix, and one layer type that has each without the other.

Answer. Sparsity: most entries zero, which a locally connected layer has without sharing. Repetition: the nonzeros take few distinct values, which a recurrent network has without sparsity.
WarmW4 · Which word

Shifting the input shifts the output identically. Is that invariance or equivariance, and which does pooling give?

Answer. Equivariance: convolution. Pooling gives approximate invariance, which is nearly the opposite: the output does not change rather than moving along. The measurements differ only in whether the reference is shifted to compensate.
WarmW5 · Reach

What is the theoretical receptive field of ten stacked $3\times3$ stride-1 layers, and roughly what fraction of it carries most of the gradient?

Answer. $1+2(10) = 21$ pixels. Figure 4 measured the effective field at about 60–90% of the theoretical at that depth, falling toward a third by depth 48, because it grows like $\sqrt{L}$ and the theoretical field grows like $L$.
WarmW6 · The separable ratio

Standard against depthwise separable, $256\to512$ with $k=3$. Give the ratio from the formula and then the two parameter counts.

Answer. $1/512 + 1/9 = 0.1131$. Standard $512\times256\times9 = 1{,}179{,}648$; separable $256\times9 + 256\times512 = 133{,}376$. Ratio 0.1131: exact, not approximate.
WarmW7 · A one-pixel kernel

What does a $1\times1$ convolution do, given that it cannot see any spatial structure?

Answer. It is a dense layer applied independently at every position, mixing channels and not space. That is why it appears as a channel reducer in Inception, the pointwise half of a separable convolution, and the head of a segmentation network.
WarmW8 · Checkerboard

Which of $(k,s) = (4,2)$, $(3,2)$, $(6,4)$, $(4,4)$ produce checkerboard artefacts?

Answer. $(3,2)$ and $(6,4)$. The condition is exactly $k \bmod s \neq 0$: $4\bmod 2 = 0$ and $4\bmod 4 = 0$ are clean, $3\bmod 2 = 1$ and $6\bmod 4 = 2$ are not.
WarmW9 · The right diagnosis

A 40-layer plain convolutional network without normalisation sits at chance on its training set. What does that rule out?

Answer. Overfitting, and capacity. The network can express the shallower network that works (set the extra layers to the identity) so the function is in the search space and the optimiser failed to find it. It is an optimisation failure, which is ResNet's own argument.
WarmW10 · The prior

State convolution as a prior over a dense layer's weights, in two clauses.

Answer. Each unit's weights must be identical to its neighbour's, shifted; and must be zero outside a small contiguous receptive field. Both are infinitely strong: zero probability elsewhere, regardless of the data.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Build the matrix

Construct $M$ explicitly for a $3\times3$ kernel on an $8\times8$ image and verify $M\mathbf{x}$ against a library convolution. Count the nonzeros and the distinct values.

Answer. Agreement to about $10^{-16}$; $36\times64$ with 324 nonzero of 2,304 and only 9 distinct values. Those two numbers are sparsity and sharing respectively, and separating them is the point of the exercise.
CoreC2 · The backward pass is a convolution

Show that $M^\top\mathbf{g}$ equals a full-padding convolution of $\mathbf{g}$ with the flipped kernel. Verify numerically.

Answer. Exactly zero difference in float64. Row $q$ of $M^\top$ collects every kernel weight that touched input pixel $q$, and reading them off in order reverses the kernel; the border pixels are reached by fewer positions, which is full padding. This is why a library that can convolve can already differentiate through convolution.
CoreC3 · Measure equivariance

Verify $f(g(\mathbf{x})) = g(f(\mathbf{x}))$ numerically for translations, then try a 90° rotation and a $2\times$ rescale. Exclude a border and say why you must.

Answer. Translation agrees to float32 rounding, around $10^{-7}$ relative. Rotation and scale come out near 0.9 of the activation range, meaning unrelated. The border must be excluded because a circular shift wraps content in from the far side; the margin needs to be at least the shift plus the receptive field, and getting this wrong makes translation equivariance appear to fail.
CoreC4 · Measure the receptive field

Take a stack of $3\times3$ layers, backpropagate from one central output unit, and look at the input gradient. Plot the nonzero support and the region holding 95% of the mass, against depth.

Answer. The support matches $1+2L$ exactly. The 95% region grows with a log-log slope near 0.5 ($\sqrt{L}$) and reaches about a third of the theoretical field by depth 48. Use a large enough canvas that the field never touches the edge, or you will measure the canvas.
CoreC5 · Invariance, correctly measured

Measure how much a representation changes when the input is translated, for convolution alone, max pooling at two window sizes, and global pooling. State the one detail that distinguishes this from C3.

Answer. Do not shift the reference. Convolution alone changes by 1.4–1.5 at every shift; max-pool-4 by 0.13 at one pixel rising to 0.36 by eight; global max pooling by 0.005. The graded shape of the pooling curve is the result: invariance has a range, set by the window.
CoreC6 · im2col

Implement convolution by gathering patches into a matrix and calling one matrix multiply. Compare against a scalar loop (one multiply-accumulate per iteration, no NumPy vectorisation) for correctness and for time, and measure the memory the patch matrix costs.

Answer. Identical to float32 rounding, thousands of times faster than a genuine scalar loop, and the buffer is $k^2$ times the input, measured at 8× for $k=3$ and 16–17× for $k=5$. The speedup against a scalar baseline is stable. Against a baseline that is itself vectorised (a spatial loop whose inner work is a numpy matmul), the im2col advantage shrinks or inverts depending on hardware and BLAS configuration, because the contiguous-copy cost can dominate when the matmul is already efficient. The copy is still the point (the gathered matrix must be contiguous before BLAS can use it), but the comparison must be honest about what the baseline is.
CoreC7 · Gradient check the whole layer

Write forward and backward for a strided, padded, multi-channel convolution and check both gradients against finite differences.

Answer. Agreement to about $10^{-6}$ with a central difference and a step near $10^{-5}$ in float64; Part III §3's cancellation sets the floor. The stride is where the errors are: the kernel gradient sums over output positions and the input gradient scatters back to strided locations, and it is easy to write one as if the other's indexing applied.
CoreC8 · The separable formula

Derive $1/C_{\text{out}} + 1/k^2$ and verify it against real layer definitions across several shapes.

Answer. $(C_{\text{in}}k^2 + C_{\text{in}}C_{\text{out}}) / (C_{\text{out}}C_{\text{in}}k^2)$, and cancel. Agreement is exact (0.00e+00) because it is an identity rather than an approximation. Then measure the wall-clock saving and find it is far smaller than $9\times$, which is the more useful discovery.
CoreC9 · Grouped convolution

Confirm that groups=g divides the parameter count by exactly $g$, and that groups=in_channels gives the depthwise operation of §19.

Answer. Exactly $g$. The two limits are worth naming: $g=1$ is the ordinary layer, $g=C_{\text{in}}$ is depthwise, and everything in between is a channel-mixing budget. AlexNet's grouping was two GPUs; ResNeXt's was a design decision.
CoreC10 · VGG's substitution

Show that two $3\times3$ layers match one $5\times5$ in receptive field with 28% fewer parameters, then say what is not equivalent about them.

Answer. $18C^2$ against $25C^2$, same 5-pixel reach, and one extra nonlinearity. Not equivalent: the stack weights the centre of that region far more heavily than the corners, because corner pixels are reachable by one path and central ones by many. Same support, different operator.
CoreC11 · Depth and degradation

Train plain and residual networks at increasing depth, with and without normalisation, and record the best training loss.

Answer. Without normalisation the plain network degrades to chance ($\ln C$ for $C$ classes) and the residual one diverges past a certain depth, because each block adds to the signal and the variance grows. With normalisation both train. Report all four; the popular account has only one of them.
CoreC12 · Checkerboards, exactly

Apply a transposed convolution with a constant kernel to a constant input, for several $(k,s)$. Count the distinct values in the interior.

Answer. One when $k \bmod s = 0$, three otherwise. Using a constant input and kernel is what makes it exact: any non-uniformity in the output is the operator's, not the data's.
CoreC13 · Dilation and gridding

Build a stack with dilations $2,2,2,2$ and one with $1,2,5,9$, and compare their gradient receptive fields.

Answer. The constant-dilation stack shows a lattice: each output depends only on inputs congruent modulo 2, so the sublattices never mix. Coprime dilations fill the field. This is the reason published schedules are not powers of two, and it is invisible unless you look at the field itself.
CoreC14 · The complex cell

Build a Gabor quadrature pair from §31's formula and confirm the pair is orthogonal, then measure how $s_0$, $s_1$ and $\sqrt{s_0^2+s_1^2}$ respond as a grating translates along the preferred direction.

Answer. Correlation about $10^{-17}$. Each simple cell sweeps through 199.8% of its peak; the complex cell varies by 0.00%. Negating the image flips the simple cells by 200% and leaves the complex cell unchanged. Detector-then-pool, derived from V1 and verified exactly.
CoreC15 · What the network actually learns

Train a small network on a task whose structure you control, look at the first-layer kernels, and fit Gabor functions to them.

Answer. On synthetic oriented bars the filters are clean bar detectors and the Gabor fits are poor, median $R^2$ about 0.18. That is the correct outcome: Gabors are a property of natural image statistics, not of convolution, and a synthetic task with different statistics gives different filters. Many algorithms find Gabors on natural images, which is why finding them tells you little about the algorithm.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · Sparsity against sharing

Implement a locally connected layer and compare it against a convolution with the same connectivity, on the same task, at several training-set sizes.

Answer. The convolution wins, and the margin widens as data shrinks, which is §11's prior priced empirically. With enough data the locally connected layer can learn the same detector at every position; the shared one never had to. Report the crossover if you find one.
StretchS2 · Break the prior deliberately

Permute the pixels of every image with one fixed permutation and retrain. Compare a convolutional network against a dense one.

Answer. The dense network is essentially unaffected; it never knew which pixels were adjacent. The convolutional one collapses. That is what §11 means by an infinitely strong prior, and it is why the two model families are not comparable on the same benchmark without saying so.
StretchS3 · Buy an invariance three ways

On a task with rotated inputs, compare rotation augmentation, pooling across rotated copies of each filter, and doing nothing. Report accuracy, parameters and wall-clock.

Answer. All three columns matter. Augmentation is free in parameters and costs training time; filter-copy pooling costs compute at every forward pass, including at deployment; doing nothing costs accuracy. Which wins depends on how much data you have, and the exercise is to find where the crossover is rather than to pick a winner.
StretchS4 · The ConvNeXt ablation

Take a plain ResNet and add, one at a time, the modern training recipe (AdamW, cosine schedule with warmup, heavy augmentation, label smoothing), then the architectural changes. Attribute the total gain across them.

Answer. A large share is the training recipe rather than the architecture, which is the published result and worth reproducing at small scale because it changes how you read every architecture paper. Note that the ordering matters: gains are not additive, and reporting only the total is how the attribution went wrong for a subfield.
StretchS5 · Localise something

Build an encoder–decoder that predicts a coordinate to within a few pixels. Compare a transposed-convolution decoder against upsample-then-convolve, and look at the outputs.

Answer. With a badly chosen $(k,s)$ the transposed decoder shows the lattice of Figure 9 in its predictions, not merely in its images. Upsample-then-convolve cannot produce it. Also measure what happens when you add skip connections from the encoder: the fine spatial information was destroyed on the way down and cannot be recovered from the coarse representation, so the skips are not an optimisation trick here but a requirement.
StretchS6 · Where the time goes

Profile a depthwise separable network against its standard equivalent. Compare the FLOP ratio, the parameter ratio and the wall-clock ratio.

Answer. Parameters and FLOPs both fall by about $9\times$; latency by far less, and on some hardware not at all. Depthwise convolution is memory-bound where a standard convolution is compute-bound, so it does not use the arithmetic units it saved. The general lesson is that a paper reporting only FLOPs is reporting the number that flattered it.
