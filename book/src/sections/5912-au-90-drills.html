PracticeDrills

Forty-three drills. The harness in parts/30-audio/drills/ covers twenty of them with twenty-six tests. Numpy only, and every signal synthetic, which is what lets the reconstruction tests assert machine precision rather than a tolerance.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · The cost of a second of audio

One second of 16 kHz mono speech. Before computing anything, write down: the number of samples; the number of frames at a 25 ms window and 10 ms hop; the size of an 80-band mel spectrogram; and how many tokens a neural codec at 75 Hz with eight quantiser stages produces. Then write down the compression factor from the first to the last.

Answer. 16,000 samples, ~98 frames, 80×98 = 7,840 mel values, and 600 tokens. From 16,000 numbers to 600 symbols is about 27× in count and far more in bits, which is the whole reason a language model can be trained on audio at all. Most people underestimate how few tokens a second of speech becomes. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

Warm-upW1 · Why not the waveform

Give three reasons audio models rarely read raw samples.

Answer. Length; phase variation that changes the samples enormously and the sound not at all; and the fact that the structure (pitch, formants, timbre) is a property of the spectrum, so a model reading samples must discover the transform first.
Warm-upW2 · Read Figure 1

How many FFT bins does the lowest mel band pool, and the highest?

Answer. 3 and 42, a factor of 14. A 200 Hz separation spans 6.3 mel bands at the bottom of the range and 0.61 at the top, falling inside a single band above about 4,560 Hz.
Warm-upW3 · What the mel scale asserts

State the mel scale as a modelling assumption rather than a preprocessing step.

Answer. That frequency resolution should degrade with frequency the way human hearing's does. Right for speech intended for people; wrong for machine fault detection, animal vocalisation, sonar and radio, where Figure 1's pooling is discarding the signal.
Warm-upW4 · What a spectrogram deletes

Name the quantity a mel spectrogram discards entirely, and what Part XXVII measured about it.

Answer. Phase. Part XXVII Figure 4 measured a reconstruction correlating +0.736 with its phase donor and −0.125 with its magnitude donor. Deleting it is why a vocoder exists.
Warm-upW5 · Read Figure 2

What does iterative phase estimation converge to, in each of the two spaces?

Answer. Spectrogram error falls monotonically from 0.666 to 0.048; waveform correlation dips and then rises to 0.53. It converges in spectrogram space and only incidentally in waveform space, which is what a metallic-sounding reconstruction is.
Warm-upW6 · The alignment problem

Why is speech recognition not ordinary sequence classification?

Answer. A hundred frames map to a dozen characters, monotonically, with no annotation of where each begins. The alignment is a latent variable and the answer is to sum over every one, Part XXVIII §12's recursion.
Warm-upW7 · Why CTC needs a blank

What breaks without it?

Answer. Repeated symbols. Consecutive identical frames collapse, so "aa" is unreachable without a blank between them, which is also why Figure 4's leaked mass lands on "a" and "b" rather than "aa" and "bb".
Warm-upW8 · Read Figure 4

The audio is equally consistent with two readings. What is the best a CTC model can do?

Answer. 0.25 on each: half the mass leaks onto outputs the target never contains, at every input length. A property of the factorisation, not of the fit, so sharper per-frame predictions do not help.
Warm-upW9 · What a language model is doing there

Why does a CTC recogniser need an external language model?

Answer. It is restoring the term the factorisation deleted, not refining a good estimate. Which is why a transducer, which keeps that term, needs one less.
Warm-upW10 · The repeating syllable

An autoregressive speech synthesiser sometimes repeats a syllable forever. What removes it?

Answer. Predicting duration explicitly, one number per input token, and expanding deterministically. Nothing in autoregression forces the alignment to advance; an explicit duration does, and it makes speaking rate a control.
Warm-upW11 · Read Figure 5

At zero correlation, how much better is a vector quantiser, and why is it better at all?

Answer. 1.51×. The remaining gain is geometric: a hypercube is a poor cell because its corners are far from its centre, and no amount of decorrelation removes that.
Warm-upW12 · Read Figure 6

How many of 512 negatives carry the gradient at a temperature of 0.01?

Answer. 1.1, with the hardest one taking 98.5% of the weight. The temperature is not a scale on the logits so much as a choice of how many negatives you are training against.
Warm-upW13 · Read Figure 7

What does a recall@1 figure without a gallery size tell you?

Answer. Nothing comparable. The same embedding scores 0.49 or 0.07 depending only on how many items it was searched against.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Build a mel filterbank

Implement the hertz-to-mel map and the triangular filters, and check the band widths.

Answer. Reproduce Figure 1's 3-to-42 pooling. Then check the filterbank sums to roughly one per FFT bin in the interior: if it does not, your energy is being rescaled with frequency and every downstream statistic is tilted.
CoreC2 · Two tones, one band

Find the frequency above which a fixed separation falls inside a single mel band.

Answer. About 4,560 Hz for a 200 Hz separation in the standard configuration. Measure it as bands spanned rather than by counting spectral peaks: peak counting is not monotone in frequency, because two wide adjacent bands can still form two local maxima, so it reports a crossover that is an artefact of the criterion.
CoreC3 · Write the short-time transform and its inverse

Implement both and verify the round trip.

Answer. Exact to machine precision on the interior. The first and last window have fewer overlapping frames, so their error is boundary handling; measure there and a correct implementation looks broken.
CoreC4 · Randomise the phase

Keep the magnitude, replace the phase, and listen, or measure.

Answer. Identical spectrogram, correlation −0.024 with the original. Which is the audio version of Part XXVII Figure 4 and the reason a magnitude-only loss cannot hear what it is doing.
CoreC5 · Iterative phase estimation

Implement it and track both the spectrogram error and the waveform correlation.

Answer. The first falls monotonically; the second dips before it rises and stops well short of one. Tracking only the first is how you conclude it works.
CoreC6 · The CTC collapse rule

Implement it and enumerate every frame sequence that maps to a given target.

Answer. Delete repeats, then delete blanks. Enumerating is how you discover that "aa" needs a blank in the middle and "aa" from two frames is unreachable, which most descriptions state and few make concrete.
CoreC7 · The forward recursion

Implement it and check against enumeration.

Answer. About 10−15. The extended target with blanks interleaved is where the indexing goes wrong, and the enumeration check is the only thing that catches it: a transposed recursion still produces a plausible probability.
CoreC8 · Count the alignments

Count them by the same recursion with all probabilities set to one, and verify against enumeration.

Answer. 330 at T = 9 for a two-symbol target; 1.7×106 at T = 80, where enumeration is impossible. Counting by recursion is how the right panel of Figure 3 extends past the left.
CoreC9 · The leak, measured

Find the CTC distribution closest to an equal mixture of two sequences.

Answer. 0.25 on each, half the mass elsewhere, at every length. Optimise the per-frame logits directly rather than training a network, so the result is about the family and not about the fit.
CoreC10 · Beam search with a language model

Decode a CTC output with and without an external model.

Answer. The gap is large, and it is largest exactly on the ambiguous inputs Figure 4 describes. Note that the weight on the language model is a hyperparameter that trades word error rate against hallucinated fluency.
CoreC11 · Explicit duration

Expand a token sequence by predicted durations and compare against an autoregressive alignment.

Answer. The failure mode disappears and speaking rate becomes a multiplier you can set. The cost is that durations need supervision, usually from a forced alignment.
CoreC12 · Scalar against vector quantisation

Implement both and compare at matched bitrate across correlations.

Answer. Figure 5's numbers. The part to check carefully is zero correlation, where the vector quantiser still wins by half again: if yours does not, your bit accounting is wrong.
CoreC13 · Residual quantisation

Stack small codebooks, each encoding the previous stage's residual.

Answer. Within about 1.5× of full vector quantisation with a codebook of 16 rather than 256. This is how the exponential codebook is avoided, and it is what a neural codec's stages are.
CoreC14 · Codebook collapse

Train a vector quantiser and count how many entries are ever used.

Answer. Often far fewer than the codebook size, because unused entries receive no gradient and stay where they were initialised. Re-initialising dead entries from the data is the standard fix, and the count is the diagnostic nobody prints.
CoreC15 · The contrastive loss

Implement it symmetrically and check the gradient is zero when the matched pair is already nearest by a margin.

Answer. It is not zero: the softmax always pushes. That is different from a hinge loss and it is why these systems keep training long after retrieval is perfect.
CoreC16 · Effective negatives

Compute the perplexity of the softmax weights across temperature.

Answer. Figure 6's curve. Then check the batch size at which extra negatives stop changing it: below some temperature, a larger batch buys nothing.
CoreC17 · False negatives

Construct a batch containing two genuinely matching pairs and see what the loss does.

Answer. Pushes them apart, because the loss cannot distinguish a true negative from an unlabelled positive. On captioned data this is common and it is why very large batches have a ceiling.
CoreC18 · Recall against gallery size

Sweep the gallery and fit the slope per decade.

Answer. Roughly linear in the logarithm, at about −0.11 and −0.17 per decade for the two embeddings in Figure 7. Report the gallery size with the number, always.
CoreC19 · Prompt sensitivity

Score a zero-shot classifier with several wordings of the same class set.

Answer. Several points of spread from wording alone. A zero-shot number without its prompts is not reproducible, which is Part XX §22 in another modality.
CoreC20 · Within against across

Measure mean similarity within each modality and across, on a trained shared space.

Answer. Compare the two before trusting any cross-modal threshold. If they differ, a score means different things in the two directions and a threshold tuned on one does not transfer.
CoreC21 · The text-only control

Run a multimodal benchmark with the image removed.

Answer. Startlingly high on most of them. One line of work, rarely present, and it bounds how much of the reported score is about the other modality at all.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · A recogniser, end to end

Build a small CTC recogniser on synthetic audio with known transcripts.

Answer. Synthetic means you know the alignment, so you can check what the model learned against what was true, which is unavailable on real speech and is most of the value.
StretchS2 · A transducer

Add a prediction network and compare against CTC on ambiguous inputs.

Answer. The leak of Figure 4 shrinks, because the output is no longer conditionally independent. Measure it on the same constructed ambiguity rather than on word error rate, where it is invisible.
StretchS3 · A neural codec

Build an autoencoder with a residual vector quantiser and measure rate against distortion.

Answer. Report the used-codebook count alongside the rate; a system with half its entries dead is running at a lower rate than it claims.
StretchS4 · A language model on audio tokens

Train a small autoregressive model over codec indices.

Answer. It works with no audio-specific machinery, which is the point of §22. Spend capacity on the first quantiser stage: the later stages are detail and are cheaper to predict.
StretchS5 · A learned front end

Train a filterbank from raw samples and look at what it converges to.

Answer. Bandpass filters at roughly logarithmic spacing, the mel scale, rediscovered. Then find a task where it beats mel, and check it is one where §4's assumption is wrong.
StretchS6 · Two towers, and the geometry

Train a contrastive pair on synthetic paired data and measure the embedding geometry throughout.

Answer. Retrieval works long before the geometry is tidy, which is why the arrangement of the space went unnoticed. Measure within-modality and across-modality similarity separately at every checkpoint.
StretchS7 · Temporal pairs

Train on clip-level pairs, then evaluate on a task that needs timing.

Answer. It fails, and clip-level retrieval cannot detect that it fails. If timing matters, the pairs and the metric both have to be temporal.
StretchS8 · Audit an audio system you run

Check the resampling, the split, the loss and the evaluation.

Answer. The most common findings are a resample with no anti-aliasing filter and a split that shares speakers between train and test. Both are one line and both invalidate the number above them.
