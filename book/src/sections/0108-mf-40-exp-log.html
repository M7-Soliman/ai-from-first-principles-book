Part DExponentials and logarithms

If you skip one part of this document, do not let it be this one. Logarithms appear in every loss function in machine learning, and exponentials build every activation and every probability distribution you will meet.

§9 Exponentials and the number $e$

Ahead: $e^x$ builds the sigmoid (§18), the softmax (Part II §20) and the Gaussian (Part II §15). Three of the most-used objects in the field, one function.

An exponential function has the variable in the exponent:

$$f(x) = 2^x, \qquad f(x) = e^x, \qquad f(x) = 10^x$$

That is a different species from $x^2$, where the variable is in the base. The distinction is worth stating aloud because the two are easy to conflate and behave completely differently.

What makes exponential growth different

A polynomial adds a fixed amount of "shape" as $x$ grows. An exponential multiplies by a fixed factor for every unit step. Each step of $2^x$ doubles the previous value, forever.

That distinction sounds mild and is not, because human intuition for growth is additive and simply does not extend. Everyone can extrapolate a savings account that gains ten pounds a month. Almost nobody can extrapolate one that doubles, and the failure is not a lack of arithmetic; it is that the answer feels wrong even after you have computed it.

Take a sheet of paper and fold it in half. Fold it again, and again. After ten folds you have a thick little block, about the height of a paperback. Guess how tall it is after fifty folds. Whatever number came to mind is too small by an enormous margin: fifty folds of ordinary paper stands about $1.1 \times 10^{11}$ metres tall, which is three quarters of the way from here to the sun. Nothing about that calculation is subtle; it is $0.1\,\text{mm} \times 2^{50}$, and you can check it on a phone, but the answer is unbelievable anyway, and it stays unbelievable after you have checked it. That gap between the arithmetic and the intuition is the thing to take from this section.

The practical version of the same fact is what Figure 2 shows. Compare $x^3$ and $e^x$ at $x = 5$: the cube is 125, the exponential about 148, close enough to argue about. At $x = 20$ the cube is 8,000 and the exponential is around 485 million. At $x = 100$ the cube is a million and the exponential has forty-four digits. The exponential does not win by being bigger at the start. It wins because it is gaining faster, and it keeps being true forever, against any polynomial you care to name. Whenever you see a paper claim an algorithm is exponential in some quantity, this is what is being asserted: not that it is slow now, but that no improvement in hardware will ever matter.

The number $e$

History: $e$ was found in a bank, not a laboratory

In 1683 Jacob Bernoulli was working on compound interest. If a bank pays 100% annual interest compounded once, a unit of money becomes 2. Compound it twice a year and you get 2.25. Monthly: about 2.61. Daily: about 2.71.

Bernoulli asked what happens if you compound continuously, infinitely often, infinitesimally. The answer does not run away to infinity. It converges to a specific number a little over 2.718, and he could show it was between 2 and 3 without being able to say what it was.

Euler pinned it down in the 1720s, computed it to eighteen decimal places, proved it irrational, and gave it the letter $e$, probably just because it was the next vowel available after $a$, though the coincidence with his own name has been noticed by everybody since.

The relevant point for this book: $e$ is what shows up when growth is proportional to current size. That is why it governs interest, populations, decay, and why it is the natural base for anything about rates.

$e \approx 2.71828\ldots$, an irrational number like $\pi$. Every treatment of it raises the same reasonable objection: why this ugly number rather than a clean base like 2 or 10?

The answer is a single property, and it is the reason $e$ is everywhere in calculus:

$$\frac{d}{dx} e^x = e^x$$

Before the intuition box makes that precise, it is worth sitting with how strange the statement is. It says the function's steepness, at every point, equals the function's height at that point. Where the curve is at height 5, it is rising at a rate of 5. Where it is at height 1000, it is rising at 1000. The curve is chasing itself: the higher it gets, the faster it climbs, and the faster it climbs the higher it gets. No other function does this except multiples of this one, and that uniqueness is not a curiosity; it is essentially the definition of $e$.

Where it comes from is easier to see if you think about growth rather than curves. A population where every individual reproduces at the same per-capita rate grows faster when there are more individuals, in exact proportion to how many there are. Money at compound interest earns more when there is more of it, in exact proportion. Both are the sentence "the rate of increase equals the current amount, times a constant"; and the function satisfying that sentence is $e^{kt}$. Bernoulli found the number in a bank because banks are where that sentence was first written down carefully, but the number was never really about banking.

Intuition: $e^x$ is its own derivative

$e^x$ is the unique function whose rate of change equals its current value. At any point on the curve, the steepness is exactly the height.

This is not an aesthetic nicety; it makes $e$ the natural base for anything involving rates. Growth that is proportional to current size, which describes compound interest, population growth, radioactive decay and a great deal else, is described by $e$ with no correction factors. Other bases work but drag a constant along: $\frac{d}{dx}2^x = 2^x \ln 2$. That stray $\ln 2$ is the price of not using $e$, and $e$ is defined precisely to make it disappear.

The rules from §4 apply unchanged, and they are worth restating in exponential form:

$$e^a \cdot e^b = e^{a+b}, \qquad \frac{e^a}{e^b} = e^{a-b}, \qquad (e^a)^b = e^{ab}, \qquad e^0 = 1$$

Two facts to internalise now, because they are load-bearing later:

  • $e^x$ is always strictly positive, for every real $x$. Never zero, never negative. This is exactly why softmax works: exponentiating anything gives you a legal probability weight.
  • $e^{-x} = 1/e^x$, so a negative exponent means "small". As $x$ grows, $e^{-x}$ collapses toward zero very fast.

§10 Logarithms

In 60 seconds

A logarithm is an exponent, retrieved: $\log_b(y)$ answers "what power of $b$ gives $y$?" Its defining property is that it turns multiplication into addition, which is why every likelihood in machine learning gets a log wrapped around it, and why the notation survives four centuries after the arithmetic it was invented to save.

History: invented to spare astronomers their lives

In 1614 John Napier, a Scottish laird with an interest in mathematics and a reputation for eccentricity, published a table that changed professional computation.

The problem he was solving was brutally practical. Astronomy and navigation required multiplying six- and seven-digit numbers, by hand, thousands of times. A single multiplication took minutes and could go wrong silently. Napier's insight was that if you tabulate numbers by their exponents, multiplication becomes addition, and addition is fast, and its errors are obvious.

He coined the name from Greek: logos (ratio) and arithmos (number). Henry Briggs visited him in 1615, suggested basing the tables on powers of ten, and spent years computing them.

The effect was immediate and enormous. Laplace later wrote that logarithms, by shortening the labours, doubled the life of the astronomer, and spared him the errors and distaste of long calculations.

Four hundred years on, §11 will show that machine learning takes logarithms of its likelihoods for a version of the same reason: products are hostile to computation, and sums are not. The hardware changed. The trick did not.

Definition: logarithm

$\log_b(y)$ answers the question: what power of $b$ gives $y$?

$$\log_b(y) = x \quad \text{means exactly} \quad b^x = y$$

That is the whole idea. A logarithm is an exponent, retrieved. $\log_2(8) = 3$ because $2^3 = 8$. $\log_{10}(1000) = 3$ because $10^3 = 1000$.

Logarithms have a reputation for difficulty that they do not deserve, and the reason is worth naming: the question is phrased backwards from the direction people find natural. You are comfortable being handed 2 and 3 and asked for $2^3$: that is a computation, and you can just do it. A logarithm hands you 2 and 8 and asks what the exponent was. Same three numbers, same relationship, different unknown. It feels harder in exactly the way that "what times 7 gives 56" feels harder than "what is 7 times 8", which is to say not harder at all once you notice that it is the same fact being read from the other end.

The intuition that makes logs feel like something rather than a rule is this: a logarithm measures how many digits a number has. Not exactly, but close enough to be useful and exact enough to be right. $\log_{10}$ of 1000 is 3, of 10,000 is 4, of a million is 6, and each of those is one less than the digit count, because each new digit means another multiplication by ten. So when someone says a quantity is "five orders of magnitude larger", they are saying its log is bigger by five, and they are describing a factor of 100,000 with a single small number.

That is the real service logarithms perform, and it is not primarily algebraic. They take a range of numbers that is unmanageably wide and compress it into one you can hold in your head and draw on a page. Sound intensities across the audible range span about thirteen factors of ten, which is why we speak in decibels. Earthquake energies span a similar spread, which is why we speak of magnitudes. Model losses in this book will run from thousands down to $10^{-8}$ in a single training run, which is why every loss curve you will ever see is plotted on a log axis. A logarithm is a change of unit from "how many" to "how many times bigger", and the second unit is very often the one that matches what you actually want to know.

So logs and exponentials are inverse functions, in exactly the §7 sense:

$$\ln(e^x) = x, \qquad e^{\ln x} = x \;\; (x > 0)$$

Which base?

  • $\ln$: natural log, base $e$. The default in mathematics and machine learning. When a paper writes $\log$ with no base, assume $\ln$.
  • $\log_2$: base 2. Used in information theory, where the unit is bits.
  • $\log_{10}$: base 10. Used in engineering and for plot axes.

They differ only by a constant multiple, so switching base never changes where a function is minimised, which is why nobody frets about it during optimisation.

The three rules (and they are the whole point)

$$\boxed{\;\log(ab) = \log a + \log b\;}$$ $$\log\!\left(\frac{a}{b}\right) = \log a - \log b, \qquad \log(a^n) = n \log a$$

The first rule is the important one, and it is worth understanding rather than memorising. Logs are exponents; multiplying powers adds exponents; therefore taking the log of a product adds the logs. The rule is inherited directly from $e^a \cdot e^b = e^{a+b}$.

Walk it through once with numbers and it stops being a rule at all. Take $100 \times 1000$. In log terms that is $10^2 \times 10^3$, and multiplying powers of ten just piles up the zeros: two zeros followed by three more is five, giving $10^5$. The exponents added ($2 + 3 = 5$), not because of a theorem but because that is what multiplying does to counts of factors. Now write the same sentence in log notation and you have $\log(100 \times 1000) = \log 100 + \log 1000$. Nothing was proved. The rule is the observation, restated.

Which also explains why the three rules are the ones they are, and why there is no fourth. Exponents have exactly three behaviours (they add when you multiply, subtract when you divide, and scale when you raise to a power), so a logarithm, being an exponent, inherits exactly those three and nothing else. If you ever find yourself trying to remember whether some log identity exists, ask what it would say about exponents. If the exponent version is not one of those three, the log version does not exist either.

Intuition: logarithms turn multiplication into addition

That single sentence is why logarithms exist, why they were invented in the 1600s to make astronomical calculation tractable, and why every likelihood in machine learning is wrapped in one.

Multiplication is hard: it grows numbers explosively and destroys numerical precision. Addition is easy. A logarithm is a translator between the two.

Pitfall: there is no rule for $\log(a + b)$

$\log(a+b)$ does not simplify. Not to $\log a + \log b$, not to anything. The rules are about products, quotients, and powers; never sums.

Test it: $\log_{10}(10 + 90) = \log_{10}(100) = 2$, while $\log_{10}(10) + \log_{10}(90) \approx 1 + 1.95 = 2.95$. Not equal.

This is why the "log-sum-exp" trick from Part II needs a whole named technique: the log of a sum genuinely is awkward, and there is no algebraic escape.

§11 Why machine learning lives in log space

In 60 seconds

Likelihoods are products over every training example, and products of probabilities underflow to exactly zero within a few dozen terms. Taking a log turns the product into a sum, which computers handle without complaint. It is legal because $\log$ is monotonic, so the location of the maximum does not move, and it makes derivatives simpler as a bonus. This is why you see log-likelihood, log-softmax and cross-entropy everywhere.

Now the payoff, and it justifies the whole part.

Here is the situation this section resolves. You have a model and a dataset, and you want a single number saying how well the model explains the data. The natural construction is to ask what probability the model assigns to everything you observed, all of it, at once. If the examples are independent, the probability of seeing all of them is the product of the individual probabilities (the same rule that makes the chance of three heads in a row $\tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 \times \tfrac12$). That product is called the likelihood, and maximising it is a completely reasonable definition of "fit the model to the data".

It is also, taken literally, unrunnable. Everything below is the story of why, and of the one-line fix that the entire field uses without comment.

Reason 1: products underflow, sums do not

A model's likelihood over a dataset is a product over every example:

$$L = \prod_{i=1}^{m} p(x^{(i)})$$

Every factor is a probability, hence at most 1. Multiply ten thousand of them and the result is astronomically small, far below what a computer can represent, so it becomes exactly zero and all information is destroyed.

It is worth appreciating how fast this happens, because "astronomically small" invites you to imagine some remote edge case. Suppose your model is doing well and assigns each example a probability of $0.9$ (better than most real models manage). Multiply $0.9$ by itself a thousand times and you get about $10^{-46}$. Ten thousand times and you get $10^{-458}$, which a 64-bit float cannot represent at all; it stores exactly zero. Your excellent model and a catastrophically bad one now both score zero, and no comparison between them is possible. This is not a rare failure at the boundary. It is the ordinary outcome on any dataset worth training on.

And the failure is silent, which is worse. Nothing raises an exception. There is no warning. The number simply becomes zero, the optimiser sees a flat landscape in every direction, and training does nothing while appearing to run. A great deal of this book's attention to numerical detail traces back to failures with exactly that shape: arithmetic that ran, produced a number, and was believed.

Applying $\log$ converts that product into a sum, by the first rule of §10:

$$\log L = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log p(x^{(i)})$$

A sum of ten thousand moderate negative numbers is entirely unremarkable to a computer. This is the single reason every likelihood in machine learning appears as a log-likelihood. It is not theoretical elegance; it is that the alternative does not run.

Reason 2: the maximum does not move

Taking a log is legitimate only because $\log$ is monotonically increasing: bigger input, bigger output, always. So whatever maximises $L$ also maximises $\log L$. The location of the optimum is untouched; only the numbers are made manageable.

Intuition: why monotonic transformations are free

If you rank a set of numbers and then apply any strictly increasing function to all of them, the ranking is unchanged. Since optimisation only cares which input is best (the $\arg\min$ of §2, not the $\min$), you may apply any monotonic transform that makes the arithmetic easier.

This is the same licence that lets people minimise squared error instead of error, and it is used constantly and usually without comment.

Reason 3: derivatives get simpler

Differentiating a product of $m$ terms requires the product rule $m$ times and produces an unmanageable expression. Differentiating a sum means differentiating each term independently, since the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives. Since training is repeated differentiation, this alone would justify the log.

Why deep learning cares: where you will actually see this

Every one of these is the same idea:

  • Cross-entropy loss is $-\log$ of the probability assigned to the correct answer.
  • Log-likelihood is what maximum likelihood estimation maximises.
  • Log-softmax is computed directly rather than as $\log$ of softmax, for stability.
  • Log-sum-exp is the standard trick for summing in log space without leaving it.
  • Perplexity, the standard language model metric, is an exponentiated average log-probability.

When Part II §26 derives cross-entropy loss from maximum likelihood, the very first move is taking a log of a product. That step is this section.

Try it

(a) Simplify $\ln(e^{3x})$.   (b) Simplify $\ln\!\left(\frac{a^2 b}{c}\right)$ into separate logs.   (c) A model assigns probabilities $0.1$, $0.2$, $0.5$ to three independent examples. Compute the likelihood and the log-likelihood.   (d) Solve $e^{2x} = 7$ for $x$.

Answers. (a) $3x$: the log and the exponential cancel outright.

(b) $2\ln a + \ln b - \ln c$. Product becomes sum, quotient becomes difference, the power comes down as a coefficient.

(c) Likelihood $= 0.1 \times 0.2 \times 0.5 = 0.01$. Log-likelihood $= \ln(0.1) + \ln(0.2) + \ln(0.5) \approx -2.303 - 1.609 - 0.693 = -4.605$. Confirm: $\ln(0.01) \approx -4.605$. ✓ Both routes agree, but with ten thousand examples only the second one survives.

(d) Take $\ln$ of both sides: $2x = \ln 7$, so $x = \frac{\ln 7}{2} \approx 0.973$.
