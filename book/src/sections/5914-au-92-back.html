BuildThe project

Twelve to sixteen hours. You build the audio pipeline from the transform upward, measure what each stage discards, and then join it to a second modality, finding out where the representation stops being able to express what you want.

P.1 What you are building

hearsay: a short-time transform with an exact inverse, a mel filterbank, phase estimation, a CTC implementation checked against enumeration, a residual vector quantiser, and a two-tower contrastive trainer with the diagnostics this part says it needs.

The rule: numpy only, and every signal synthetic. Synthetic means you know the true alignment, the true phase and the true pairing, none of which you have on real audio, and all of which are what make the checks possible.

P.2 Stage 1: the representation

  1. Implement the short-time transform and its inverse. Verify the round trip on the interior, then look at what the boundary does and say why.
  2. Build a mel filterbank. Check the bands sum to about one per bin.
  3. Measure how many FFT bins each band pools, and how many bands a fixed hertz separation spans across the range.
  4. Sweep the band count and watch the crossover move.

Deliverable: Figure 1 reproduced, plus your own band count. Step three is the one to measure as bands spanned rather than by counting peaks: peak counting is not monotone, and it will tell you the tones resolve again at high frequency.

P.3 Stage 2: phase

  1. Reconstruct with the true phase and confirm it is exact.
  2. Reconstruct with random phase. Confirm the magnitude spectrogram is identical.
  3. Implement iterative phase estimation and track both the spectrogram error and the waveform error at every iteration.
  4. Plot them together.

Deliverable: the two curves on one axis. Tracking only the first is how you conclude the method works; the gap between them is what a vocoder is for.

P.4 Stage 3: alignment

  1. Implement the collapse rule and enumerate every frame sequence mapping to a target.
  2. Implement the forward recursion and check it against that enumeration.
  3. Count alignments by the same recursion with probabilities set to one, verify against enumeration, then extend far past where enumeration is possible.
  4. Optimise the per-frame logits toward an equal mixture of two sequences and record the best achievable split.
  5. Then optimise toward putting everything on one of them, and record that too.

Deliverable: the exactness check, the count curve, and the two optimisation results side by side. Step five is what makes step four a statement about the factorisation rather than about your optimiser.

P.5 Stage 4: quantisation

  1. Implement k-means and scalar quantisation at matched bitrate.
  2. Compare them across correlations, including zero.
  3. Implement residual quantisation and compare against a full codebook.
  4. Count how many codebook entries are ever used.

Deliverable: the rate–distortion table with the zero-correlation row, and the usage count. If your vector quantiser does not beat scalar at zero correlation, your bit accounting is wrong.

P.6 Stage 5: two towers

  1. Write down your predictions first, in a file, before running anything.
  2. Implement the symmetrised contrastive loss.
  3. Compute the effective number of negatives across temperature.
  4. Find the batch size at which extra negatives stop changing it, at two temperatures.
  5. Construct a batch with a duplicated pair and observe what the loss does to it.
  6. Sweep gallery size and fit the recall slope per decade.
  7. Measure within-modality, across-modality and matched-pair similarity at every checkpoint.
  8. Score your predictions.

Deliverable: the temperature curve, the recall sweep with its slope, the three similarity traces, and the scored predictions. Step seven is the one whose result you should not assume before running it.

P.7 Stage 6: the evaluation

  1. Split your data by speaker and at random, and compare.
  2. Resample a signal with and without an anti-aliasing filter and measure the spectrum.
  3. Score a reconstruction by spectral distance and by waveform error, and find a case where they disagree.
  4. Report every retrieval number with its gallery size.

Deliverable: the two split results and the disagreeing pair of metrics. Step three is the one that explains why a magnitude-only loss can be optimised to a good number and a bad result.

P.8 Stretch

  • A CTC recogniser on synthetic audio with known alignments.
  • A transducer, compared against it on constructed ambiguity rather than on error rate.
  • A neural codec, with the used-codebook count reported beside the rate.
  • A language model over codec tokens.
  • A learned front end, with the filters it converges to plotted.
  • An audit of an audio system you actually run.

P.9 Done when

  • Your round trip is exact on the interior and you can explain the boundary.
  • Your filterbank sums to about one per bin.
  • You measured band separation in bands, not by counting peaks.
  • You have the spectrogram error and the waveform error on one plot.
  • Your forward recursion matches enumeration to machine precision.
  • You counted alignments beyond where enumeration can reach.
  • You have both CTC optimisation results, the even split and the confident one.
  • Your vector quantiser beats scalar at zero correlation.
  • You know how many of your codebook entries are dead.
  • You know the effective negative count at your temperature.
  • You have found the batch size at which more negatives stop helping.
  • Every retrieval number you report has a gallery size beside it.
  • You have the three similarity traces and have looked at them.
  • You have a speaker-disjoint split and a random one, and the gap between them.
  • You wrote predictions down before Stage 5 and scored them afterwards.
Why this project

Audio is the modality where the standard pipeline is most obviously a set of decisions and least often examined as one. Building it makes the decisions visible: a window length that is a guess about speech, a scale that is a claim about hearing, and a deletion that requires an entire second model to undo.

Stage 4 step five is the one to keep. Showing that CTC can put everything on one sequence is what turns "it cannot represent an even split" from a claim about your optimiser into a claim about the family; and that distinction is the whole method of this book in one experiment.

The framework, and the representation

Dataset, model family, loss, optimiser. This part is almost entirely about a fifth thing the framework never named: the representation you convert the data into before any of the four apply.

A mel spectrogram is not preprocessing. It is a window length asserting how fast the signal changes, a warping asserting that resolution should degrade like hearing's, and the deletion of the quantity Part XXVII measured as carrying the structure. Every one of those is a modelling decision made before the model exists, none is visible in a loss curve, and each bounds what any amount of capacity can recover. The same is true one level up: two towers fix what a shared embedding can express before a single parameter is trained. Choose the representation with the care you would give the model, because the model cannot recover what it deleted.
