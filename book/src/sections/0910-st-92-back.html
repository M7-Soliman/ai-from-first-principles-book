BuildThe project

Six to nine hours. You build the tools for honest measurement, then turn them on a claim and find out whether it survives.

P.1 What you are building

tinystat: an estimation and evaluation toolkit, then an audit that uses it to test whether a reported improvement is real.

The rule: NumPy only. No scipy.stats, no statsmodels, no sklearn.metrics. If a library provides it, you are writing it.

P.2 Stage 1: estimators and their properties

FunctionNotes
sampling_distribution(estimator, sampler, n, reps)The workhorse. Draw reps samples of size n, apply the estimator, return the array of estimates.
bias_variance_mse(estimates, truth)All three, and assert the identity holds.
standard_error(estimates)Spread of the estimator, not of the data.

Deliverable: a log-log plot of measured standard error against $n$ for three different source distributions: Gaussian, heavy-tailed, and skewed. All three should sit on a $1/\sqrt{n}$ line. Then find a distribution for which they do not, and explain it. (Hint: the central limit theorem requires finite variance.)

P.3 Stage 2: intervals

  • normal_ci(data, alpha): the textbook interval.
  • bootstrap_ci(data, stat, n_boot, alpha): percentile bootstrap, working for any statistic passed in.
  • coverage_test(ci_fn, sampler, truth, reps): the honest check: build many intervals and count how often they contain the truth.

Deliverable: a coverage table for both interval methods at $n \in \{5, 10, 30, 100\}$ across three source distributions. A 95% interval should cover 95% of the time; report where each method falls short and by how much. This is the stage that teaches you to distrust intervals you did not check.

P.4 Stage 3: comparison machinery

  • permutation_test(a, b, stat): the assumption-free test: if the labels are meaningless, shuffling them should not change the statistic.
  • paired_bootstrap(a_correct, b_correct): interval on the difference between two models evaluated on the same examples.
  • power_curve(effect_sizes, n, reps)
  • bonferroni and benjamini_hochberg

Deliverable: confirm your permutation test produces uniform p-values under the null. If the histogram is not flat, the test is wrong; this is the sharpest check available and it catches real bugs.

P.5 Stage 4: the audit

Now use the toolkit on a claim. Construct a scenario in which two models genuinely differ by a small amount, then answer:

  1. How much test data is needed to detect a 0.5 percentage point difference with 80% power? Compute it, then verify by simulation.
  2. How much does pairing buy? Compare interval widths on the difference, paired and unpaired, from identical data.
  3. What does search cost? Simulate $k$ identical models, report the best, and plot optimism against $k$.
  4. What does test-set reuse cost? Simulate the adaptive researcher of drill S2 and plot reported against true accuracy over 200 rounds.
  5. Reproduce double descent. Sweep capacity past the interpolation threshold; note what happens with pinv's default rcond versus 1e-15.

P.6 Stretch

  • Nested cross-validation: separate selection from evaluation, and measure the bias you remove.
  • Bayesian coin: full posterior with a conjugate prior; compare credible and confidence intervals as $n$ grows.
  • The bootstrap's failure modes: the maximum, and a statistic near a boundary.
  • Audit a real paper. Take a published result with reported numbers, and work out what interval those numbers actually support. This is the most useful thing in the project.

P.7 Done when

  • Your MSE identity check passes for several deliberately biased estimators.
  • Coverage tables exist for both interval methods, and you can say where each fails.
  • Your permutation test gives a flat p-value histogram under the null.
  • You can state the test-set size needed to detect a given difference.
  • Your double descent sweep shows the peak at the interpolation threshold.
  • Your README names one claim you previously believed that this project made you retract.
Why this project

Everything here is a tool you will use for the rest of your working life, and almost none of it is standard practice. Reporting an interval on a metric takes ten lines and changes what you can honestly claim. A permutation test needs no distributional assumptions and settles most "is this real?" questions in a few seconds of compute.

The audit stage is the point. Once you have measured how much optimism a hyperparameter search injects, you will not report a search-selected number as an estimate again; and that single habit puts you ahead of a great deal of published work.

  • Define bias, and give an estimator that is unbiased and useless.
  • Explain why the sample variance divides by $n-1$.
  • Distinguish the standard deviation of the data from the standard error of an estimate.

  • Say what the central limit theorem buys you when the data is not Gaussian.

The one thing to carry forward

Every number you report is a random variable.

An accuracy, a loss, a benchmark score, a claimed improvement: each is one draw from a distribution you did not see. The question is never "what did I measure?" but "how much would this move if I ran it again?"

Most of the errors in this part are the same error: treating a draw as though it were the truth. Once you are in the habit of asking for the spread before believing the number, the rest is technique.
