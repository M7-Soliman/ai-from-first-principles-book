Where the data comes from, how to evaluate a system whose failures are rare and correlated, and which of this part's problems will still be there after the next model.

§40 The data engine

The loop that actually produces a perception dataset

Nobody labels a driving dataset by labelling random frames. The loop is: deploy, find the cases the system handles badly, label those, retrain, repeat.

Mining. Disagreement between an ensemble, between frames, or between a model and a slower offline model that can use the future as well as the past. The offline model is the useful trick: it can be far more expensive than the deployed one and is not required to be causal.

Auto-labelling. Run the offline model, have humans correct rather than annotate. The cost per label falls by an order of magnitude and the error correlates with the model's, which is the danger.

Simulation. Unlimited exact labels for scenarios that are rare or dangerous, at the cost of a domain gap that is largest exactly where the appearance is unusual.

Pitfall: the loop optimises for what it can measure

Mining on model disagreement finds cases the model is uncertain about. It does not find cases the model is confidently wrong about, and those are the ones that cause incidents. Nothing in the loop closes that gap on its own; it needs an independent source: a different sensor, a different model family, or a human looking at what the system did rather than at what it was unsure of.

§41 Evaluating a perception system

Six things, and the first is not a metric

Slice. A single AP over a test set is nearly uninformative. Report by range, by object size, by occlusion level, by lighting, by weather. The aggregate hides the slices, and the slices are what fails.

Check the geometry separately. Reprojection error against radius (§27), depth error against range (§29). These have known correct values and are not statistical.

Measure temporal consistency, not just per-frame accuracy.

Split by sequence, never by frame.

Calibrate the confidences and check them (Part XIV §5). A detector's AP is unchanged by monotone rescaling; a downstream fusion is not.

Count the rare events with an interval. Zero pedestrian misses in a thousand sequences bounds the rate at about 0.3% and no lower, which is Part XXI §27's rule of three and usually a looser bound than people assume.

§44 What is unsolved

Four, distinguished by kind, and this part's honest caveat

Every figure in this part measures geometry or arithmetic, which is why all twenty of its predictions held. The genuinely uncertain questions are the ones a figure script cannot reach, and they are these.

Not yet measured. How much of a modern detector's remaining error is localisation and how much is recognition. The decomposition exists and is almost never reported.

Not currently measurable. Whether a perception system will fail on a scenario nobody has thought of. Every evaluation is on a distribution of scenarios someone assembled, and the long tail is by construction not in it.

Genuinely open. Whether an end-to-end system can be validated to the standard a safety-critical deployment requires. This is not a question about accuracy; it is a question about whether an argument can be made at all without intermediate representations.

Not empirical. What counts as an object. A pedestrian pushing a bicycle is one object, or two, and the answer depends on what the planner will do about it.

§45 What to carry forward

Five things

The metric contains a threshold and the threshold changes the ranking. Figure 1 measures AP falling from 1.000 to 0.116 on one detector, and two systems trading places.

Background dominates by construction and gets worse with resolution. Figure 3 measures 672 negatives per positive at 512 px and 10,752 at 2,048 px, which is why the loss rather than the architecture closed the one-stage gap.

Small objects are lost to arithmetic. Figure 4 measures a 16 px object occupying one feature pixel after four stride-2 stages: it is not hard to detect, it is absent.

Some errors are geometric and training cannot touch them. Figure 6 measures 164 px of reprojection error from uncorrected distortion; Figure 7 measures stereo depth error growing at an exponent of exactly 2.000; and Figure 5 measures an edge's tangential motion as unrecoverable to exactly zero.

A four-state linear filter is the cheapest accuracy in the stack. Figure 8 measures 100% misidentification without one and 0.0% with one, until its uncertainty outgrows the spacing between objects.

Sixty seconds

Classification returns a label; everything a camera is actually used for returns a structure (a set of objects, a value per pixel, a position in metres), and each needs a matching rule, a metric with a threshold in it, and a way of surviving the fact that almost every pixel is background. Two of the hardest parts are not learning problems at all. A single view cannot determine scale and a local window cannot determine motion along an edge, both exactly; stereo depth error grows with the square of range whatever the network; and an uncalibrated wide-angle lens misplaces the image corner by more than any detector's precision. So the first question about a perception error is not which model to try but whether the information was ever there, and the second is whether a four-state Kalman filter would have fixed it, because it usually would.
