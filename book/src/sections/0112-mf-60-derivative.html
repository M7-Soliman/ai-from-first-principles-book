§14 Limits, informally

History: calculus worked for 150 years before anyone could justify it

Newton and Leibniz built calculus in the 1660s–80s on a foundation nobody could defend. Both reasoned with infinitesimals, quantities small enough to discard at the end of a calculation, but not so small as to be zero during it, since you had just divided by one.

In 1734 Bishop George Berkeley attacked this directly, in a pamphlet aimed at mathematicians who scoffed at theology while accepting far worse in their own subject. His description of the vanishing quantities has stuck ever since: he asked whether they might be called the ghosts of departed quantities.

He was right, and it did not matter. Calculus kept working (predicting orbits, designing bridges) for another century before Cauchy and Weierstrass replaced infinitesimals with the limit and made the whole edifice rigorous in the 1820s–1870s.

There is a lesson in that sequence, and it recurs constantly in machine learning: usefulness routinely arrives before justification. Deep learning has spent its entire modern history in exactly this position.

You need limits only to define the derivative, and you need them informally. The rigorous treatment takes weeks and buys you nothing here.

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$

reads: as $x$ gets arbitrarily close to $a$, $f(x)$ gets arbitrarily close to $L$. Note what it does not say: it says nothing about the value at $a$. The limit is about the approach, not the arrival.

That distinction is the one thing to take from this section, so here it is in a form that sticks. Imagine driving toward a town. At every moment you can read the distance on the signs (twenty miles, ten, five, one, a hundred metres) and long before you get there you know exactly where you are heading. The limit is that destination, inferred entirely from the approach. And it is a genuinely separate question whether the town exists when you arrive. Perhaps there is a crater where it should be. The signs would have said the same things regardless, because the signs describe the journey, not the endpoint.

That sounds like an odd thing to build a subject on until you notice that in the one case we care about, there is nothing at the destination. The derivative is a ratio whose denominator we intend to send to zero, and at zero the ratio is $0/0$, a genuine crater, not a technicality. The limit is what lets us speak precisely about where the value was heading without ever asking it to arrive.

That distinction is the entire reason limits exist. Consider

$$f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$$

At $x = 1$ this is $0/0$, undefined, a genuine hole in the function. But factor the numerator: $x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$, so for every $x \neq 1$ the expression simplifies to $x + 1$. As $x$ approaches 1, the value approaches 2. The limit is 2 even though the function has no value there at all.

Intuition: limits let you divide by something that vanishes

You cannot divide by zero. But you can ask what happens as a denominator shrinks toward zero while the numerator shrinks too, and often the ratio settles on a perfectly ordinary number.

That manoeuvre is the whole trick of §15. A derivative is a ratio of two changes, both of which we then shrink to nothing. Without limits the expression is $0/0$ and meaningless; with them it is exact.

§15 The derivative

In 60 seconds

A derivative is the slope of a curve at a single point, found by taking the slope between two nearby points and sliding them together. Three readings: geometrically a tangent, physically a rate of change, and practically "if I nudge the input slightly, how much does the output move?" That third one is the whole of training a model.

Section 8 defined the slope of a line: rise over run. Curves have no single slope; the steepness changes as you move. So we ask a narrower question: what is the slope at one specific point?

Stop and notice that the question is, on its face, incoherent. Slope was defined as a change in output divided by a change in input, and at a single point nothing changes. Rise over run at one point is $0/0$. So we are not asking for a quantity that obviously exists; we are asking whether a sensible number can be attached to a place where the direct definition gives nothing. That is why the construction below looks roundabout. It is roundabout because the straight route is closed.

Intuition: a curve looks straight if you zoom in far enough

This is the picture worth carrying, and it makes the rest of the section feel inevitable rather than clever.

Take any smooth curve and magnify a tiny region of it. It looks slightly less curved. Magnify again and it looks straighter still. Keep going and the visible piece becomes indistinguishable from a straight line, not approximately, but in the sense that any bend you could still detect will vanish at the next magnification.

The derivative is the slope of that line. A curve has no single slope, but every small enough piece of it does, and the derivative at a point is the slope of the piece around that point once you have zoomed in far enough that the curvature has gone.

You already believe this about one curve in particular. The Earth is round, and the floor is flat. Both are true, and the second is true because you are looking at a small enough patch. Calculus is the systematic exploitation of that fact.

Here is the construction. Pick a point $a$. Pick a second point a distance $h$ away. Draw the line through both (a secant) and compute its slope:

$$\text{secant slope} = \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$

That is an average rate of change over the interval. Now shrink $h$. The second point slides toward the first, and the secant pivots toward the line that just grazes the curve: the tangent.

The manoeuvre is worth stating plainly, because it is the trick that the whole of calculus is built from and it gets used again for integrals in §25. We could not answer the question we wanted (the slope at a point), so we answered a slightly wrong question that we could answer: the average slope over a short interval. Then we made the interval smaller and smaller and watched where the answers were heading. The wrong questions have honest answers, the answers converge, and the number they converge on is what we take the right question to mean.

You do this already whenever you read a speedometer. Speed at an instant is the same incoherent request, distance travelled divided by time elapsed, at a moment when no time has elapsed. What a speedometer actually reports is distance over some very short interval, and it is a perfectly good answer because over a short enough interval nothing changes much. Instantaneous velocity is a limit of averages, and everyone accepts the concept long before anyone shows them the definition.

Definition: the derivative

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$ the slope of $f$ at the point $a$, when this limit exists.

Notation

History: the notation war, and why the loser's notation won

Newton and Leibniz developed calculus independently within about a decade of each other, and the resulting priority dispute was one of the ugliest in the history of science. The Royal Society convened an inquiry in 1712 to settle it; Newton was president of the Royal Society at the time, and, as later became clear, wrote much of the supposedly impartial report himself.

Newton won the argument in England. English mathematics then used his dot notation for roughly a century, and fell behind the Continent for most of it.

Because Leibniz's notation was better. Writing $\frac{dy}{dx}$ rather than $\dot{y}$ keeps the variables visible, extends cleanly to several variables, and (crucially for §17) makes the chain rule look like fractions cancelling:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}$$

That is not a proof, and the $du$ terms are not really cancelling. But the notation makes the correct manipulation feel inevitable, which is most of what good notation does. Every equation in this book uses the loser's.

Four ways to write the same thing, and you will meet all of them:

$$f'(x), \qquad \frac{df}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}f(x), \qquad \dot{f}$$

The $\frac{df}{dx}$ form is the most informative: it explicitly says "change in $f$ per change in $x$" and keeps the variable visible, which matters once several variables are in play.

Intuition: three readings of the same object

Geometric: the slope of the tangent line.

Physical: an instantaneous rate of change. If $f$ is position, $f'$ is velocity.

Practical (the one that matters for machine learning): if I nudge the input a tiny bit, how much does the output move, and in which direction?

That third reading is the whole of training. "If I increase this weight slightly, does the loss go up or down, and by how much?" The answer is a derivative, and knowing it tells you which way to adjust.

Named: $f(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)h$, the first-order (Taylor) approximation, is what every "nudge and see" argument here, and the whole logic of a gradient step in §24, silently relies on.

Doing it once from the definition

You should compute a derivative from first principles exactly once, to see that nothing is hidden.

Worked: the derivative of $f(x) = x^2$

$f'(x) = \displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h}$

Expand the numerator: $(x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2$, so the numerator is $x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2 = 2xh + h^2$.

$f'(x) = \displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h}$

Every term has an $h$, so cancel one (legal because $h \neq 0$ while we are approaching):

$f'(x) = \displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h) = 2x$

Now the limit is trivial: as $h$ goes to zero, the expression goes to $2x$.

Check against Figure 5: at $x = 1$ we get $f'(1) = 2$. The picture and the algebra agree.

§16 The rules

Memorise four: power, sum, product, chain. The quotient rule can always be avoided by writing the denominator as a negative power.

Nobody uses the limit definition in practice. These rules are derived from it once, and then reused forever.

That is a real transition and it is worth marking, because from here the character of the subject changes. Up to this point you were doing something conceptually delicate: constructing a limit, worrying about what happens as a denominator vanishes. From here you are doing something mechanical. Each rule below was proved once, by somebody, from the definition, and having been proved, it never needs proving again. You apply it the way you apply the multiplication table, without re-deriving why $7 \times 8$ is 56.

This is how mathematics protects its own attention, and it is the same reason you do not implement sorting from scratch every time you need a sorted list. The hard thinking is done once, packaged, and then spent on the next problem instead. The one thing worth having done yourself is the single derivation above ($x^2 \to 2x$, from the definition), so that you know the packages contain something real.

Constant$\frac{d}{dx}c = 0$$\frac{d}{dx}7 = 0$

Constant multiple$\frac{d}{dx}[c\,f] = c\,f'$$\frac{d}{dx}3x^2 = 6x$
Sum$\frac{d}{dx}[f+g] = f' + g'$differentiate term by term
Product$\frac{d}{dx}[fg] = f'g + fg'$see below

The power rule is the workhorse: bring the exponent down in front, subtract one from it. It works for every exponent, including negative and fractional ones:

$$\frac{d}{dx}x^{-1} = -x^{-2} = -\frac{1}{x^2}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x} = \frac{d}{dx}x^{1/2} = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$$
Pitfall: the product rule is not what you would guess

$\frac{d}{dx}[fg]$ is not $f'g'$. That is the single most common wrong guess in calculus.

Sanity check it with $f = g = x$. Then $fg = x^2$, whose derivative is $2x$. The correct rule gives $f'g + fg' = 1 \cdot x + x \cdot 1 = 2x$. ✓ The wrong guess gives $1 \cdot 1 = 1$. ✗

Whenever you are unsure of a rule, test it on a case you can verify independently.

§17 The chain rule: the important one

In 60 seconds

To differentiate a composed function, multiply the rates: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}$. Differentiate the outer function leaving the inner one alone, then multiply by the inner one's derivative. It composes to any depth (one factor per layer), which is exactly why it scales to a fifty-layer network. Backpropagation is this rule, organised.

Go and read

Linnainmaa (1970), The representation of the cumulative rounding error of an algorithm. The chain rule you are about to meet, stated in full generality and turned into an algorithm, years before anyone thought to point it at a neural network. It is worth seeing that backpropagation was not invented for learning; it was found lying around.

This is the section. Backpropagation is the chain rule applied systematically to a composed function with millions of pieces. Every other topic in this document is support for this one.

Begin with the problem, because the rule is much easier to hold onto once you know what it is for. The rules in §16 handle functions built by adding and multiplying: $3x^2 + 5x$ is fine, $x^2 \sin x$ is fine. What they cannot touch is a function built by nesting, such as $e^{-x^2}$, or $\ln(1 + e^x)$, or a neural network. And nesting is exactly how everything interesting in this book is constructed, so without a rule for compositions the previous section is nearly useless in practice.

The chain rule fills that gap, and it says something that sounds too simple to be the answer: when things are chained, the rates multiply.

Why multiply, of all things

This is the step where most people accept the rule without believing it, so it is worth slowing down. The instinct is that combining two things should add. Here is why it does not.

Suppose you are converting money, twice. Pounds to dollars at 1.3, dollars to pesos at 20. How many pesos is one pound? Nobody reaches for $1.3 + 20$. You multiply, getting 26, because the second conversion acts on whatever the first one produced: it takes the 1.3 dollars and scales them again. Each stage in a chain does not contribute an independent amount; it re-scales what arrived.

Derivatives are conversion rates. $\frac{du}{dx}$ says "one unit of change in $x$ buys you this much change in $u$". $\frac{dy}{du}$ says "one unit of change in $u$ buys you this much change in $y$". So a nudge to $x$ produces a nudge in $u$, and that nudge in $u$ produces a nudge in $y$; the effect passes through and gets rescaled at each stage. Multiply.

The units make the same point and are, if anything, more convincing. Write the two rates as $\frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{pound}}$ and $\frac{\text{pesos}}{\text{dollar}}$. Multiplying them cancels "dollars" and leaves $\frac{\text{pesos}}{\text{pound}}$, which is exactly the quantity asked for. Adding them would produce nothing meaningful at all: you cannot add dollars-per-pound to pesos-per-dollar any more than you can add a speed to a temperature. Leibniz's notation was chosen so that this cancellation is visible on the page, which is precisely why it beat Newton's.

One more framing, for when the algebra gets deep. A chain of derivatives is a chain of amplifiers. A signal enters at one end, and each stage multiplies it by some gain. Gains of 2 and 3 give an overall gain of 6. Gains of $0.5$ and $0.5$ give $0.25$: the signal came out weaker than it went in, and no stage did anything wrong. Hold onto that image, because §18 will show you that the sigmoid's gain never exceeds $0.25$, and a twenty-stage chain of quarter-gain amplifiers is the vanishing gradient problem in one sentence.

The chain rule

$$\frac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$$

Or, in the notation that makes it memorable, writing $u = g(x)$:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}$$

Intuition: gears

Gear A turns gear B three times as fast. Gear B turns gear C twice as fast. So gear A turns gear C six times as fast. Rates through a chain multiply.

Or in units, which is the reading that sticks: if a car goes 60 miles per hour and uses 2 gallons per mile, it consumes $60 \times 2 = 120$ gallons per hour. The "miles" cancels, and that cancellation is literally what $\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}$ is doing.

How to actually apply it

Applying the rule is easy. Identifying the pieces is where people lose time, and there is a reliable test for it.

Ask yourself: if I had to evaluate this expression with a calculator, what would I press last? That final operation is the outer function, and everything it acts on is the inner one. For $(3x+1)^5$ you would compute $3x+1$ first and raise to the fifth power last, so the outer function is "fifth power" and the inner is $3x+1$. For $e^{-x^2}$ you would square, negate, and exponentiate last; outer is $e^\square$, inner is $-x^2$.

The test works because a composition is an assembly line, from §7, and the outer function is simply the last station. It also tells you what to do when there are three or four layers: identify the last station, peel it off, and ask the same question again of what remains. You are unwrapping a parcel, and there is never any ambiguity about which layer is on the outside.

The procedure, which works every time:

  1. Identify the outer and inner functions. Ask: what would I compute last? That is the outer one.
  2. Differentiate the outer, leaving the inner untouched.
  3. Multiply by the derivative of the inner.
Worked: three examples, increasing in difficulty

1. $y = (3x + 1)^5$

Outer: something to the fifth power. Inner: $3x+1$.

Outer derivative, inner left alone: $5(3x+1)^4$. Inner derivative: $3$.

$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 5(3x+1)^4 \cdot 3 = 15(3x+1)^4$

2. $y = e^{-x^2}$

Outer: $e^{\square}$. Inner: $-x^2$.

Outer derivative: $e^{-x^2}$ (since $e^u$ differentiates to itself). Inner derivative: $-2x$.

$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = e^{-x^2} \cdot (-2x) = -2x\,e^{-x^2}$

This one matters: it is the derivative of the shape of a Gaussian.

3. $y = \ln(1 + e^{x})$, softplus, from Part II.

Outer: $\ln(\square)$, whose derivative is $\frac{1}{\square}$. Inner: $1 + e^x$, whose derivative is $e^x$.

$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{x}} \cdot e^{x} = \dfrac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}$

Divide top and bottom by $e^x$ and this becomes $\dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$, the sigmoid. So the derivative of softplus is exactly the sigmoid, which is the fact Part II §19 states without proof. You have now proved it.

Chains of three or more

The rule composes. For $y = f(g(h(x)))$:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(h(x))) \cdot g'(h(x)) \cdot h'(x)$$

Multiply one factor per layer, working from the outside in. There is no limit to the depth, which is precisely why it scales to a fifty-layer network.

Why deep learning cares: this is backpropagation

A network computes $L = \text{loss}(f_4(f_3(f_2(f_1(x)))))$. Training needs $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$ for every weight $w$ in every layer.

For a weight in layer 1, the chain rule gives:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial f_4}\cdot\frac{\partial f_4}{\partial f_3}\cdot\frac{\partial f_3}{\partial f_2}\cdot\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial f_1}\cdot\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial w_1}$$

One factor per layer, multiplied together. Backpropagation is the algorithm that computes this product efficiently, by working right to left and reusing shared work rather than recomputing it for every weight.

Two famous problems fall straight out of this formula. If those factors are consistently smaller than 1, their product shrinks exponentially with depth: the vanishing gradient problem, and the reason sigmoid activations were abandoned in hidden layers. If they are consistently larger than 1, the product explodes: the exploding gradient problem, and the reason gradient clipping exists. Both are visible in this one line, before you know anything about neural networks.

Try it: spend real time on these

Differentiate:   (a) $y = (x^2 + 1)^3$   (b) $y = e^{3x}$   (c) $y = \ln(5x)$   (d) $y = \sqrt{4x + 7}$   (e) $y = (e^{2x} + 1)^4$

Answers.

(a) Outer $\square^3 \to 3\square^2$; inner derivative $2x$. So $3(x^2+1)^2 \cdot 2x = 6x(x^2+1)^2$.

(b) Outer $e^\square$; inner derivative 3. So $3e^{3x}$.

(c) Outer $\ln\square \to \frac{1}{\square}$; inner derivative 5. So $\frac{5}{5x} = \frac{1}{x}$. Interesting: the constant vanishes entirely, because $\ln(5x) = \ln 5 + \ln x$ and the constant differentiates away.

(d) Rewrite as $(4x+7)^{1/2}$. Outer gives $\frac12(4x+7)^{-1/2}$; inner gives 4. So $\frac{4}{2\sqrt{4x+7}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{4x+7}}$.

(e) Two chains nested. Outer $\square^4 \to 4\square^3$, giving $4(e^{2x}+1)^3$. The inner is $e^{2x}+1$, whose derivative needs its own chain rule: $2e^{2x}$. So the answer is $8e^{2x}(e^{2x}+1)^3$.
