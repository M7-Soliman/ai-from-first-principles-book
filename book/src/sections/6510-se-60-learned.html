Part FSearch with a learned model

Everything in this part was invented for problems with a written-down state space and a computable goal test. Modern systems have neither: the proposal is a sample from a network, the evaluation is another network, and both are wrong in correlated ways. This movement is what survives the translation, what does not, and the one number that decides whether searching is worth doing at all.

§38 The shape almost everything has

Propose, search, verify

A generator proposes candidates: whole answers, next steps, programs, actions. This is the learned policy, and it defines the branching factor.

A search procedure decides which proposals to extend and which to abandon. Sampling $k$ independent candidates is the degenerate case: a tree of depth one. Beam search over reasoning steps and MCTS over partial solutions are the non-degenerate ones.

A verifier scores candidates, and the search uses the score. It may be a real checker (a compiler, a test suite, a proof assistant) or a learned model, and the difference between those two is the largest single fact in this movement.

Reading it this way makes the classical questions apply again, and they are questions nobody asks of these systems. What is the branching factor, and is it the generator's temperature? What is the depth, and does the evaluation work at intermediate depths or only at the end? Is the selection rule admissible in any sense: that is, can it discard a candidate that would have been best? For sampling-and-selecting the answer to the last one is yes, routinely, and §41 measures the consequence.

§39 The degenerate case, which is most of the field

Three things you can do with $k$ samples

Best-of-$n$. Sample $k$, score each with a verifier, return the best. Requires a verifier and inherits its errors exactly.

Self-consistency. Sample $k$, return the most common final answer. Requires no verifier at all, and works because independent errors scatter while a correct derivation concentrates. It fails precisely when errors are not independent: when the model has a systematic misconception it reproduces every time, majority voting amplifies it. This is the same distinction as Part VIII §18's ensembles: averaging removes variance, never bias.

Pass@$k$. Report success if any of the $k$ is correct. This is not a method (there is no selection rule); it is the ceiling that a perfect selector would reach, and it belongs on every plot as the upper bound the other two are being measured against. Reporting pass@$k$ as a capability number is the most common measurement error in this area.

§40 Verifiers

Two kinds, and the asymmetry that makes them useful

A sound verifier never accepts a wrong answer; a complete one never rejects a right one. A compiler, a test suite and a proof checker are sound and incomplete, and soundness is what makes search over their outputs work: false positives are what search converts into confident errors, and false negatives merely cost samples.

An outcome verifier scores a finished answer. A process verifier scores each intermediate step. Only the second can guide a search that expands partial solutions, because only the second has an opinion about an unfinished one, which is why step-level supervision is worth its much greater labelling cost, and why systems that cannot afford it are stuck with the depth-one tree of §39.

Verification is easier than generation, sometimes

The premise underneath all of this is that checking is cheaper than producing. It is exactly true for problems in NP, approximately true for code with tests and for arithmetic, and false for open-ended questions where "is this a good answer" is as hard as writing one.

The mistake to avoid is assuming the premise holds because the pipeline has a box labelled verifier. When the verifier is a network trained on the same data as the generator, its errors are correlated with the generator's: it is convinced by exactly the answers the generator is most confident about, right or wrong. Independent error is the entire value of a verifier, and a learned one supplies less of it than its accuracy suggests.

§41 How good a verifier has to be

More candidates raises the bar for the verifier, it does not lower it

This is the counter-intuitive result and the reason the figure exists. The instinct is that sampling more gives the verifier more to work with, so a mediocre verifier should do better with a larger $k$. The measurement says the opposite: 0.49, 0.56, 0.63, 0.68, 0.71 as $k$ goes 2, 4, 8, 16, 32.

The mechanism is simple once seen. Sampling more candidates surfaces more of everything, including more copies of the one wrong answer the verifier likes best. Selection takes a maximum, and a maximum over more draws finds the tail, which is the failure mode when the tail is the verifier's blind spot. It is the same arithmetic as reward hacking under KL-free optimisation in Part XXI: optimising harder against an imperfect judge finds the judge's errors, and $k$ is how hard you are optimising.

What to do with this. Measure the break-even before scaling $k$, not after. If solve rate peaks at $k=4$ and declines, that is not noise and the fix is not more samples: it is a better verifier, or a selection rule that does not take a hard maximum. Majority voting among verifier-accepted candidates is the cheap mitigation, because it stops the tail from winning outright.

§42 Test-time compute is a search budget

An old quantity with a new name

Spending more computation at the moment of use is the oldest idea in this part: it is the node budget. What is new is that the proposal distribution is learned, so the budget can be spent along several axes (more samples, longer chains, more steps of a tree search, more rounds of revision) and they are not interchangeable.

Three results from earlier in this part predict what happens as the budget grows, and all three have been rediscovered in the reasoning-model literature. Figure 6: the tuning that was right at a small budget is wrong at a large one, and a badly tuned search does not improve with compute at all. Figure 4: a wider search maximises the proxy more exactly, which helps only while the proxy is good. Figure 8: selection quality must improve with the budget or the budget hurts. A system that gets worse with more thinking is not paradoxical; it is one of these three, and the three are distinguishable by measurement.

§43 Search inside the model, and around it

Two places to put it, with different properties

Around the model, search is explicit: a program samples, scores and selects, and every quantity in this part is inspectable. You can count nodes, log the branching factor, swap the selection rule, and prove things about the result given assumptions about the parts.

Inside the model, a long chain of tokens can implement backtracking, case analysis and self-correction, learned rather than programmed. This is more flexible (the model decides how much to spend and on what) and it is not inspectable in any of the ways above. There is no node count, no admissibility, and no way to bound the answer's quality.

The engineering point is that these compose rather than compete, and they fail differently. An internal search that goes wrong produces a confident wrong answer with a fluent justification. An external search that goes wrong produces a number you can plot against a budget. When something has to be checkable (which is most of the cases where any of this matters), the external structure is what makes the checking possible, and it is worth keeping even when the internal version is stronger on average.

§44 When not to search

Four questions, in order, before adding search to anything

Is there a verifier with errors independent of the generator's? If not, §41 says the search will find the correlated failure, and more budget makes it worse. A real checker (tests, a type system, an interpreter) answers this yes and a same-family reward model usually answers it no.

Does the ceiling justify the cost? Compute pass@$k$ first. It is the best any selection rule could do, it costs one run to measure, and if it is close to pass@1 the generator cannot solve the problem and no search will make it.

Can partial solutions be evaluated? If not, every tree search degenerates to sampling and the tree machinery is decoration.

Is the budget better spent on the generator? §33's lesson runs both ways: search amplifies a policy, and amplifying a stronger policy is cheaper. A fixed compute budget has to be split, and the split is an empirical question that is almost never measured because the two halves usually belong to different teams.

§45 What to carry forward

Six things

A heuristic buys less than you think, and buys it late. Figure 1 measures a perfect heuristic at 4.0× on a grid, with half a perfect one worth 1% of the saving, because A* must expand everything with $f\lt C^*$ and only exactness prunes the optimal corridor.

A bound describes the worst case, not the behaviour. Figure 2 measures 1.25× where the guarantee permitted 8×. Choose the weight by measuring, not by deciding what you can tolerate.

Admissible is not consistent. Figure 3 measures 59% of expansions as re-expansions at sparse coverage. A learned heuristic is the sparse inconsistent kind, so closing states forfeits optimality and not closing them forfeits the running time: pick knowingly.

Ordering beats the pruning rule. Figure 7 measures perfect ordering at exactly the Knuth–Moore count, $b^{d/2}$, worth a doubling of depth, and most of it is available from cheap approximations.

The bound's tightness is the algorithm. Figure 5 measures two valid bounds differing by 19× at $n=18$, one of which does not finish at all beyond it. Validity is correctness; tightness is whether it works.

More samples raises the bar for the verifier. Figure 8 measures break-even accuracy rising from 0.49 to 0.71 as $k$ goes 2 to 32. Measure it before scaling, because past it the extra compute is buying worse answers.

What this part deliberately leaves out

Propositional and first-order logic, unification and resolution theorem proving, knowledge representation and ontologies, SAT and SMT solving, constraint propagation and arc consistency, and STRIPS-style classical planning with planning graphs and hierarchical task networks.

All are real and several are beautiful. They are excluded because this book is organised around what a practitioner needs rather than around the field's history, and none of them is load-bearing for building, training or reasoning about modern systems. The half of the tradition that is load-bearing (state-space search, admissibility, bounds, ordering, sampling-based planning and search against a verifier) is the half that is here. Where to read the rest is in the sources.

Sixty seconds

Search is how a system spends more computation on a harder question, and every version of it is the same algorithm with a different rule for what comes off the frontier next. A heuristic that never overestimates makes A* optimal, and the proof is three lines; what it does not tell you is how much work you save, which turns out to be far less than advertised and to arrive only when the heuristic is nearly exact. Give up optimality deliberately and you get a bound, which will be far looser than what actually happens. When the space is too big to hold, you keep the best few and accept that a wider beam is sometimes a worse answer because your objective is not what you wanted; or you keep a bound and throw away regions unexamined, where the bound's tightness rather than its validity is the whole performance story. In continuous spaces you stop enumerating and start sampling, which buys probabilistic completeness and nothing else. Against an adversary, pruning is exact and free, and the order in which you try moves is worth a doubling of search depth, more than any evaluation function ever was. And in the modern form, where the proposals come from a network and the scoring comes from another one, the number that decides everything is how good the verifier is: sampling more candidates raises the accuracy it must have, because a maximum over more draws finds the tail, and the tail is where a verifier trained on the same data as the generator is wrong.
