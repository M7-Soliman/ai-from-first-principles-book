PracticeDrills

Thirty-eight problems. This is the part that actually rebuilds fluency: reading the sections gives you recognition, and only working problems gives you the ability to do it. If you are short on time, skip the reading and do these.

Work them on paper before looking. The answers are immediately below each, which makes it very easy to cheat yourself.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · The size of a layer

A layer with 1,000 inputs and 1,000 outputs. Before computing anything, write down three numbers: how many parameters it has, how many multiply–adds one example costs, and how many megabytes the parameters occupy at float32.

Answer. One million, one million, and 4 MB. The first two are the same number because a dense layer does one multiply–add per parameter per example, which is the single most useful fact in this whole arithmetic, and the reason a parameter count tells you the compute cost for free. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Algebra and functions: rebuild the reflexes

WarmW1 · Expand

Expand $(2x + 3)^2$.

Answer. $4x^2 + 12x + 9$. The cross term is $2 \cdot 2x \cdot 3 = 12x$, the piece people drop.
WarmW2 · Factor

Factor $x^2 - 5x + 6$.

Answer. $(x-2)(x-3)$. Find two numbers multiplying to 6 and adding to $-5$.
WarmW3 · Spot the error

Which of these are wrong? (a) $\sqrt{a^2} = a$   (b) $(a+b)^2 = a^2+b^2$   (c) $\frac{a+b}{c} = \frac{a}{c}+\frac{b}{c}$   (d) $\frac{a}{b+c} = \frac{a}{b}+\frac{a}{c}$

Answer. (b) and (d) are wrong. (c) is fine: splitting over a common denominator is legal. (a) is subtly wrong too: $\sqrt{a^2} = |a|$, since $\sqrt{(-3)^2} = 3$, not $-3$.
WarmW4 · Exponent rules

Simplify $\dfrac{x^5 \cdot x^{-2}}{x^{3}}$.

Answer. $x^{5-2-3} = x^0 = 1$.
WarmW5 · Solve

Solve $5 - 2x = 3x + 15$.

Answer. $5 - 15 = 3x + 2x$, so $-10 = 5x$ and $x = -2$.
WarmW6 · Composition

$f(x) = 3x$, $g(x) = x + 4$. Find $f(g(1))$ and $g(f(1))$.

Answer. $f(g(1)) = f(5) = 15$.   $g(f(1)) = g(3) = 7$. Order matters.
WarmW7 · Inverse

Find the inverse of $f(x) = 3x - 6$.

Answer. Set $y = 3x-6$, solve for $x$: $x = \frac{y+6}{3}$. So $f^{-1}(x) = \frac{x+6}{3}$. Check: $f^{-1}(f(4)) = f^{-1}(6) = 4$. ✓
WarmW8 · Domain

What is the domain of $f(x) = \ln(x - 2)$?

Answer. $x > 2$. The log needs a strictly positive argument, so $x - 2 > 0$.
WarmW9 · Log rules

Write $\ln\!\left(\frac{x^3 y}{z^2}\right)$ as separate logs.

Answer. $3\ln x + \ln y - 2\ln z$.
WarmW10 · Log values

Without a calculator: $\ln(1)$, $\ln(e^4)$, $\log_2(32)$, $\log_{10}(0.01)$.

Answer. $0$;   $4$;   $5$ (since $2^5=32$);   $-2$ (since $10^{-2} = 0.01$).
WarmW11 · Solve with logs

Solve $3e^{2x} = 24$.

Answer. $e^{2x} = 8$, so $2x = \ln 8$ and $x = \frac{\ln 8}{2} \approx 1.040$.
WarmW12 · Summation

Compute $\sum_{i=1}^{4} (2i - 1)$.

Answer. $1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16$. (The first four odd numbers sum to $4^2$; that pattern always holds.)
WarmW13 · Product to sum

Rewrite $\ln\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i\right)$ as a sum, and say why anyone bothers.

Answer. $\sum_{i=1}^n \ln p_i$. Because the product of many probabilities underflows to zero in floating point, while the sum of their logs stays perfectly well-behaved (§11).

Tier 2 Derivatives: the core skill

CoreC1 · Power rule

Differentiate: (a) $x^7$   (b) $3x^4 - 2x + 9$   (c) $\frac{1}{x^2}$   (d) $\sqrt{x}$

Answer. (a) $7x^6$. (b) $12x^3 - 2$. (c) Rewrite as $x^{-2}$: $-2x^{-3} = -\frac{2}{x^3}$. (d) $x^{1/2} \to \frac12 x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt x}$.
CoreC2 · From the definition

Compute the derivative of $f(x) = 3x^2$ using the limit definition. Do not use the power rule.

Answer. $\frac{3(x+h)^2 - 3x^2}{h} = \frac{6xh + 3h^2}{h} = 6x + 3h$. As $h \to 0$ this gives $6x$. Matches the power rule, as it must.
CoreC3 · Product rule

Differentiate $y = x^2 e^x$.

Answer. $f'g + fg' = 2x e^x + x^2 e^x = e^x(2x + x^2)$. Factoring out $e^x$ is standard practice and makes later work easier.
CoreC4 · Quotient rule

Differentiate $y = \dfrac{x}{x+1}$.

Answer. $\dfrac{(1)(x+1) - (x)(1)}{(x+1)^2} = \dfrac{1}{(x+1)^2}$.
CoreC5 · Chain rule, basic

Differentiate: (a) $(5x+2)^4$   (b) $e^{4x}$   (c) $\ln(x^2+1)$

Answer. (a) $4(5x+2)^3 \cdot 5 = 20(5x+2)^3$. (b) $4e^{4x}$. (c) $\frac{2x}{x^2+1}$.
CoreC6 · Chain rule, nested twice

Differentiate $y = e^{(x^2+1)^3}$.

Answer. Three layers. Outer $e^\square$ keeps itself: $e^{(x^2+1)^3}$. Next layer $\square^3 \to 3(x^2+1)^2$. Innermost $x^2+1 \to 2x$. Multiply: $e^{(x^2+1)^3} \cdot 3(x^2+1)^2 \cdot 2x = 6x(x^2+1)^2 e^{(x^2+1)^3}$.
CoreC7 · Derive the sigmoid derivative

Show that $\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))$, starting from $\sigma(x) = (1+e^{-x})^{-1}$.

Answer. See the worked example in §18. The key move is splitting $\frac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^2}$ into $\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}} \cdot \frac{e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}}$ and recognising the second factor as $1 - \sigma(x)$. Do it yourself before rereading.
CoreC8 · Softplus

Show that the derivative of $\ln(1+e^x)$ is the sigmoid.

Answer. Chain rule gives $\frac{e^x}{1+e^x}$. Divide top and bottom by $e^x$: $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} = \sigma(x)$.
CoreC9 · Critical points

Find and classify the critical points of $f(x) = 2x^3 - 9x^2 + 12x$.

Answer. $f' = 6x^2 - 18x + 12 = 6(x-1)(x-2)$, zero at $x=1,2$. $f'' = 12x - 18$. At $x=1$: $f''=-6 <0$, local max. At $x=2$: $f''=6>0$, local min.
CoreC10 · Minimise

Find the value of $x$ minimising $f(x) = (x-4)^2 + 3$.

Answer. $f' = 2(x-4) = 0$ gives $x = 4$. $f'' = 2 > 0$, confirming a minimum. The minimum value is 3, but the $\arg\min$ is 4 (§2's distinction), in practice.
CoreC11 · Partial derivatives

For $f(x,y) = 3x^2y + y^3 - 2x$, find $\partial f/\partial x$ and $\partial f/\partial y$.

Answer. $\partial f/\partial x = 6xy - 2$ (the $y^3$ is constant here). $\partial f/\partial y = 3x^2 + 3y^2$ (the $-2x$ is constant here).
CoreC12 · Gradient

Find $\nabla f$ at $(1,2)$ for $f(x,y) = x^2 + xy + y^2$.

Answer. $\partial f/\partial x = 2x + y$; $\partial f/\partial y = x + 2y$. At $(1,2)$: $\nabla f = (4, 5)$.
CoreC13 · One descent step

Minimise $f(x,y) = x^2 + 2y^2$ from $(2, 1)$ with $\alpha = 0.1$. Take one step.

Answer. $\nabla f = (2x, 4y) = (4, 4)$. Update: $(2,1) - 0.1(4,4) = (1.6, 0.6)$. Loss went from $4 + 2 = 6$ to $2.56 + 0.72 = 3.28$. ✓
CoreC14 · Backprop by hand

Model $\hat y = \sigma(wx)$ with $x=1$, $w=0$, target $y=1$, loss $(\hat y - y)^2$. Compute $\partial L/\partial w$.

Answer. $z = 0$, $\hat y = \sigma(0) = 0.5$. $\partial L/\partial \hat y = 2(0.5-1) = -1$. $\partial \hat y/\partial z = 0.5(0.5) = 0.25$. $\partial z/\partial w = x = 1$. Product: $-0.25$. Negative, so gradient descent increases $w$, correct, since raising $w$ pushes $\hat y$ toward the target of 1.
CoreC15 · Numerical derivative

Implement numerical_derivative(f, x, h) using the central difference $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}$. Test against the known derivative of $x^2$.

Answer. Central difference is far more accurate than the forward version; its error shrinks like $h^2$ rather than $h$. Best accuracy comes around $h \approx 10^{-5}$; smaller $h$ makes it worse, because subtracting two nearly equal numbers destroys precision (§26).
CoreC16 · Gradient check

Implement gradient_check(f, grad_f, x): compare an analytic gradient against a numerical one, component by component.

Answer. Perturb one coordinate at a time. Compare with relative error $\frac{|a-n|}{\max(|a|,|n|)}$: below about $10^{-6}$ means agreement. This is a genuine professional technique: it is how you verify hand-written backprop, and it will catch real bugs in your Part VII project.
CoreC17 · Stable sigmoid

Implement a sigmoid that survives $x = -1000$ and $x = 1000$.

Answer. Branch on sign: for $x \geq 0$ use $1/(1+e^{-x})$; for $x < 0$ use $e^x/(1+e^x)$. Either way you only ever exponentiate a non-positive number, which cannot overflow.
CoreC18 · Gradient descent, coded

Implement gradient_descent(grad_f, start, lr, steps) returning the full path. Run it on $f(x,y) = x^2 + 10y^2$ with $\alpha = 0.01, 0.05, 0.2$.

Answer. You should reproduce Figure 11: crawl, converge, diverge. The elongated bowl also shows the zigzag caused by unequal curvature in the two directions; that is ill-conditioning, and it is exactly what momentum in Part IX fixes.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · Chain of four

Differentiate $y = \sigma(\ln(x^2+1))$.

Answer. Let $u = \ln(x^2+1)$. Then $\frac{dy}{dx} = \sigma(u)(1-\sigma(u)) \cdot \frac{2x}{x^2+1}$. Leave it in terms of $u$; expanding buys nothing and invites errors.
StretchS2 · Log-derivative trick

Show that $\frac{d}{dx}\ln f(x) = \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}$, and use it to differentiate $y = \ln(x^2 e^{3x})$ easily.

Answer. The identity is the chain rule with outer $\ln$. For the example, simplify first: $\ln(x^2 e^{3x}) = 2\ln x + 3x$, whose derivative is $\frac{2}{x} + 3$. Simplifying with log rules before differentiating is almost always faster than brute force.
StretchS3 · Why squared error

Show that $\frac{d}{dx}|x|$ is undefined at 0 but $\frac{d}{dx}x^2$ is fine everywhere. Connect this to why losses are usually squared.

Answer. $|x|$ has slope $-1$ on the left and $+1$ on the right, so no tangent exists at 0, making a corner. $x^2$ has derivative $2x$, smooth everywhere and conveniently zero at the minimum. Since optimisation means repeated differentiation, smoothness is worth a lot. This is the same argument Part I §14 makes for the squared $L^2$ norm.
StretchS4 · Vanishing gradients, quantified

The sigmoid's derivative peaks at $0.25$. If a 10-layer network multiplies one such factor per layer, what is the largest possible gradient scale at layer 1?

Answer. $0.25^{10} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-7}$, and that is the best case, assuming every unit sits at its most favourable point. In practice it is far smaller. Early layers effectively stop learning. This single calculation explains why deep sigmoid networks failed for years, and why ReLU (whose derivative is exactly 1 where active) changed things.
StretchS5 · Derive linear regression

For $L(w) = \sum_i (wx_i - y_i)^2$, find the $w$ minimising it, by hand.

Answer. $\frac{dL}{dw} = \sum_i 2(wx_i - y_i)x_i = 0$. Expand: $w\sum_i x_i^2 = \sum_i x_i y_i$, so $w = \frac{\sum_i x_i y_i}{\sum_i x_i^2}$. You have just derived one-dimensional least squares from calculus alone, and Part VI opens by generalising exactly this.
StretchS6 · Two-layer backprop

For $\hat y = \sigma(w_2 \sigma(w_1 x))$ with squared error loss, derive $\partial L/\partial w_1$ by hand. Then verify it with a gradient check.

Answer. Write $a = \sigma(w_1x)$ and $\hat y = \sigma(w_2 a)$. Then $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = 2(\hat y - y)\cdot \hat y(1-\hat y) \cdot w_2 \cdot a(1-a) \cdot x$, five factors, one per link in the chain. Note the two $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ terms multiplying together: that is S4's vanishing gradient appearing in a concrete two-layer network.
