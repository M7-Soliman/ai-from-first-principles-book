Part ABefore you train

§1 The loop

In 60 seconds

Four steps, in this order: decide what you are measuring and what value would be good enough; get an end-to-end system running badly; instrument it until you know which thing is wrong; change that thing. Most wasted effort in this field comes from skipping step three.

The process
  1. Determine your goal. Which error metric, and what value of it would make the system worth having. Both driven by the problem, not by what is easy to compute.
  2. Establish an end-to-end pipeline as soon as possible, including the measurement. It will be bad. That is the point: a bad number you can improve beats no number.
  3. Instrument it well enough to identify the bottleneck: overfitting, underfitting, a data defect, or a bug.
  4. Make one change, based on what the instruments said, and measure again.
Why the ordering is the whole content

Every step is obvious in isolation. The value is in the ordering, and specifically in step three sitting between the problem and the fix.

The common failure is to skip it: performance is disappointing, so you try a different architecture, then a different optimiser, then more layers, then a longer schedule. Each is a plausible response to some problem. Without step three you do not know which problem you have, so you are sampling from the space of fixes rather than choosing from it.

This part is mostly step three. §34 assembles the instruments into a ladder that runs in the order of what is cheapest to rule out.

§2 The metric, and the number that would be good enough

Choosing the metric first is not bureaucracy. Without it you cannot tell whether a change helped, and every subsequent decision is guesswork dressed as judgement.

Two questions, both answered before training

Which metric? It is usually not the training loss. Cross-entropy is what the optimiser needs; what you care about is some property of the deployed system, and the gap between them is where §33's trouble lives.

What value would be good enough? Absolute zero error is not available: Part V §20's irreducible error is a property of the problem. So the target comes from outside: what a human achieves, what the previous system achieves, what makes the application safe or worth paying for.

Without a target you cannot answer the only question that matters at the end, which is whether to stop.

§3 Precision, recall, and where the threshold goes

Accuracy fails on imbalanced problems in a way that is easy to state and easy to forget. A test for a condition affecting one person in a million achieves 99.9999% accuracy by always saying no.

The two rates, and why you need both

Precision: of the cases you flagged, how many were real. Recall: of the real cases, how many you flagged.

A detector that flags nobody has perfect precision and zero recall. One that flags everybody has perfect recall and precision equal to the base rate. Neither is a model; both score well on one of the two numbers, which is why they are always reported together.

Summaries: the $F_1$ score $2pr/(p+r)$, their harmonic mean, which is near zero if either is near zero, unlike an arithmetic mean. Or the area under the precision–recall curve, which summarises every threshold at once.

Why the threshold is a separate decision from the model

A classifier produces a score. The threshold converts it into a decision, and the two are independent: you can change the threshold after training, per deployment, without retraining anything.

This is why Part XIII §10 said resampling is usually the wrong fix for imbalance. If the model ranks well and predicts the majority everywhere, the ranking is fine and the threshold is wrong, and the threshold is free to move.

It also means a single number hides a choice. Reporting "$F_1 = 0.54$" reports one point on the right-hand panel. Report the curve, or state the operating point and why it was chosen, usually from the relative cost of the two errors, which is a fact about the application and not about the model.

§4 Coverage: the option to refuse

A metric the field uses less than it should, and which changes what a system can be used for.

Coverage

Let the model decline to answer when its confidence is below a threshold. Coverage is the fraction of inputs it answers at all; accuracy is then measured on those it answered.

You can always reach 100% accuracy by answering nothing. The useful quantity is the pair, and the useful design question is: at the accuracy this application requires, how much of the work can be automated?

Why this reframes an impossible target into an achievable one

Consider transcribing street addresses from photographs to place them on a map. A wrong address is worse than no address, so accuracy must be near human level: 98%.

Stated as a classification problem that is a very hard target. Stated as coverage it becomes tractable: hold accuracy fixed at 98% by refusing whenever the model is unsure, and optimise coverage instead. The system is useful the moment it can handle a worthwhile share of the volume, and it improves by handling more.

The Street View transcription system did exactly this, targeting 98% accuracy at 95% coverage, and the project's main metric became coverage. That reframing is available far more often than it is used, and it requires only that a human can take the remainder.

It has a prerequisite, which is §5: refusing sensibly requires the confidence to mean something.

§5 Calibration

Go and read

Guo et al. (2017), On calibration of modern neural networks. Establishes that modern networks are worse calibrated than the smaller ones that preceded them, despite being more accurate, so this got harder as the field improved. Then finds that a single temperature, fitted on validation data, fixes most of it. Read §4 for the comparison against more elaborate methods that do not do better.

A model that outputs probabilities is making a claim: among the cases where it says 90%, it should be right 90% of the time. Usually it is not.

Check the claim directly: bin the predictions by the confidence reported, and within each bin compare the mean confidence to the fraction that were actually correct. The result is a reliability diagram: perfect calibration is the diagonal, and a model that is right less often than it claims falls below it. Collapsed to a single number, the gap becomes the expected calibration error: the average of $|\text{confidence} - \text{accuracy}|$ across the bins, each bin weighted by how many predictions land in it, so a bin with five points does not count as much as one with five thousand.

Temperature scaling

Divide the logits by a scalar $T$ before the softmax, and fit $T$ by minimising cross-entropy on validation data:

$$p_i = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)}$$

$T > 1$ softens the distribution, $T < 1$ sharpens it. One parameter, fitted after training, costing nothing and changing no prediction.

Why accuracy is untouched, and why that is the point

The prediction is $\arg\max_i z_i$, and dividing every $z_i$ by a positive constant leaves the ordering identical. So temperature scaling cannot change any decision, measured above as 0.634 before and after.

Which makes it a rare thing: an intervention with no downside on the metric people report, fixing a property they do not report and which everything downstream depends on. §4's coverage needs it. Any threshold on confidence needs it. Any decision weighing model output against cost needs it.

Maximum-likelihood training on a model with enough capacity to fit its training set produces overconfidence as a matter of course: it is pushed toward probability 1 on training examples and there is nothing in the objective that pulls back. So assume overconfidence, measure it, and correct it: the measurement is a reliability diagram and it takes an afternoon.

§6 The baseline

Get something working end to end before improving anything. Two rules about what that something should be.

Start simpler than you think, and copy what works

Do not start with deep learning if the problem might not need it. If a few linear weights might do, fit logistic regression first: Part VI's classical methods are a baseline, not a history lesson, and a linear model that reaches 90% of your target in an hour changes what the rest of the project is about.

If the task resembles a solved one, copy the solution. Architecture, hyperparameters, and often the trained weights. Part IX §26's transfer: starting from a model trained on a large corpus is usually better than anything you will design, and it is a first line of code rather than a research direction.

Sensible defaults when you must choose: fixed-size input, a feedforward network; grid topology, convolution (Part X); sequence, attention (Part XII). AdamW with a cosine schedule and warmup (Part IX §18, Part XII §23). Normalisation from the start. Early stopping always, and mild regularization unless the corpus is enormous (Part VIII §22).

Why the end-to-end pipeline comes before the good model

A working bad system tells you things no amount of design does. It tells you the data loads, the shapes are right, the metric computes, the evaluation runs, and roughly where the problem is hard. It gives you the number that every later decision is measured against.

And it front-loads the failures that are cheapest to fix early: a label column misaligned, a split that leaks (Part XIII §11), an evaluation that silently measures the wrong thing. Those cost a day at the start and a month after you have built on top of them.
