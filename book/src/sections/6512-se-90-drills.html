PracticeDrills

Forty-four drills. The harness in parts/33-search/drills/ covers twenty-one of them with forty-one tests. Numpy only, and unlike the last part every number here is exact: these are algorithms with provable properties, so a disagreement between your answer and the reference is a bug in one of them rather than a difference between machines.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · How deep can you look

A game with branching factor 30. You can evaluate 107 positions per move. Before computing: how deep can plain minimax search, and how deep with perfect alpha–beta ordering? Then say what a 10× faster machine buys you in each case.

Answer. Minimax: $30^d=10^7$ gives $d\approx4.7$, so four to five plies. Alpha–beta with perfect ordering costs about $30^{d/2}$, so $d\approx9.5$: nine to ten plies, twice as deep for the same work. A 10× faster machine adds $\log_{30}10\approx0.7$ ply to minimax and 1.4 plies to alpha–beta. Hardware buys fractions of a ply; ordering bought five. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.
EstimateE2 · Gridding a robot arm

A seven-joint arm, each joint discretised to one degree over its full 360°. Estimate the number of configurations, and the storage for one byte per cell. Then say how long an exhaustive collision check takes at a microsecond each.

Answer. $360^7\approx7.8\times10^{17}$ configurations, so 780 petabytes at a byte each, roughly the storage of a large data centre. At a microsecond per check, $7.8\times10^{11}$ seconds, about 25,000 years. This is §24's arithmetic and it is the entire reason sampling-based planning exists. Within a factor of two is a pass.
EstimateE3 · The cost of sampling more

A generator solves 30% of problems per sample. Before computing: what fraction does a perfect selector solve at $k=8$? And roughly how much accuracy does a real verifier need to beat taking one sample at that $k$?

Answer. Perfect selection is pass@8 $=1-0.7^8=0.942$, so 94%, a 3× improvement that is entirely a ceiling, not a result. The break-even for a real verifier in Figure 8's population was about 0.63 pairwise accuracy at $k=8$, and rising with $k$. The gap between 94% and what you actually get is the verifier, and it is the whole engineering problem. Within a factor of two is a pass.
EstimateE4 · Where the memory goes

Breadth-first search on a graph with branching factor 10, solution at depth 8, 100 bytes per node. Estimate the frontier's peak memory, then say what iterative deepening costs in extra node generations as a percentage.

Answer. The frontier peaks at about $10^8$ nodes, so 10 GB, at depth 10 it would be a terabyte. Iterative deepening holds $O(bd)$, about 80 nodes, and re-generates the shallow levels: total work is $b/(b-1)=1.11$ times a single pass, so about 11% extra. Ten gigabytes to eight kilobytes for eleven per cent more work. Within a factor of two is a pass.

Tier 1 Warm-up

Warm-upW1 · The five things

What defines a search problem?

Answer. States, an initial state, actions available in each state, a transition function, and a goal test, plus a step cost if the problem has an objective as well as a target. Everything else (path, solution, optimality) is derived from those.
Warm-upW2 · What separates the uninformed four

Breadth-first, depth-first, uniform-cost and iterative deepening differ in exactly one thing. What?

Answer. Which node comes off the frontier next: shallowest, deepest, cheapest, or shallowest-within-a-limit. Same algorithm, different priority. A* is the same algorithm again with priority $g+h$.
Warm-upW3 · The four questions

What should you ask of any search algorithm, including a learned one?

Answer. Completeness, optimality, time, space. Applied to "sample 64 candidates and pick the best": not complete, not optimal, time linear in $k$, space constant. None of those four failures appears in an accuracy number.
Warm-upW4 · Admissible

Define admissibility and say what it buys.

Answer. $h(n)\le h^*(n)$ everywhere: never an overestimate. It buys optimality for A*, by the three-line argument in §9: any node on an optimal path has $f\le C^*$, so a worse goal cannot be expanded first.
Warm-upW5 · Read Figure 1

Figure 1 shows path cost as a flat line. Why is that the interesting panel?

Answer. Because it isolates the claim. Every admissible heuristic returns the identical optimal cost, so the entire effect of heuristic strength is on work done, never on answer quality. A heuristic is a speedup, not an improvement.
Warm-upW6 · Consistency

State the consistency condition and what it lets you do.

Answer. $h(n)\le c(n,n')+h(n')$ for every edge, a triangle inequality. It makes $f$ non-decreasing along any path, so the first expansion of a state already has its cheapest route, so closing states is safe. Admissibility alone does not give this.
Warm-upW7 · Where relaxation heuristics come from

Why does deleting a constraint give an admissible heuristic?

Answer. Every solution to the original problem is still a solution to the relaxed one, so the relaxed optimum is no larger than the true one. Admissibility is automatic. Manhattan distance is the 8-puzzle without the rule that tiles block each other.
Warm-upW8 · Read Figure 5

Both bounds in Figure 5 are valid. Why does one of them not work?

Answer. Validity is a correctness property: both return the true optimum. Tightness decides how much of the tree survives pruning, and it is worth 19× at $n=18$ and the difference between finishing and not finishing beyond that. The theorem in the paper states validity; the performance is entirely the other thing.
Warm-upW9 · What UCB's second term does

Why does the exploration term grow with $t$ and shrink with $n_i$?

Answer. It is a confidence width. More pulls of arm $i$ means a tighter estimate, so a smaller bonus; more total time means more opportunity cost in leaving an arm untested, so every neglected arm eventually becomes attractive again. That second half is what makes the algorithm never permanently abandon an arm.
Warm-upW10 · Pass@k is not a method

Why is reporting pass@$k$ as a capability number a measurement error?

Answer. There is no selection rule: it credits success if any sample is right, which requires an oracle to identify. It is the ceiling a perfect selector would reach, and belongs on a plot as the upper bound. The gap between it and the achieved rate is the verifier's contribution, which is the quantity of interest.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Implement A*

Write A* on a 4-connected grid returning cost, path, and the number of expansions. Handle stale frontier entries correctly. Verify against breadth-first search on unit costs.

Answer. Push $(f, \text{tiebreak}, \text{node})$; on pop, skip if $f$ exceeds the current $g+h$, which is the standard substitute for decrease-key. With $h=0$ the cost must match BFS exactly on every instance; if it does not, the bug is in the stale-entry check.
CoreC2 · Reproduce the flat part of Figure 1

Compute $h^*$ by backward BFS, run A* with $h=\alpha h^*$ for $\alpha\in\{0,0.5,0.9,1\}$, and confirm both the equal costs and the shape of the expansion curve.

Answer. Costs identical for all $\alpha$. Expansions roughly flat to $\alpha=0.5$, then falling, then collapsing at 1. The reason is exact: for a node on an optimal path $g+\alpha h^*=C^*-(1-\alpha)h^*\lt C^*$ for every $\alpha\lt 1$, so the optimal corridor is never pruned until the heuristic is exact.
CoreC3 · Tie-breaking is worth more than $h$

Run A* on a grid with an exact heuristic, then again breaking $f$-ties toward larger $g$. Compare expansions.

Answer. A large reduction from one line. With unit costs there are many equally optimal paths, all at $f=C^*$ exactly, and preferring larger $g$ drives toward the goal instead of fanning across the tie. This is why Figure 1's perfect heuristic still expands a quarter of the grid.
CoreC4 · Weighted A*

Implement weighted A* and measure, for $w\in\{1,1.5,2,5\}$, expansions and the ratio of returned cost to optimal. Check the ratio never exceeds $w$.

Answer. Expansions fall steeply then flatten as the search becomes greedy. The ratio stays far below $w$: Figure 2 measured 1.25× worst at $w=8$. A guarantee is a statement about the worst case over all instances, not a prediction about yours.
CoreC5 · Prove the weighted bound

Show that weighted A* with an admissible $h$ returns a path costing at most $w\,C^*$.

Answer. Let $n$ be a frontier node on an optimal path when the goal $G$ is expanded. Then $g(G)\le f_w(n)=g(n)+w\,h(n)\le w\,(g(n)+h(n))\le w\,(g(n)+h^*(n))=w\,C^*$, using $w\ge1$ for the middle step. The looseness is visible right there: two inequalities are slack on almost every instance.
CoreC6 · Build an inconsistent heuristic

Take an exact $h^*$ and zero it on a random 60% of cells. Prove it is admissible, show it is inconsistent, and count re-expansions with and without a closed list.

Answer. Admissible because $0\le h^*$ and $h^*\le h^*$. Inconsistent because stepping from a kept cell with large $h^*$ to a zeroed neighbour changes $h$ by far more than the unit edge cost. With reopening you pay re-expansions; with a hard closed list you get a wrong answer on some instances: that is the trade in §12, and it is worth seeing both failures once.
CoreC7 · Matched-information comparison

Reproduce Figure 3's control: scale $\alpha h^*$ so its mean matches the inconsistent heuristic's, and compare total expansions at 20% and 80% coverage.

Answer. At 20% the inconsistent one costs about 1.6× as much; at 80% it is about 0.4×. Matching the mean is what makes the comparison mean anything: without it you are comparing a strong heuristic to a weak one and learning nothing about consistency.
CoreC8 · Dominance

Show that if $h_2\ge h_1$ and both are admissible, A* with $h_2$ expands no node that A* with $h_1$ does not.

Answer. A* expands every node with $f\lt C^*$. If $g+h_2\lt C^*$ then $g+h_1\le g+h_2\lt C^*$, so the $h_2$-expanded set is contained in the $h_1$-expanded set. Hence $\max(h_1,h_2)$ is free to use whenever both are admissible.
CoreC9 · When adding heuristics is illegal

Give a case where $h_1+h_2$ is inadmissible, and the condition under which it is not.

Answer. Two pattern databases over overlapping tile sets both count the moves of a shared tile, so their sum can exceed $h^*$. The sum is admissible when the heuristics are disjoint (no single action contributes cost to more than one of them), which is exactly the condition additive pattern databases are constructed to satisfy.
CoreC10 · Hill climbing and its failures

Implement hill climbing on a random landscape with plateaus. Measure the fraction of random starts reaching the global optimum, and confirm the expected restarts to success is $1/p$.

Answer. The count of restarts is geometric with mean $1/p$. Restarts are the first thing to try because they are embarrassingly parallel and need no tuning; a plateau is where they stop helping, since the search cannot tell which direction is uphill and a restart lands on another plateau.
CoreC11 · Annealing is Metropolis

Implement simulated annealing and show that at fixed $T$ the visit distribution approaches $\propto e^{-E/T}$.

Answer. The acceptance rule $\min(1,e^{-\Delta/T})$ with symmetric proposals is Metropolis–Hastings, so the stationary distribution is Boltzmann. Cooling is then a schedule over target distributions, concentrating on the minimum as $T\to0$, the same object as Part XXVIII, and the guaranteed-convergence schedule is too slow to use.
CoreC12 · Beam search

Implement beam search over a trellis and verify against exact Viterbi at large widths. Make sure both start from the same state.

Answer. At width $\ge V^{L}$ the beam is exhaustive and must match Viterbi's score exactly. The start-state convention is the classic silent bug: if Viterbi maximises over initial symbols and the beam is pinned to one, the two are incomparable and every conclusion drawn from the comparison is manufactured.
CoreC13 · Make a wider beam worse

Construct a case where beam width 8 returns a higher-quality sequence than exhaustive search over the same model.

Answer. Let the model score and the true quality be correlated but not equal. The exact argmax of the model maximises the wrong objective exactly; a narrow beam is biased toward sequences with high per-step score, and when the objectives disagree that bias can point the right way. Figure 4 measured beam 8 at 10.97 against the exact optimum's 10.90.
CoreC14 · Branch and bound for knapsack

Implement it with the fractional relaxation as the bound and with the sum of remaining values, and count nodes at $n=18$ and $n=26$.

Answer. Both return the same optimum: check this, it is the test that the bounds are valid. The node counts differ by an order of magnitude at 18 and by more than can be measured at 26 without a cap. Sorting by value density before searching matters as much as the bound, because it makes the incumbent good early.
CoreC15 · Censoring

Your branch-and-bound run hits a node cap on 8 of 8 instances. What may you report?

Answer. That it did not finish within the cap, and the cap. Averaging the cap value in as though it were a measurement understates the true mean by an unknown amount and reports a number that is an artefact of your budget. Censored observations must be marked as censored, which is what the crosses in Figure 5 are.
CoreC16 · IDA*

Implement IDA* and compare its node expansions and peak memory against A* on the same instances.

Answer. Memory drops from $O(b^d)$ to $O(d)$; expansions rise by the re-expansion factor, which is modest when the number of distinct $f$ values is small and severe when costs are real-valued and every iteration raises the limit by one node. That failure mode is the reason IDA* is used on unit-cost puzzles and not on weighted graphs.
CoreC17 · RRT

Implement RRT in a 2D box with obstacles. Plot the tree, then measure how path length changes with more samples.

Answer. The tree fans toward unexplored regions without being told to: extension probability is proportional to Voronoi volume. Path length does not improve with more samples: the first path found is kept. That is what RRT* fixes, by choosing the cheapest parent and rewiring neighbours.
CoreC18 · UCT on a bandit tree

Implement UCT on a two-level tree and reproduce the shape of Figure 6: sweep $c$ at two budgets.

Answer. An interior optimum at both budgets, and the optimum moves with the budget. Build the tree so the best arm has the best child and the worst average child: otherwise the mean is already the right answer and the search is not being tested.
CoreC19 · Alpha–beta is exact

Implement minimax and alpha–beta and assert equal values on 1,000 random trees, then count leaves under three orderings.

Answer. The values must be identical on every tree: alpha–beta prunes only branches that provably cannot affect the root. Leaves: best-first about $b^{d/2}$, random in between, worst-first close to but below the full tree. If the values ever differ, the alpha/beta window is being passed unnegated somewhere.
CoreC20 · The Knuth–Moore count

Check the measured best-first leaf count against $b^{\lceil d/2\rceil}+b^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}-1$ at several depths.

Answer. It matches exactly, not approximately: at $b=3,d=8$ both are 161. This is one of the few places in the book where a measured count lands on a closed form to the unit, and it is worth checking yourself, because it means "optimal ordering" has a precise cost rather than an asymptotic one.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · Break A* with a learned heuristic

Fit a small regressor to predict $h^*$ from coordinates on a set of grids, use it as $h$ on held-out grids with a hard closed list, and measure how often the returned path is suboptimal.

Answer. Suboptimal on a substantial fraction, because the regressor overestimates somewhere on almost every instance and one overestimate on the optimal path is enough. The instructive part is that the average error can be tiny while the failure rate is large: admissibility is a property of the maximum, not the mean, which is why "accurate heuristic" and "safe heuristic" are unrelated claims.
StretchS2 · Make it admissible anyway

Take the regressor from S1 and make it usable: subtract a calibrated offset so that it under-predicts on a validation set with high probability. Measure what the correction costs in expansions.

Answer. A conformal-style offset at the desired quantile buys probabilistic admissibility, and the expansions rise toward the $h=0$ end because a uniformly deflated heuristic is a weaker heuristic. Figure 1 predicts the shape of that loss and it is steep: deflating by even a modest fraction gives back most of the benefit, which is the real reason learned heuristics are used inadmissibly.
StretchS3 · Anytime weighted A*

Implement anytime weighted A*: solve at $w=5$, then re-solve at decreasing weights reusing the $g$ values, reporting the bound at each step. Plot cost against time.

Answer. A staircase converging to optimal with a certified bound at every instant. The engineering value is the bound rather than the cost: a planner interrupted at any moment returns not just a path but a path with a known worst case, which is what makes it usable inside a fixed control cycle.
StretchS4 · Bidirectional search, correctly

Implement bidirectional Dijkstra and get the termination condition right. Show a case where stopping at the first frontier meeting returns a suboptimal path.

Answer. The first meeting is not optimal in general; you must continue until the sum of the two frontier minima exceeds the best meeting cost found. Construct a graph with one cheap long path and one expensive short one and the naive version fails. This is why the theoretical square-root saving is so often not realised in practice.
StretchS5 · RRT* and asymptotic optimality

Implement RRT* with the shrinking rewiring radius. Plot path cost against sample count for RRT and RRT*, and check what happens when the radius is held constant.

Answer. RRT's cost is flat, RRT*'s decays toward the optimum. A constant radius breaks the guarantee in both directions: too small and it stops connecting, too large and every step costs $O(n)$ rewiring. The $(\log n/n)^{1/d}$ schedule is what balances connectivity against cost, and the $1/d$ exponent is where the dimension reappears.
StretchS6 · Narrow passages

Build a two-room environment joined by a corridor of width $\epsilon$. Measure how PRM's success rate scales as $\epsilon$ shrinks, then implement obstacle-biased sampling and measure again.

Answer. Uniform sampling's success falls with the corridor's volume fraction, which is exponential in the dimension. Bias toward the obstacle boundary and it improves sharply. This is the failure mode that governs whether sampling-based planning works on a real problem, and it is invisible in any average-case analysis.
StretchS7 · Reproduce Figure 8's crossover

Simulate a candidate population with an attractive-error mode, sweep the attractive share, measure verifier accuracy empirically, and find the break-even accuracy at $k=4$ and $k=32$.

Answer. Break-even rises with $k$: about 0.56 and 0.71 in the printed population. Sweep the attractive share rather than the verifier's noise: accuracy is not monotone in noise (a sharp verifier is confidently wrong about the attractive error), so the noise sweep gives two very different solve rates at the same accuracy and the plot stops being a function.
StretchS8 · A selection rule that does not take a maximum

On the same population, compare argmax selection against majority voting among verifier-accepted candidates. Find where each wins.

Answer. Argmax wins when the verifier is good, because it exploits the ranking fully. Voting among accepted candidates wins when the verifier is weak, because it stops a single high-scoring attractive error from taking the answer. The maximum is what converts a tail into an output, and it is the operation to soften when the scorer is unreliable.
StretchS9 · Correlated errors kill self-consistency

Simulate self-consistency with independent errors and with a shared systematic misconception. Plot accuracy against $k$ for both.

Answer. Independent errors scatter and the majority converges to the correct answer. A shared misconception is reproduced by every sample, so the majority converges to it: accuracy falls with $k$. Voting removes variance and never bias, which is Part VIII §18's result about ensembles arriving in a different vocabulary.
StretchS10 · Split a compute budget

With a fixed total budget, compare spending it on a larger $k$ against spending it on a better generator (simulate as a higher base solve rate). Find the split that maximises solve rate at two verifier accuracies.

Answer. With a strong verifier, extra samples pay well and the optimum tilts toward $k$. With a weak one the optimum sits at small $k$ and everything else should go to the generator, since past the break-even more samples are actively harmful. The split is an empirical question with a measurable answer, and it is almost never measured because the two halves usually belong to different teams.
