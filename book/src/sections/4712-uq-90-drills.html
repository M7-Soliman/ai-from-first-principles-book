PracticeDrills

Thirty-nine drills. The harness in parts/24-uncertainty/drills/ covers seventeen of them with twenty-one tests. Nearly every one is a coverage measurement, because that is the only thing separating an uncertainty estimate from a number.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · How much calibration data

Split conformal prediction at 90% nominal coverage, with 99 calibration points. Write down the coverage the finite-sample guarantee actually gives.

Answer. Exactly 0.90: ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/(n+1) = 90/100. The guarantee is exact rather than asymptotic, which is what makes it unusual; but it is marginal, so it says nothing about coverage on any particular subgroup, and that is where deployed conformal predictors disappoint. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

Warm-upW1 · The promise

What does a 90% interval claim, and over what?

Answer. That over repeated draws it contains the truth 90% of the time. It is a property of the procedure, not of the interval in front of you (Part V §9's distinction), and the same misreading is available.
Warm-upW2 · Which kind

A weather model says 30% chance of rain. Two doctors disagree about a diagnosis. Which uncertainty is which?

Answer. The first is mostly aleatoric: the atmosphere is genuinely stochastic at that resolution. The second is epistemic: there is a fact and nobody knows it. More data helps only the second.
Warm-upW3 · The test that separates them

You have one number. How do you find out which kind it is?

Answer. Add data and look. Epistemic falls; aleatoric does not. Figure 1 measures 213-fold against zero. If you cannot add data, use an ensemble and read the spread between members.
Warm-upW4 · Why calibration is not enough

Your model is perfectly calibrated on the validation set. Name two things you still do not know.

Answer. How it behaves on inputs unlike that set, and which kind of uncertainty a given number represents. Both matter more than calibration for anything you deploy.
Warm-upW5 · Read Figure 2

Why is the posterior variance larger in the gap than where the data is?

Answer. It reverts toward the prior as the correlation with observed points falls. Note it does not depend on the observed values at all: only on where you observed.
Warm-upW6 · Over and under

One method covers at 0.99 against a nominal 0.95, another at 0.79. Which is worse?

Answer. The second, and it is not close. Too wide wastes information; too narrow means every downstream decision was taken on evidence that did not support it.
Warm-upW7 · The conformal bargain

Split conformal gives a guarantee for any model. What does the model still control?

Answer. The width. Figure 4's constant predictor is exactly as valid and roughly twice as wide. Validity is free; informativeness is the modelling.
Warm-upW8 · Marginal, not conditional

Your conformal intervals cover 90% overall. What might they be doing to your smallest segment?

Answer. Anything. 99% on the easy majority and 60% on the hard minority averages to 90%. This is Part XXIII §43's worst-group problem wearing a guarantee.
Warm-upW9 · The one assumption

What breaks conformal prediction, and does it announce itself?

Answer. Exchangeability between calibration and test. It does not announce itself: Figure 5's coverage reaches 0.496 against a promised 0.90 with no signal from the method.
Warm-upW10 · Where the threshold belongs

A miss costs nine times a false alarm. Where do you threshold?

Answer. At $1/(1+9) = 0.1$. It depends only on the cost ratio: not on the base rate, not on the model.
Warm-upW11 · What 0.5 asserts

Your system thresholds at 0.5. What has it claimed?

Answer. That a false positive and a false negative cost exactly the same. Nobody wrote that down and it is almost never true. Figure 6 prices the error at 4.7× under a 50:1 ratio.
Warm-upW12 · When information is worthless

A test predicts the outcome almost perfectly. When is it worth nothing?

Answer. When it never changes which action is best. Value of information is about decisions, which is why feature importance answers a different question.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Measure coverage

Implement coverage, coverage_by_group and interval_width. Run them on any model you already have.

Answer. One line each, and the first honest number most systems have ever produced about their own uncertainty. Do the by-group version before you believe the marginal one.
CoreC2 · Reproduce Figure 1

Generate data with a noise level you set, fit an ensemble across sample sizes, and separate the two variances by the law of total variance.

Answer. Epistemic falls 213× over the well-conditioned range; aleatoric sits at the square of the noise you chose. Note what happens when $n$ approaches the number of basis functions, and why that number is not interesting.
CoreC3 · A heteroscedastic head

Train a model with two outputs, mean and log-variance, on the Gaussian likelihood. Then check the mean's accuracy separately.

Answer. Watch for the escape route: the loss is reduced by declaring hard points noisy rather than fitting them. A well-calibrated inaccurate model is the symptom.
CoreC4 · Is your ensemble real

Measure member disagreement on in-distribution and out-of-distribution inputs.

Answer. If disagreement does not rise off-distribution, the members found the same mode and your epistemic estimate is decorative. Vary initialisation, data order and augmentation before concluding the uncertainty is genuinely small.
CoreC5 · The GP posterior, from scratch

Implement rbf_kernel and gp_posterior with a Cholesky factorisation. Reproduce Figure 2.

Answer. Thirty lines. The interval widens in the gap without anyone asking it to, and the variance is unchanged if you replace every observed value with nonsense, which is worth verifying directly.
CoreC6 · Sweep the length-scale

Vary the kernel length-scale across three orders of magnitude and watch the posterior.

Answer. Too short and it reverts to the prior between every pair of points; too long and it cannot represent the variation. The intervals are confidently wrong at both ends, which is what "the kernel is the assumption" means operationally.
CoreC7 · Latent against predictive

Compare the variance of the latent function with the variance of a new observation.

Answer. They differ by the noise term, and confusing them is the most common GP implementation bug. Coverage of held-out observations needs the predictive variance.
CoreC8 · Marginal likelihood as model selection

Implement log_marginal_likelihood and use it to recover a length-scale you generated data from.

Answer. It finds it, without holding anything out. Then generate from a kernel outside the family you search and watch it report no distress: the failure mode of trusting the model class.
CoreC9 · Four methods, one coverage number

Reproduce Figure 3: exact posterior, ensemble, MC dropout and point estimate, measured against the same nominal level.

Answer. 0.955, 0.967, 0.993, 0.790. Then restrict the queries to the densely observed region and watch the point estimate look fine, which is why benchmarks that only evaluate in-distribution do not detect this.
CoreC10 · Split conformal

Implement conformal_quantile and conformal_interval. Verify coverage at five nominal levels.

Answer. Within 0.002 of nominal at every level. The whole method is a sort and an index.
CoreC11 · The $n+1$

Implement the quantile with $n$ instead of $n+1$ and measure the coverage difference at small calibration sets.

Answer. A small, systematic under-coverage that vanishes as $n$ grows and is free to avoid. Finite-sample exactness is the selling point; do not give it away in the indexing.
CoreC12 · Break the model on purpose

Run conformal with a good model and with a constant predictor. Compare coverage and width.

Answer. Identical coverage, roughly double the width. This is the experiment that makes the guarantee believable, because it is the one that should break it and does not.
CoreC13 · Make the width adapt

Implement normalised scores using a predicted difficulty, and compare interval widths on easy and hard inputs.

Answer. The intervals stretch where the model is uncertain and the marginal guarantee survives however bad the difficulty estimate is. That robustness is the reason to prefer this over trusting $\hat{\sigma}$ directly.
CoreC14 · Prediction sets

Implement conformal_set for classification. Find inputs producing sets of size 1, size 5, and size 0.

Answer. Set size is an interpretable difficulty signal, and the empty set is a system able to say it has nothing, which is §37's abstention with a guarantee attached.
CoreC15 · Break exchangeability

Reproduce Figure 5: shift the test distribution away from calibration and track coverage.

Answer. 0.901 down to 0.496. Smooth, silent, and not detectable from anything the method reports. Then implement weighted conformal and recover it.
CoreC16 · The optimal threshold

Implement optimal_threshold and expected_cost. Verify by brute force that the formula wins.

Answer. It does, at every cost ratio. Then apply it to a system you run and compute what the default has been costing.
CoreC17 · Reproduce Figure 6

Build a calibrated model by construction (draw the probability, then the outcome) and sweep the cost ratio.

Answer. 1.0×, 1.0×, 1.1×, 1.4×, 2.2×, 4.7×. Every one of those losses is the threshold. Constructing the model by drawing outcomes from the probabilities is what makes the claim airtight.
CoreC18 · Value of information

Implement it. Construct one case where an informative signal is worth nothing and one where a weak signal is worth a lot.

Answer. Worth nothing whenever the action does not change; worth a lot when it flips a close, expensive decision. The two cases differ in the utility matrix, not in the signal's predictiveness.
CoreC19 · Price a refusal

Implement abstain_band and find the cost at which abstaining is never worth it.

Answer. When the abstention cost exceeds what either mistake would have cost in the region where they are close. Then compute the band for a system you run and compare it with whatever refusal rule it currently uses.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · Conformal on top of an ensemble

Build both, use the ensemble spread as the difficulty estimate for normalised conformal scores, and measure coverage and width against each alone.

Answer. The strongest common combination: the ensemble makes it adaptive, conformal makes it valid. Report width alongside coverage or the comparison means nothing.
StretchS2 · Bayesian optimisation

Implement expected improvement over a GP and optimise a function you can afford to evaluate a hundred times. Compare with random search.

Answer. It wins in low dimension with a well-matched kernel and loses when either condition fails, which is exactly Part XIV §16's finding, now with a mechanism.
StretchS3 · A Laplace approximation

Fit one post hoc to a small trained network using a diagonal and then a Kronecker-factored Hessian. Measure coverage for each.

Answer. The approximation quality shows up directly in the intervals, and the diagonal version is usually over-confident because it discards the correlations that carry most of the posterior's mass.
StretchS4 · Weighted conformal under real shift

Take data from two periods, estimate the density ratio, and recover coverage on the later period.

Answer. It works to the extent the shift is covariate shift and positivity holds. Where the conditional moved, no weighting helps, and finding out which you have is the useful part.
StretchS5 · The coverage report

For a system you run, produce the five items from §42: nominal, measured, by group, width, and the named assumption.

Answer. Most systems cannot produce the third and have never checked the second. Producing it once is the highest-value hour in this part.
StretchS6 · Find your threshold's owner

Identify who in your organisation is entitled to set the cost ratio, and ask them for it.

Answer. Usually nobody has been asked, and the answer is not a technical quantity. The conversation is the deliverable; the threshold falls out of it in one line.
StretchS7 · Detect the shift before it costs you

Instrument a running system with rolling coverage on the newest slice, and alert when it departs from nominal.

Answer. This is the monitor Part XXI §35 asks for, made concrete: coverage drift is visible earlier than accuracy drift and needs only the labels you were already collecting.
