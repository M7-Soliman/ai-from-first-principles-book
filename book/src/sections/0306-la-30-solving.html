§9 Systems of equations as $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$

History: Gaussian elimination is about two thousand years older than Gauss

The method of eliminating variables by adding multiples of one equation to another appears in the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, a Chinese text compiled by roughly 200 BCE. Its eighth chapter, concerning rectangular arrays, sets up systems of equations as grids of coefficients and solves them by column operations. It is recognisably the algorithm taught today, including the arrangement of numbers into a table.

Gauss described the procedure in 1810 while computing asteroid orbits, and his name attached to it during the twentieth century, largely through textbooks.

This is a specimen of what the statistician Stephen Stigler called the Law of Eponymy: no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer. Part II §15 contains another, and Stigler (with visible enjoyment) attributed his law to Robert Merton, thereby making it an instance of itself.

Here is a system of two equations in two unknowns, the kind you met in high school:

$$\begin{aligned} 2x_1 - x_2 &= 1 \\ x_1 + 1.5x_2 &= 4.5 \end{aligned}$$

Now notice that the coefficients form a grid, the unknowns form a vector, and the right-hand sides form another vector. Package them:

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4.5 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}}$$

That is the same system, written as $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. Check it against the row view of §4: row 1 of $A$ dotted with $\mathbf{x}$ gives $2x_1 - x_2$, which is the first equation. The compression is lossless.

This is more than tidy notation. It is a change of subject. The scalar version invites you to think about procedure: eliminate a variable, substitute back. The matrix version invites you to think about structure: what kind of object is $A$, and what does it do? Structural questions have answers that hold for a million equations as readily as for two, and procedural ones do not.

Intuition: two readings of the same question

Row reading: each equation describes a line (in 2-D) or a plane (in 3-D). A solution is a point lying on all of them at once. Solving means intersecting.

Column reading: using §4's ingredient metaphor, we are asking what recipe $\mathbf{x}$ mixes the columns of $A$ to produce $\mathbf{b}$? Solving means reverse-engineering a recipe from a finished dish.

The column reading is the more powerful one, and it is the one that makes the existence question in §12 almost obvious. Our example was built from Figure 2 (where we mixed $1.5$ and $2$ parts of the columns to land on $(1, 4.5)$), so the solution is $\mathbf{x} = (1.5, 2)$, and you can read it straight off the picture.

§10 Identity and inverse

The identity matrix

The matrix that does nothing. Ones down the diagonal, zeros elsewhere:

$$I_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad I\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x} \;\text{ for every } \mathbf{x}$$

Under the column view, why this works is immediate: the columns of $I$ are exactly $\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, \mathbf{e}_3$, so mixing them with recipe $\mathbf{x}$ reassembles $\mathbf{x}$ itself. Under the function view, $I$ is the transformation that leaves space untouched. It plays the role that $1$ plays for numbers.

The inverse

The inverse of $A$, written $A^{-1}$, is the matrix that undoes it:

$$A^{-1}A = I$$

If it exists, solving becomes a one-liner. Start from $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, multiply both sides on the left by $A^{-1}$, and:

$$A^{-1}A\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} \;\Longrightarrow\; I\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$$

Clean, closed-form, and (as §13 explains at some length) usually the wrong way to actually compute anything. But as a piece of theory it is exactly right, and it tells us where to look next: when does $A^{-1}$ exist?

Intuition: inverses undo, and some things cannot be undone

Under the function view, $A^{-1}$ reverses whatever $A$ did. Rotations are reversible: rotate back. Stretches are reversible: stretch by the reciprocal. But suppose $A$ flattens, suppose it squashes all of 3-D space onto a plane. Then infinitely many input points landed on each output point, and no function can possibly recover which one you started from. The information is destroyed, not hidden.

That is the whole story of invertibility. A matrix is invertible exactly when it loses no information. Everything in §11 and §12 is machinery for detecting when information is lost.

Pitfall: $A^{-1}$ is not "one over $A$"

There is no division of matrices, and $A^{-1}$ has nothing to do with taking reciprocals of entries. The inverse of $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ is $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.25 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, which happens to look like entrywise reciprocals, but only because the matrix is diagonal. For anything else the pattern breaks completely. Also note that only square matrices can have inverses; a $3 \times 5$ matrix maps between spaces of different sizes and cannot be undone in this sense.

§11 Span, linear independence, rank

In 60 seconds

Three names for one question: how much of space can you actually reach? Span is everything you can build by scaling and adding. Independence means no vector is redundant. Rank counts the genuinely distinct directions: useful columns, not columns. A matrix that is rank-deficient has destroyed information, and every difficulty in this part traces back to that.

Go and read

Hu et al. (2021), LoRA: low-rank adaptation of large language models. Rank, doing real work. A weight update is constrained to be low-rank, which cuts the trainable parameters of a fine-tune by orders of magnitude and costs almost nothing. It is the clearest modern demonstration that the ideas in this section are not bookkeeping.

Three definitions, one idea. The idea is: how much of space can you actually reach?

Span

Definition: span

The span of a set of vectors is the set of all linear combinations of them: every point you can reach by scaling them and adding the results.

Two examples in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The span of the single vector $(1,2)$ is the line through the origin in that direction: scaling is all you can do, so you slide along a line. The span of $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ is the entire plane, since $a(1,0) + b(0,1) = (a,b)$ reaches every point.

But now the interesting case. What is the span of $(1,2)$ and $(2,4)$? The second is just twice the first, so mixing them gives $a(1,2) + b(2,4) = (a + 2b)(1,2)$, still a scalar multiple of $(1,2)$. Two vectors, but still only a line. The second vector added no new reach whatsoever.

Linear independence

Definition: linear independence

A set of vectors is linearly independent if no vector in it can be written as a combination of the others. Equivalently: each one contributes reach that the rest cannot supply. If some vector is expressible from the others, the set is linearly dependent and that vector is redundant.

The formal test, which is worth understanding rather than just memorising: the vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k$ are independent if the only solution to

$$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}$$

is the boring one, $c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_k = 0$. Why does that capture the idea? Because if some non-zero combination reaches the origin, you can rearrange it to express one vector in terms of the others. From $2\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{0}$ you immediately get $\mathbf{v}_2 = 2\mathbf{v}_1$, and the redundancy is exposed. A non-trivial route back to zero is a redundancy.

Rank

Definition: rank

The rank of a matrix is the number of linearly independent columns it has: the size of its genuinely distinct ingredient set. Equivalently, the dimension of its column space.

Rank counts useful columns, not columns. A $100 \times 50$ matrix has fifty columns but might have rank 3, meaning forty-seven of them are combinations of the other three and the entire output lives in a 3-dimensional slice of $\mathbb{R}^{100}$.

The way to hold onto rank is that it is the true number of dimensions, as opposed to the number of columns you were handed. Those two counts are the same only when nobody is repeating themselves.

Suppose you are given a spreadsheet of employees with columns for salary in pounds, salary in euros, and years of service. Three columns. But the first two carry one piece of information between them (knowing either determines the other), so the table describes each person with only two genuinely independent numbers. Rank 2, not 3. Adding a fourth column for monthly salary would not change that, and neither would adding forty more currencies. Columns are free; dimensions are not.

That distinction is why rank is the right notion and column count is not. Every question you actually want to ask (can this system be solved, is this matrix invertible, how much information survives the transformation, how many numbers do I really need to store this) is a question about the honest count, and is answered wrongly by the superficial one. It also explains why the shape of a matrix tells you so little on its own: shape is a promise about how many ingredients are in the pantry, and rank is how many of them are actually different.

A matrix is called full rank when its rank is as large as its shape permits, namely $\min(m, n)$, and rank-deficient otherwise. Rank deficiency is exactly information loss; it is the algebraic fingerprint of the flattening described in §10.

Reading rank off a small matrix

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$. Three columns: $(1,2)$, $(2,4)$, $(3,6)$. But the second is twice the first, and the third is three times the first. Only one independent direction, so $\rank(A) = 1$.

Notice how thoroughly the shape misleads here. A $2\times3$ matrix looks like it should map $\mathbb{R}^3$ onto the whole plane; this one maps all of $\mathbb{R}^3$ onto a single line. You cannot read rank off the dimensions; you have to look at the columns.

Why deep learning cares

Rank is the honest measure of how much distinct information a matrix carries, and that idea recurs everywhere downstream. Redundant features in a dataset make the data matrix rank-deficient, which breaks naive least squares. Low-rank approximation (deliberately throwing away all but the top few directions) is the engine behind PCA in §23, behind modern model-compression methods, and behind LoRA, the technique that makes fine-tuning large language models affordable by restricting weight updates to a low-rank form. The whole family of ideas rests on this one definition.

Try it

What is the rank of $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$? And is it full rank?

Answer. The columns are $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$ and $(1,1,0)$. The first two are clearly independent. The third is their sum, so it adds nothing. Rank 2. This is a $3\times3$ matrix, so full rank would be 3; it is rank-deficient, and consequently not invertible. Every output lies in the plane $z = 0$, so the third dimension is annihilated. Information destroyed; no way back.

§12 When does a solution exist? The complete answer

In practice the overdetermined row is the normal case: 60,000 training images, 500 parameters. No exact solution exists, so we settle for the closest, which is §20.

Now we can answer the question §9 raised, completely and in one picture. Recall the column view: $A\mathbf{x}$ is a mixture of $A$'s columns, so the set of achievable outputs is precisely the span of those columns. That set has a name.

Definition: column space

The column space of $A$ (also called its range or image) is the span of its columns: every vector $A\mathbf{x}$ can possibly equal, as $\mathbf{x}$ ranges over everything.

From which the central theorem is nearly a restatement:

The existence condition

$A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution if and only if $\mathbf{b}$ lies in the column space of $A$.

That is the whole answer. If $\mathbf{b}$ is mixable from the pantry, a recipe exists; if it is not, no amount of cleverness will produce one. Everything else in this section is bookkeeping about how many solutions there are.

The three cases

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, with $m$ equations and $n$ unknowns.

One thing in the table below reliably surprises people, so it is worth flagging in advance: more equations makes the problem harder, not easier. Coming from machine learning, where more data is better, that reads backwards.

It resolves once you see that an equation is a constraint, not a piece of evidence. Two unknowns and one equation leaves you a whole line of answers. Add a second equation and the line usually collapses to a point, which is the tidy case. Add a third and you are now demanding that a single point satisfy three separate conditions, and unless the third happens to agree with what the first two already decided, nothing satisfies all of them. Three lines drawn at random in a plane do not meet at a common point.

So an overdetermined system normally has no exact solution, and that is the ordinary condition of machine learning rather than a pathology. Sixty thousand training images are sixty thousand demands placed on five hundred parameters, and no setting of those parameters satisfies all of them. The field's response is not to find a cleverer solver. It is to change the question: stop asking for a setting that satisfies every demand, and ask instead for the one that comes closest. That is §20.

$m > n$

Intuition: why the count is 0, 1, or ∞ and nothing else

A linear system can never have exactly two solutions. Suppose $\mathbf{x}_1$ and $\mathbf{x}_2$ both solve it. Then any blend $t\mathbf{x}_1 + (1-t)\mathbf{x}_2$ also solves it, for every real $t$; you can verify this in one line by expanding $A(t\mathbf{x}_1 + (1-t)\mathbf{x}_2)$ and using linearity. So two distinct solutions instantly generate an entire line of them. Zero, one, or infinity: those are the only possibilities, and this is a direct consequence of the same linearity that made matrices tractable in the first place.

Why deep learning cares

The overdetermined case is the ordinary condition of machine learning. You have 60,000 training images and 500 parameters: 60,000 equations, 500 unknowns. There is no parameter setting that fits every example exactly, and $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has no solution. So the field stops demanding exactness and instead asks for the $\mathbf{x}$ that comes closest, which is least squares, which is a projection, which is §20. Training a model is this question with extra steps.

Modern deep networks flip it: with more parameters than training examples, the system is underdetermined and infinitely many settings fit the data perfectly. Which one you land on is then decided by regularization and by the optimizer's own preferences, a theme that runs through Part VI, Part VIII and Part III.

§13 Why nobody actually inverts matrices

History: least squares was invented to find a lost planet

On 1 January 1801 Giuseppe Piazzi discovered Ceres, then taken to be a new planet. He tracked it for forty-one days across a few degrees of sky before it disappeared into the sun's glare. When it re-emerged months later it would be somewhere else, and nobody could compute where from so short an arc.

Gauss, then twenty-four, took the problem on. He had a method for fitting a curve to noisy observations by minimising the sum of squared errors, and an insight about how observational error is distributed that would later become the Gaussian of Part II §15. He produced a prediction. In December 1801, astronomers pointed their telescopes where he said and Ceres was there.

It made him famous across Europe at a stroke. It also means that least squares (the computation at the heart of linear regression, and therefore of Part III §20 and of supervised learning generally) was invented to recover a lost asteroid from insufficient data.

Legendre published the method first, in 1805. Gauss claimed he had been using it since 1795 and did not publish. The resulting priority dispute was bitter and never really resolved.

Section 10 gave $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$. It is correct. It is also, in practice, close to malpractice, and the reasons are worth understanding because they generalise to a great deal of numerical work.

Reason one: cost

Explicitly forming $A^{-1}$ and then multiplying costs substantially more arithmetic than solving the system directly by elimination, and the gap widens with size. For large $n$ this is the difference between a computation you run and one you abandon.

Reason two: numerical stability

This is the serious one. Computers store real numbers approximately, and some matrices amplify those tiny approximation errors dramatically. The amplification factor has a name (the condition number), and inverting explicitly tends to make the damage worse than solving directly does. A matrix that is nearly rank-deficient is said to be ill-conditioned: it is technically invertible, but the inverse is enormous and numerically untrustworthy, so small errors in $\mathbf{b}$ produce large errors in $\mathbf{x}$.

Here is that failure, made concrete:

import numpy as np

# nearly dependent columns: the second is the first, nudged
A = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0001]])
b = np.array([2.0, 2.0001])

print(np.linalg.solve(A, b))          ### [1. 1.]  -- the true answer
print(np.linalg.cond(A))              ### ~40002  -- badly ill-conditioned

# perturb b in the 5th decimal place and watch the answer detonate
b2 = np.array([2.0, 2.0002])
print(np.linalg.solve(A, b2))         ### [0. 2.]  -- completely different

A change of one part in ten thousand in the input moved the answer from $(1,1)$ to $(0,2)$. Nothing is wrong with the code, and nothing is wrong with NumPy: the problem itself is unstable. The condition number told us so in advance, which is precisely what it is for.

Pitfall: inv is almost never the right call

If you catch yourself writing np.linalg.inv(A) @ b, replace it with np.linalg.solve(A, b). Same answer mathematically, faster and more accurate in practice. The genuine uses for an explicit inverse are rare and mostly theoretical; if you need it for a formula on paper, fine, but do not compute it.

In the framework: and a warning that costs nothing to demonstrate

The advice above transfers directly. Every library provides a solver, and calling it rather than forming an inverse is the same discipline in every one of them:

import torch

torch.linalg.solve(A, b)        # correct: solve the system
torch.linalg.inv(A) @ b         # slower and less accurate. avoid.
torch.linalg.cond(A)            # check this BEFORE trusting the answer
torch.linalg.matrix_rank(A)     # and this, if you suspect dependence

But there is a trap here that has nothing to do with which function you call, and it is worth meeting now because it will recur for the rest of the book.

The default type is 32-bit, and the ill-conditioned example does not survive it

Run the same nearly-dependent system from the NumPy snippet above, in PyTorch, without saying anything about precision:

A = torch.tensor([[1., 1.], [1., 1.0001]])       # float32 -- the default
b = torch.tensor([2., 2.0001])
torch.linalg.solve(A, b)          ### tensor([1.0012, 0.9988])

A = torch.tensor([[1., 1.], [1., 1.0001]], dtype=torch.float64)
b = torch.tensor([2., 2.0001], dtype=torch.float64)
torch.linalg.solve(A, b)          ### tensor([1., 1.], dtype=float64)

The true answer is exactly $(1, 1)$. In float32 you get $(1.0012, 0.9988)$, wrong in the third decimal place, from a two-by-two system, with no warning of any kind.

Where the error came from, which is not where you would guess

The natural assumption is that the solver lost precision while working. It did not. The damage was done before the solver ran.

The number $1.0001$ is not representable in 32-bit floating point. The nearest value is $1.000100016593933$, so writing that matrix in float32 silently perturbs your data by a relative $1.66 \times 10^{-8}$. That is a tiny, entirely ordinary rounding error, and, as shown above, a matrix with $\kappa \approx 40{,}000$ multiplies tiny input errors like this one:

$$\underbrace{1.66\times10^{-8}}_{\text{input error}} \;\times\; \underbrace{4.0\times10^{4}}_{\kappa} \;\approx\; 6.6\times10^{-4}$$

and the observed error is $1.2\times10^{-3}$, the same size. The solver did its job perfectly; it returned an excellent answer to a slightly different question, because a slightly different question is what it was handed.

Why this is the lesson, not a footnote

This is Part III's conditioning-versus-stability distinction arriving early, and the reason to meet it here is that no choice of algorithm would have helped. Switching from solve to a QR factorisation, or to the SVD, changes nothing: all of them would faithfully answer the corrupted question. The only fix is to not corrupt the input, which means choosing the precision deliberately.

And the default is a real trade rather than an oversight. float32 halves memory and roughly doubles throughput on a GPU, and for a neural network (whose gradients are noisy estimates anyway, per Part III §14) the lost digits are usually worth nothing. Modern training goes further and uses 16-bit formats on purpose. But for a linear solve, a covariance matrix, or anything where $\kappa$ might be large, 32 bits is not enough and the failure is silent.

The habit to carry: check $\kappa$, then choose your precision, then solve, in that order. The condition number is the only one of the three that tells you whether the other two matter.

Why deep learning cares

Conditioning is not a footnote you can skip; it is one of the load-bearing ideas in optimization. In Part III you will meet the Hessian, whose condition number controls how badly gradient descent zigzags; an ill-conditioned Hessian is the precise reason plain gradient descent crawls through narrow valleys. Part III's momentum, RMSProp and Adam are, from one angle, all machinery for coping with bad conditioning. The intuition to carry forward: nearly-dependent directions make problems numerically fragile, and much of practical machine learning is engineering around that fragility.
