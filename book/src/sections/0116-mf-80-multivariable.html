Part IMany variables

Everything so far had one input. A neural network has millions. This part makes that jump, and it is smaller than it sounds: the trick is to change one variable at a time.

§21 Functions of several variables

A function can take several inputs and still return one number:

$$f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y, \qquad f(x,y,z) = xyz$$

With two inputs you can still picture it: the graph is a surface over the plane. Each pair $(x,y)$ has a height $f(x,y)$, like terrain over a map. With three or more inputs the picture is gone, but nothing else changes, and as Part I §3 argued, you reason in two dimensions and carry the conclusion upward.

Take that last claim seriously, because it is the one that decides whether this part feels manageable or impossible. You are about to be told that a loss function has ten million inputs, and the honest response is that nobody can visualise such a thing, not the author, not the researchers who invented these methods, nobody. What everyone actually does is picture a two-dimensional version, reason about it, and then check that the reasoning used nothing specific to two dimensions.

That is a legitimate technique rather than a coping strategy, and the reason is worth stating. Almost every idea in this part is defined one variable at a time. A partial derivative involves exactly one input; the gradient is a list of such things; a step of gradient descent adjusts each coordinate by its own number. None of those definitions mentions how many coordinates there are, so none of them changes when there are ten million instead of two. The picture in your head is a two-dimensional cartoon of a ten-million-dimensional object, and it is a reliable cartoon precisely because the mathematics never looks at more than one dimension at a time either.

Where the analogy does break (and it genuinely does) is in matters of volume and proportion: how much room there is, how likely a random direction is to point uphill, whether a critical point is more likely to be a minimum or a saddle. Low-dimensional intuition is actively misleading about all of those, and this book flags each place it happens rather than pretending otherwise. For the mechanics of differentiation, the cartoon is safe.

Why deep learning cares

A loss function takes every parameter in the network as input and returns one number. For a modest model that is a function of ten million variables, returning a single scalar. Training is finding the input point where that one number is smallest.

Impossible to visualise, entirely routine to compute with. That gap is what this part closes.

§22 Partial derivatives

With several inputs, "the slope" is ambiguous: slope in which direction? The resolution is to pick one variable, hold everything else fixed, and differentiate as usual.

Notice that the ambiguity is real and not a notational quibble. Standing on a hillside, "how steep is it here" has no single answer: steep if you face uphill, level if you walk along the contour, and steeply downward if you turn around. The question only becomes answerable once you say which way you intend to walk. What §22 does is agree on a fixed set of directions to ask about (one per input variable) and answer the question for each of them separately.

The everyday version of this is how anyone tunes anything with more than one control. A shower has two taps and you want a particular temperature and flow. Nobody solves that jointly. You hold one tap still, move the other, observe what happens, and then swap. A sound engineer at a mixing desk with forty faders does the same: nudge one, listen, nudge the next. That procedure (vary one thing, freeze the rest, see what changes) is exactly a partial derivative, and you have been doing it since long before anyone wrote a $\partial$ in front of you.

The reason it is the right move mathematically, and not merely a convenient simplification, is that the results compose. Once you know how the output responds to each input separately, you know how it responds to any combination of small changes at once: you add up the individual contributions. That is what makes the whole strategy legitimate, and it is why a single number per parameter is enough to train a network with millions of them.

Definition: partial derivative

$\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ is the derivative of $f$ with respect to $x$, treating every other variable as a constant.

The curly $\partial$ signals "there are other variables, and I am ignoring them for now".

Worked: $f(x,y) = x^2 y + 3y^2 + 5x$

With respect to $x$ (treat $y$ as a number):

$x^2 y$ differentiates to $2xy$, since the $y$ is a constant multiplier that rides along. $3y^2$ is entirely constant, giving 0. $5x$ gives 5.

$\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy + 5$

With respect to $y$ (treat $x$ as a number):

$x^2 y$ differentiates to $x^2$. $3y^2$ gives $6y$. $5x$ is constant, giving 0.

$\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + 6y$

Pitfall: a term can vanish in one partial and not another

$3y^2$ disappeared when differentiating with respect to $x$, but became $6y$ for $y$. This is correct and is the most common source of errors; before differentiating, say out loud which variable is live and which are frozen.

One piece of bookkeeping before moving on, because it gets used later and is nothing more than the above arranged in a grid.

The Jacobian

So far $f$ has taken several inputs and produced one output. Suppose instead it takes $n$ inputs and produces $m$ outputs (a layer of a network is exactly this). Then there is a partial derivative for every input–output pair, and collecting them into a rectangular array gives the Jacobian:

$$J_{i,j} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}, \qquad\text{an } m \times n \text{ array}$$

Row $i$ says how output $i$ responds to each input. Column $j$ says how input $j$ affects each output.

Nothing new is being defined: every entry is a partial derivative you already know how to compute. The Jacobian is a filing system for them, and it is worth having a name because the chain rule for vector-valued functions turns into multiplication of these arrays, which is where the whole of §17's chain rule reappears in Part VII.

§23 The gradient

In 60 seconds

Collect every partial derivative into one vector and you have the gradient, $\nabla f$. It points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length says how steep. It is always perpendicular to the contours, because moving along a contour changes nothing, so the direction of fastest change must be at right angles to it.

Collect all the partial derivatives into one vector:

Definition: gradient

$$\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} \partial f/\partial x_1 \\ \partial f/\partial x_2 \\ \vdots \\ \partial f/\partial x_n \end{bmatrix}$$ Pronounced "grad f" or "del f". One entry per input variable.

For $f(x,y) = x^2y + 3y^2 + 5x$ from above, $\nabla f = (2xy + 5,\; x^2 + 6y)$.

The gradient has one property that makes it the central object of optimisation:

The key property

$\nabla f$ points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is how steep that is.

That property is the reason the gradient exists as a named object, and it deserves an explanation rather than an assertion, because it is not obvious. We only asked about a few special directions (one per axis), so why should stapling those answers together tell us anything about the best direction, which is generally none of them?

Here is the argument, and it needs no machinery. Take a step of some small fixed length in a direction of your choosing. That step has a component along each axis, and the change in $f$ it produces is the sum of the changes each component contributes: move a little in $x$ and gain $\partial f/\partial x$ for each unit; move a little in $y$ and gain $\partial f/\partial y$ for each unit. Your total gain is the pairing of your step's components against the partials.

So now the question is a budgeting problem. You have a fixed step length to spend across the axes, and each axis pays a different rate, namely that axis's partial derivative. Where do you spend it? Obviously not equally: you put more of your step into the directions that pay well and less into the ones that pay poorly, and you spend nothing on a direction that pays nothing. Work through what "optimal allocation" means precisely and the answer is to distribute your step across the axes in exact proportion to the payoff rates, which is to say, to step in the direction whose components are the partial derivatives themselves. That direction is $\nabla f$.

The two halves of the property then come apart cleanly. The direction of the gradient is the best allocation of a step. The length of the gradient is what that best allocation earns you, large when some partials are large, and zero exactly when every partial is zero, which is the flat ground of §19 arriving in many dimensions.

The budgeting argument is the informal version of a standard result called the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, which is what makes "in exact proportion" precise. You do not need it to proceed, and this book never uses it directly, but the name is worth recognising, because it is how the claim is stated whenever you meet it proved rather than described.

Intuition: the gradient is the fog rule

You are on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the top. You cannot see the summit, but you can feel which way the ground rises most steeply under your feet. Step that way. Repeat.

That local feel is the gradient. And since we want to minimise a loss rather than maximise it, we go the opposite way: the direction of steepest descent is $-\nabla f$. That single sign flip is the whole of gradient descent.

§24 Gradient descent

In 60 seconds

$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\nabla f(\theta)$. Compute the direction of steepest ascent, go the other way, by an amount set by the learning rate $\alpha$. Repeat. Every neural network ever trained uses this line or a refinement of it, and the learning rate is the single most consequential number in the whole procedure.

Go and read

Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986), Learning representations by back-propagating errors. Four pages, almost no notation beyond what you now have, and the paper most people mean when they cite backpropagation. Read it for the tone as much as the content: it is a confident claim made when the idea was still unfashionable.

History: a century older than the computer

Gradient descent is usually presented as a modern algorithm. It was published by Augustin-Louis Cauchy in 1847, in a short note about solving systems of equations that arose in astronomy.

Cauchy's problem was that celestial mechanics generated large systems he could not solve exactly, and he wanted a method of successive approximation that a human computer (which in 1847 meant an actual person with a pen) could grind through reliably. His proposal was to move in the direction that decreases the function fastest, take a step, and repeat.

That is the algorithm in this section, unchanged. It sat as a minor numerical technique for a hundred years, waiting for a machine fast enough to iterate it millions of times and a problem large enough to need it. Both arrived in the twentieth century.

Everything in this document has been building to five lines.

The gradient descent algorithm

Repeat until satisfied:

$$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla f(\theta)$$

where $\alpha > 0$ is the learning rate, controlling how big a step to take.

Read it as a sentence: compute the direction of steepest ascent, go the other way, by an amount controlled by $\alpha$. Repeat.

That is how every neural network in existence is trained. There are refinements (momentum, Adam, learning rate schedules, all in Part IX), but every one of them is a variation on this line.

Why not just solve it?

The obvious objection deserves an answer, because it is the right question to ask. Section 19 said minima occur where the derivative is zero. We now have the derivative in every direction. So why not set $\nabla f = 0$, solve, and be finished in one step instead of grinding out thousands of small ones?

Sometimes you can, and where you can, you should: Part VI does exactly this for linear regression and gets a closed-form answer with no iteration at all. But it stops working almost immediately after, for two independent reasons.

The first is that $\nabla f = 0$ is not one equation. It is one equation per parameter, so for a modest network it is a system of ten million simultaneous equations, and they are not linear. There is no formula that solves such a system, and there is no reason to expect one to exist; the great majority of nonlinear systems have solutions that cannot be written down in closed form at all. Wanting an exact answer does not make one available.

The second reason is more interesting, and it is the one that shapes the whole field. Even when a closed-form solution does exist, computing it may cost far more than iterating. The exact solution for linear regression requires inverting a matrix whose size is the number of parameters, at a cost that grows roughly with the cube of that number. Gradient descent costs one gradient evaluation per step. Past a few thousand parameters the iterative method is not merely competitive but the only option anyone would consider, and past a few million the exact method is not describable as slow; it is impossible.

So gradient descent is not a compromise adopted for lack of something better. It is what you use when the landscape has no formula for its bottom and you must find it by feel. The fog metaphor above is exact: you cannot see the valley floor, you cannot compute where it is, but you can always tell which way is down from where you stand, and that turns out to be enough.

The learning rate is worth understanding rather than merely tuning. The gradient tells you which way to go and how steep it is there; it says nothing whatever about how far the descent continues in that direction. It is a purely local report, valid at the point where you measured it and nowhere else. So $\alpha$ is your answer to a question the gradient cannot answer: how far am I willing to trust this measurement before taking a new one?

Set it small and you trust the reading barely at all, re-measuring constantly: accurate, and unbearably slow. Set it large and you commit to a long stride based on the slope at the point you left, which was correct when you took it and may be badly wrong halfway along. Overshoot the valley floor and you land on the opposite wall, where the gradient now points back the way you came, and the next oversized step returns you across the valley slightly further out than before. That is the diverging run in Figure 11: not random failure, but a systematic oscillation that grows because every step is planned with information that expired before it was used.

Pitfall: the learning rate has no safe default

Too small wastes compute and can stall on a plateau. Too large diverges, often producing nan within a few steps. The workable range depends on the problem, the architecture, and the scale of the data.

If a training run produces nan loss in the first few iterations, lower the learning rate before investigating anything else. It is the most common cause by a wide margin.

The promised payoff: a tiny network, by hand

History: backpropagation had to be invented three or four times

The calculation you are about to do by hand has an oddly repetitive history. The underlying idea (applying the chain rule backwards through a composed function, reusing shared work rather than recomputing it) was arrived at independently by several people who did not know of each other's results.

It appears in control theory in the 1960s. Seppo Linnainmaa published the general method for automatic differentiation in 1970. Paul Werbos proposed applying it to neural networks in his 1974 doctoral thesis, where it attracted little attention. It finally became widely known through a 1986 paper by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, which is the citation most people know.

Why the repetition? Partly because the idea looks unremarkable once you have it; it is just the chain rule with careful bookkeeping. And partly because during much of that period neural networks were an unfashionable research topic, so a good result about them could be published and simply not read.

Here is a complete gradient descent step on a real (if very small) model. This is exactly what a framework does, with the same arithmetic.

Worked: one training step, from scratch

The model. One input $x$, one weight $w$, one bias $b$, sigmoid output:

$\hat{y} = \sigma(wx + b)$

The loss. Squared error against a target $y$:

$L = (\hat{y} - y)^2$

The numbers. $x = 2$, $y = 1$ (the true answer), and current parameters $w = 0.5$, $b = 0$. Learning rate $\alpha = 1.0$.

Step 1: forward pass.

$z = wx + b = 0.5(2) + 0 = 1$

$\hat{y} = \sigma(1) = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-1}} \approx 0.7311$

$L = (0.7311 - 1)^2 = (-0.2689)^2 \approx 0.0723$

Step 2: the chain. We need $\partial L/\partial w$. The dependency runs $w \to z \to \hat{y} \to L$, so the chain rule gives three factors:

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \dfrac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z} \cdot \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial w}$

Step 3: each factor.

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} = 2(\hat{y} - y) = 2(-0.2689) = -0.5379$

$\dfrac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z} = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z)) = 0.7311 \times 0.2689 \approx 0.1966$   (§18)

$\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial w} = x = 2$

Step 4: multiply.

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w} = (-0.5379)(0.1966)(2) \approx -0.2115$

And similarly, since $\partial z/\partial b = 1$:

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b} = (-0.5379)(0.1966)(1) \approx -0.1058$

Step 5: take the step.

$w \leftarrow 0.5 - 1.0(-0.2115) = 0.7115$

$b \leftarrow 0 - 1.0(-0.1058) = 0.1058$

Step 6: did it help? Recompute the forward pass with the new parameters:

$z = 0.7115(2) + 0.1058 = 1.5288$, so $\hat{y} = \sigma(1.5288) \approx 0.8218$

$L = (0.8218 - 1)^2 \approx 0.0317$

The loss fell from $0.0723$ to $0.0317$, less than half. The prediction moved from $0.7311$ toward the target of 1. One step of learning, performed entirely by hand.

What you just did

That was a complete forward pass, backward pass, and parameter update: the full training loop. A real network does the same thing with millions of weights instead of two, and matrices instead of scalars, but the arithmetic is identical. Every factor you multiplied has a counterpart in PyTorch's autograd graph.

Notice the gradient was negative, so the update increased $w$. That is the minus sign in $\theta - \alpha\nabla f$ doing its job: the loss was decreasing in the direction of larger $w$, so we went there.

In the framework: the same six steps, checked against your arithmetic

This is the only place in Part 0 that mentions a deep learning library, and it is here to settle one question: is the calculation you just did by hand actually what a framework does, or merely an illustration of it?

Run this. The numbers are not similar to yours: they are the same numbers.

import torch

w = torch.tensor(0.5, requires_grad=True)   # the parameters we are learning
b = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
x, y = torch.tensor(2.0), torch.tensor(1.0)     # the one training example

y_hat = torch.sigmoid(w * x + b)              # step 1: forward pass
loss  = (y_hat - y) ** 2

loss.backward()                               # steps 2-4: the whole chain, at once
print(loss.item(), w.grad.item(), b.grad.item())
### 0.0723   -0.2115   -0.1058

with torch.no_grad():                        # step 5: take the step
    w -= 1.0 * w.grad
    b -= 1.0 * b.grad
print(w.item(), b.item())                     ### 0.7115   0.1058
print(((torch.sigmoid(w*x+b) - y)**2).item())  ### 0.0317  -- step 6

What each piece corresponds to. requires_grad=True marks a value as something to differentiate with respect to: it is how you say "this is a parameter, not data". The single call to backward() replaces steps 2 through 4: it walks the chain $w \to z \to \hat{y} \to L$ backwards and deposits the result in .grad. Your three factors ($-0.5379$, $0.1966$ and $2$) were multiplied inside it, in that order, and you never see them.

torch.no_grad() is there because the update is bookkeeping rather than part of the model: without it the library would dutifully record the subtraction as one more operation to differentiate later. That single detail is responsible for a surprising amount of early confusion, and it makes sense only once you know that a framework is recording a graph as you go.

Nothing else is hidden. A network with fifty million parameters runs this exact procedure, with vectors in place of the two scalars, which is what Part I is for, and why it comes next.

Try it

Same model and numbers, but the target is now $y = 0$ instead of 1. Compute $\partial L/\partial w$ and the updated $w$. Which direction does the weight move, and does that make sense?

Answer. The forward pass is unchanged: $\hat y \approx 0.7311$. But now $\partial L/\partial \hat y = 2(0.7311 - 0) = 1.4621$. The other two factors are the same, $0.1966$ and $2$.

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w} = (1.4621)(0.1966)(2) \approx 0.5750$

$w \leftarrow 0.5 - 1.0(0.5750) = -0.0750$

The weight went down, and turned negative. That is exactly right: the target is now 0, the model is outputting $0.7311$, so it must reduce its output, and with a positive input $x = 2$, reducing the output requires reducing the weight. The sign of the gradient flipped because the direction of the error flipped.
