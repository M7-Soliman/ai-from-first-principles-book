Part CDeciding what to do next

§13 The decision procedure

In 60 seconds

Look at the training error first. If it is bad, more data cannot possibly help: you are not using the data you have. Only if training performance is acceptable and test performance is not does gathering more data become the right move, and then the question is how much.

The procedure, in order

1. Is training performance acceptable?

If no: the algorithm is not using the data it has. More data is pointless. In order: run §8's tiny-dataset test to rule out a bug; increase capacity (wider, deeper); tune the optimiser, learning rate first; and if a large well-tuned model still cannot fit, suspect the data: too noisy, or missing the inputs needed to predict the output. That last case means starting over with better features or cleaner labels, and it is worth reaching rather than avoiding.

2. Is test performance acceptable?

If yes: stop. §2's target existed for this moment.

If no, and there is a gap: this is variance. In order of cost: regularize harder (Part VIII §22's ordering), reduce capacity, then gather more data. Gathering is usually the most effective and often the most expensive, so decide with §14 rather than by instinct.

Why the first question comes first

It is the question that eliminates the largest number of expensive wrong turns, and it costs one number you already have.

The most common expensive mistake in applied work is collecting more data for a model that is underfitting. It cannot help: by definition the model is failing to use what it already has, and the collection takes months. Part VI §6's diagnosis prevents it, and this is the same diagnosis stated as a decision rule.

§14 Learning curves, and how far they extrapolate

Having decided data might help, the next question is how much. This is answerable rather than guessable.

Measuring the curve

Train on 10%, 20%, 40%, 80% and 100% of what you have, and plot test error against training-set size on log–log axes.

Use a logarithmic scale, doubling between points. Adding 5% more data changes nothing measurable, so a linear sweep produces a flat line and a wasted afternoon.

Why to fit it and why not to trust it far

Test error against dataset size is close to a power law over a useful range, a straight line on log–log axes. Fit the slope and you can convert "should we collect more data" from an argument into an estimate: each halving of the error costs a fixed multiple of the corpus, and you can read the multiple off.

The honest caveat is the figure. An extrapolation from the falling region is a statement about a regime you have not measured, and it can be wrong in either direction: here it is pessimistic by 23%, because the curve's shape changed between the fitting region and the far end. It approaches a floor eventually, and where that floor is (Part V §20) is exactly what the extrapolation cannot see.

Use it to distinguish "another 30% of data will do nothing" from "ten times the data would halve the error", a decision it answers reliably. Do not use it to promise a specific number at a size you have not reached.

§15 What each hyperparameter does

Manual tuning is worth doing well, and doing it well means knowing which direction each knob moves effective capacity.

Effective capacity has three constraints

Part VI §6's capacity, sharpened into something you can act on. Effective capacity is limited by:

  1. The representational capacity: what the architecture can express at all.
  2. The optimiser's ability to find a good setting of it (Parts IX, XI, XII).
  3. The regularization applied (Part VIII).

A model can underfit for any of the three, and the remedies are entirely different. That is the whole reason §13 begins where it does.

The knobs, by direction
Raise thisEffective capacityNote
Width, depthUpThe most direct route, and it costs compute
Learning rateBothToo low and too high both underfit: the only knob that is not monotone, and the first to tune
Weight decay, dropoutDownPart VIII; their strengths add
Training stepsUpEarly stopping is a capacity control
Batch sizeMixedSmaller batches add gradient noise, which regularizes (Part IX §5)

The learning rate row is why every guide says to tune it first. It is the only one where both directions produce the same symptom, so a model underfitting because the rate is too high looks exactly like one underfitting because it is too low, and Part IX §13's pitfall says the rate that looks best after a hundred steps is not the best rate.

Not every regularization knob reaches all the way to the overfitting arm the table implies. Weight decay has a floor at zero: beyond it there is no further capacity to add, only the raw architecture's own. A binary switch (dropout applied or not, weight tying on or off) has exactly two settings and nothing between them to tune. And a unit count is discrete, so it can undershoot or overshoot the boundary between under- and overfitting without ever landing on it. These knobs can only subtract capacity from the ceiling the architecture sets; raising the ceiling itself is the representational or optimiser constraint above, not the regularization one.

§16 Grid, random, and why random wins

Go and read

Bergstra & Bengio (2012), Random search for hyper-parameter optimization. The paper behind Figure 5, and one of the highest value-per-page results in the field: a method that is simpler, cheaper and better, argued from the observation that only a few hyperparameters matter and you do not know which. Their Figure 1 is the right panel of ours and it is the whole argument.

The standard automatic method is a grid: pick a few values per hyperparameter and try every combination. It is worse than sampling at random, and the reason is worth understanding rather than memorising.

Why the right panel is the whole argument

A grid over $m$ hyperparameters with $n$ values each costs $n^m$ trials: exponential, which is the usual objection and not the important one.

The important one is that a grid spends its trials on repeats of the dimension that matters. Every one of the 8 columns in the right panel is tested 8 times, at 8 values of a hyperparameter that changes nothing. Those are 8 experiments returning the same answer.

Random search never repeats a value, so all 64 trials are 64 distinct probes of every dimension including the one that matters. Since in practice only a few hyperparameters dominate (and you do not know in advance which), this is the right bet nearly always.

Two practical notes. Sample on a log scale for anything positive and scale-free: draw $\log_{10}\eta \sim U(-5, -1)$, not $\eta \sim U(10^{-5}, 10^{-1})$, which would put 90% of its samples in the top decade. And do not discretise: binning gives back grid search's defect for no benefit.

§17 Spending the budget where the evidence is

Go and read

Li et al. (2018), Hyperband: a novel bandit-based approach to hyperparameter optimization. Successive halving with the aggressiveness hedged, framed as a bandit problem rather than an optimisation one, which is the reframing that makes the analysis work. Then Falkner, Klein & Hutter (2018) on BOHB, which combines it with a surrogate model and is the honest answer to §18's objection.

Grid and random both run every configuration to completion. That is obviously wasteful: a human watching the curves would kill the hopeless ones after a few epochs, and it can be automated.

Successive halving

Start $n$ configurations with a small budget each. Evaluate, keep the best half, double the budget, repeat. Total spend is roughly $n\log n$ epoch-equivalents rather than $n$ full runs, so the same total budget starts many more configurations.

Hyperband wraps this to hedge the one free parameter (how aggressively to cut) by running several halving schedules with different starting widths. ASHA makes it asynchronous, promoting a configuration as soon as it qualifies rather than waiting for a round to finish, which is what you want on a cluster.

Why it wins, and the assumption it rests on

The mechanism is not that halving evaluates better. It is that eliminating early frees budget to start more configurations, and the dominant factor in a search is usually how many distinct settings you got to try.

The assumption is that early performance predicts final performance. That is usually true and not always: a configuration with a long warmup, a low learning rate, or a schedule that pays off late can look bad early and win eventually. Halving will kill it.

Which is why the figure's left end matters. At small budgets halving is not better: it is the same, because there is no room to eliminate. Reporting only the right end would make it look uniformly superior, and it is not.

§18 Model-based search, honestly

The natural next step is to model the objective. Fit a surrogate (usually a Gaussian process) to the validation scores you have, and propose the configuration that best trades expected improvement against uncertainty. Bayesian optimisation.

Why the honest verdict has not changed much

It sometimes matches a human expert, sometimes beats one, and sometimes fails badly. A decade after the standard implementations appeared it is used, and it has not displaced random search as the default the way its asymptotics suggest it should have.

Two structural reasons. It has its own hyperparameters (the kernel, the acquisition function, the priors), and while these are easier to set than the ones being searched, the regress is real. And in its basic form it requires each trial to run to completion before it learns anything, which throws away the information a human uses most: that a run is obviously going nowhere after two epochs.

That second objection is what §17 addresses, and combining the two (a surrogate model with early stopping, as in BOHB) is the current state of the art and a genuine improvement on either alone.

The practical advice, which has aged well: random search is the default, successive halving when trials are expensive and early signal is trustworthy, and model-based methods when you have many trials and a good reason. Do not reach for the sophisticated tool first, and note that §20 is about a hazard that gets worse the more configurations any of them try.
