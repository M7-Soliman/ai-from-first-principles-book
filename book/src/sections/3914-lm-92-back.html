BuildThe project

Ten to fourteen hours. You build a tokenizer, measure what it commits you to, and then build the evaluation instruments this part argues almost nobody has.

P.1 What you are building

lmkit: a byte-pair tokenizer with a measurement suite, a small model trained on a task with a known rule, and an evaluation harness that reports reliability rather than accuracy.

The rule: construct the task so the answer cannot be memorised. Every in-context result in this project uses a mapping drawn fresh at test time, because that is the only version of the experiment that means anything.

P.2 Stage 1: the tokenizer

  1. Pre-tokenization, training on chunk types weighted by frequency, and encoding in merge order.
  2. Verify: sequences shorten, anything encodes, and a corpus of a few repeated sentences produces a degenerate tokenizer that memorises whole sentences.
  3. Measure fertility on held-out text in several scripts and domains.
  4. Sweep vocabulary size and plot tokens against embedding bytes.

Deliverable: the fertility table and the vocabulary curve. Pick the vocabulary you would actually use, and write down why.

P.3 Stage 2: break place value

  1. Train two tokenizers on identical numeric text, one allowing digits to merge.
  2. Tokenize a range of numbers under both. Find pairs of the same length with different token counts.
  3. Then train a small model to add two numbers under each tokenizer and compare accuracy.

Deliverable: the token-count table, and the accuracy difference. Step three is the one that converts a curiosity about tokenization into a claim about capability.

P.4 Stage 3: in-context learning

  1. Build a task whose input-to-label mapping is drawn fresh for every example.
  2. Train on many such tasks; evaluate on mappings never seen.
  3. Run three conditions: correct, cyclically permuted, and random demonstration labels.
  4. Sweep the number of demonstrations from zero.

Deliverable: the three accuracies against chance, and the demonstration curve. Predict all four before running them.

P.5 Stage 4: the steps are the computation

  1. Build a task requiring several sequential steps with a known answer.
  2. Train two models on identical problems and answers, one shown the intermediate values.
  3. Sweep the number of steps and plot both, and their gap.
  4. Then corrupt an intermediate step at generation time and see whether the answer changes.

Deliverable: the gap, and the faithfulness result. If your gap is flat, your task does not require sequential computation and the experiment is not testing what you think. Do not expect the gap to grow smoothly with depth: once the direct model is at chance it can fall no further, while the chain has more steps in which to err.

P.6 Stage 5: evaluate honestly

  1. Write eight paraphrases of one task. Report best, mean, worst and spread.
  2. Report accuracy and reliability: correct under every paraphrase.
  3. Implement the chained-success arithmetic and find how many steps your model supports at 50%.
  4. Build a judge, and measure its position bias and its verbosity bias.
  5. Implement best-of-$n$ and measure the inflation as $n$ grows.

Deliverable: a results table that reports spread rather than a best number, and reliability alongside accuracy. Then write the same results the way a paper would, and compare.

P.7 Stretch

  • Induction heads: find the moment they form, and ablate to find which heads.
  • Memorisation against duplication, at 1 to 64 repetitions.
  • The position effect: a needle at controlled depths.
  • Retrieval that misleads: inject irrelevant and wrong passages.
  • Prompt injection through a retrieved document.
  • An agent, priced against the chained arithmetic, with and without verification.

P.8 Done when

  • Your tokenizer shortens sequences, encodes anything, and you have measured its fertility on text it was not trained on.
  • You have two same-length numbers that tokenize differently, and the accuracy cost of that.
  • Your in-context task uses a mapping that cannot be in the weights.
  • You have the permuted-label result and can say what it establishes.
  • Your chain-of-thought gap is large at every depth, and you can say why its shape is not the result.
  • You have checked whether the chain is faithful.
  • You report reliability and spread, not accuracy and a best number.
  • Your README names one result that changed when you stopped reporting the best prompt.
Why this project

Stage 3 is the reason for the construction rule. Almost every published in-context learning result is on a task the model may already know, which makes the central question (is it reading the demonstrations or recognising the task) unanswerable from the result. Building the fresh mapping takes twenty lines and makes the question answerable.

Stage 5 is the second reason. The instruments there are not exotic; they are ten-line functions. They are simply not what gets reported, and computing them once on your own results changes how you read everyone else's.

The one thing to carry forward

Almost nothing here is specific to language.

The architecture is Part XII's, the objective Part XVI's, the scaling Part XVII's, the alignment machinery Part XVIII's, the serving Part XIX's. What is genuinely particular is the tokenizer, and it turns out to explain a family of failures that looked like reasoning failures.

A language model is not a new kind of object. It is the objects of the preceding nine parts, composed, at a scale where the composition produced behaviour nobody designed.

And the second thing, because deployment turns on it: capability is not reliability. Benchmarks measure the first. Almost all the engineering that makes these systems usable is about the second.
