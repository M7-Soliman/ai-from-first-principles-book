Part ATwo kinds of not knowing

"The model is uncertain" names two different quantities that behave in opposite ways under more data, call for opposite responses, and are routinely reported as one number. This movement separates them, measures the separation, and states what calibration, the only uncertainty tool the book has offered so far, does not give you.

§1 The promise an interval makes

Coverage

An interval with nominal coverage $1-\alpha$ promises that, over repeated draws, it contains the truth a $1-\alpha$ fraction of the time. That promise is checkable, and checking it is the only thing that distinguishes an uncertainty estimate from a decoration.

Every figure in this part is a coverage measurement. It is a low bar and most methods clear it only under conditions worth stating precisely.

Why the book needs this now

Part XIV §5 measured whether predicted probabilities matched observed frequencies. That is necessary and it is not sufficient: a model can be perfectly calibrated on the distribution it was measured on and say nothing useful about an input unlike anything it has seen.

Part XXI §32's safety case requires a bound with a stated confidence. Part XXII §22's sensitivity analysis requires knowing how wrong an estimate could be. Both need an interval, and neither Part XIV nor Part XXIII produces one for a deployed model.

§2 Aleatoric and epistemic

The two, and the test that separates them

Aleatoric uncertainty is in the world: the same input genuinely produces different outcomes. Two patients with identical records have different results. It is a property of the data-generating process.

Epistemic uncertainty is in the model: you have not seen enough to know which function is right. It is a property of your ignorance.

The test: does more data reduce it? Epistemic uncertainty falls towards zero; aleatoric does not move, because it was never about you.

§3 Why the distinction is operational

They call for different actions

High epistemic: collect more data, particularly near the inputs in question. Active learning is exactly the strategy of sampling where this is largest.

High aleatoric: more data will not help. Either accept the irreducible spread, find a feature that explains it (at which point it was epistemic all along, relative to the smaller feature set) or decline to predict (§37).

The failure of reporting one number: a system reporting total variance cannot tell you which of those to do. This is the practical cost of the conflation, and it is common.

§4 Measuring the separation

The decomposition, and how to compute it

The law of total variance splits the predictive variance in two:

$$\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}[y \mid x]}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_\theta\big[\mathrm{Var}[y \mid x, \theta]\big]}_{\text{aleatoric}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}_\theta\big[\mathbb{E}[y \mid x, \theta]\big]}_{\text{epistemic}}$$

In words: average the noise each model reports, then add the spread between the models' means. Any method producing several plausible models (an ensemble, posterior samples, dropout masks) gives both terms for the price of one.

Try it

Take a model you have deployed and produce five variants by retraining on bootstrap resamples. Compare their predictions on your held-out set and on inputs from a different period. The spread is your epistemic uncertainty; if it is negligible in both, either you have plenty of data or your ensemble is not diverse, and distinguishing those is drill C4.

§5 Heteroscedastic prediction

Go and read

Kendall & Gal (2017), What uncertainties do we need in Bayesian deep learning for computer vision? The paper this section's loss comes from, applied to depth regression and semantic segmentation rather than a toy problem. Read §3 for the derivation and then the finding the paper does not dwell on but the data support plainly: aleatoric uncertainty matters most in large-data regimes, where epistemic uncertainty has mostly been explained away, and epistemic matters most when data is scarce; so the two are not competitors for a modelling budget, they cover for each other.

When the noise depends on the input

Assuming a single noise level everywhere is usually wrong. Predict the variance too:

$$-\log p(y \mid x) = \frac{(y - \mu(x))^2}{2\sigma^2(x)} + \frac{1}{2}\log \sigma^2(x) + \text{const}$$

Two heads, one loss. Part VII §6's "match the output unit to the distribution" taken one step further: you are now modelling the distribution's second moment rather than assuming it.

Pitfall: the model can escape by declaring everything noisy

The loss above is minimised, on any point the mean head fits badly, by inflating $\sigma^2(x)$ rather than improving $\mu(x)$. Hard examples get large predicted variance and stop contributing gradient to the mean.

The symptom is a model that is well calibrated and inaccurate, which looks fine on a reliability diagram. Watch the mean's accuracy separately, and warm up the variance head after the mean rather than jointly from the start.

§6 What calibration does not give you

Go and read

Guo et al. (2017), On calibration of modern neural networks. The paper behind Part XIV §5's calibration measurement, worth a second visit here for what it does not claim. It shows that accuracy improved through the 2010s while calibration got worse, and that a single scalar, temperature scaling, repairs most of it cheaply. It says nothing, and does not claim to, about an input unlike the distribution it was measured on, which is exactly this section's first gap.

Three things it is silent about

Inputs unlike the training data. Calibration is measured on a held-out set from the same distribution. A confidently wrong prediction on an input from elsewhere is invisible to it: Part XXIII §39's shift, arriving where it does the most damage.

Which uncertainty you have. A calibrated probability of 0.5 is consistent with a genuine coin flip and with total ignorance. §2's distinction is not recoverable from the number.

Individual predictions. Calibration is a property of a population of predictions. It says nothing about the one in front of you, in the same way Part V §9's confidence interval says nothing about the interval you computed.

§7 What movement A establishes

Three things

Two quantities, opposite behaviour. Figure 1 measures epistemic variance falling 213-fold while aleatoric stays at 0.16 exactly, because one is about the sample and the other is about the world.

They call for different actions, and a system reporting a single number cannot tell you which to take.

Calibration is necessary and not sufficient. It is measured on the distribution you sampled, it cannot distinguish the two kinds, and it is a statement about a population of predictions rather than about yours.
