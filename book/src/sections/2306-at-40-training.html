§21 Causal masking, and the thing Part XI could not have

In 60 seconds

To predict the next token, a position must not see it. Set the scores for future positions to $-\infty$ before the softmax and every position is trained on its own prefix: simultaneously, in one forward pass. Part XI §28 called this impossible for recurrence, and it is the largest single reason transformers displaced it.

The mask is trivial to state. Before the softmax, replace $s_{ij}$ with $-\infty$ wherever $j > i$. The softmax sends those to exactly zero, so position $i$'s output depends only on positions $\leq i$.

Why this is the whole efficiency argument

Consider training on a sequence of 2,048 tokens.

A recurrent model must compute $\mathbf{h}^{(1)}$ before $\mathbf{h}^{(2)}$, and so on: 2,048 sequential steps, each a small matrix multiplication. The hardware sits idle waiting for the previous step.

A masked transformer computes all 2,048 positions in one pass, because position $i$'s computation never consults position $i-1$'s output; it consults position $i-1$'s input, which is already known. One large matrix multiplication, which is what accelerators do.

And every position supplies a training signal. A 2,048-token sequence is 2,048 next-token predictions, all computed at once, all contributing gradient. This is why the same hardware trains a transformer on orders of magnitude more text than a recurrent model of similar size, and Part XVII is about what follows from that.

Pitfall: the mask is the correctness boundary of the whole model

An off-by-one in the mask lets each position see the token it is predicting. The result is a training loss that falls delightfully to near zero and a model that generates nonsense: it learned to copy the answer it was shown.

This is the single most common serious bug in an implementation, and it does not raise. Part IX §29's ladder has the test: a masked model must be unable to fit beyond the entropy of the data. A loss that goes to zero on natural text is not a triumph.

The direct check is better still: change a token at position $j$ and confirm that outputs at positions $< j$ are bit-identical. This part's project makes it a required deliverable.

§22 Initialisation, and what depth does to it

Part VII §11 derived the variance-preserving initialisation and Part IX §16 completed it. Transformers need one addition, and §16's residual-stream reading says why.

Every sublayer adds to the stream. If each contributes variance $v$, the stream's variance after $L$ blocks is $O(Lv)$: it grows with depth, and by 100 blocks the input embedding is a negligible fraction of what the model is reading.

Residual scaling

Scale the output projection of each sublayer by $1/\sqrt{2L}$ for $L$ blocks, two sublayers each. The contributions then sum to $O(1)$ rather than $O(L)$.

Equivalently, initialise the final projection of each sublayer near zero, so that at initialisation every block is close to the identity and the network starts as a very deep linear pass-through. Both are in wide use and they are the same idea.

Why starting at the identity is the recurring answer

Part X §23's residual block asked the layer to learn the difference from the identity, so that a block doing nothing is what an untrained block already does. This is the same move, applied at initialisation rather than in the architecture.

A network that begins as the identity has a well-behaved gradient at every depth on the first step, and grows its own nonlinearity as training proceeds. That principle (start at something trivial and provably stable, then depart from it) has now produced residual connections, this initialisation, and §17's pre-norm. It is worth carrying as a design heuristic in its own right.

§23 The optimiser, and what transfers

Transformers are trained with AdamW essentially universally, and the reason is sharper than "it works better".

Why plain SGD is not competitive here, unusually

For convolutional networks, SGD with momentum is competitive with Adam and often generalises better. For transformers the gap is large and consistent, and the leading explanation is heterogeneity.

A transformer contains an embedding table whose rows are updated only when their token appears, LayerNorm gains, attention projections and feedforward matrices: quantities whose gradients differ in scale by orders of magnitude. Part III §16's per-parameter step size is exactly the remedy for that, and Part XI §14 already showed Adam rescuing a model that plain SGD could not train.

The W matters too. Part III §17's framework box distinguished $L^2$ regularization from decoupled weight decay, and with an adaptive optimiser they are different operations. Every large transformer uses the decoupled form.

The settings, and the ones with reasons
  • $\beta_2 = 0.95$ rather than the usual $0.999$ at large scale. A shorter memory for the second moment reacts faster to the gradient-scale changes that cause §25's loss spikes.
  • Weight decay on matrices only. Not on LayerNorm gains, not on biases, usually not on embeddings. Decaying a normalisation gain toward zero is decaying the layer toward outputting nothing.
  • Gradient clipping at 1.0, always. Part XI §13's argument survives intact: transformers have loss spikes and clipping is what makes them survivable.
  • Cosine decay with a short warmup. Warmup is still standard even with pre-norm: §17 removed the necessity, not the benefit.
Why the learning rate is the one that will not transfer

Tune hyperparameters on a small model and most transfer to a large one. The learning rate does not: the optimum falls as width grows, so a rate tuned at $d = 256$ is unstable at $d = 4096$, and the standard practice was to re-tune at every scale, at a cost that grows with the thing you are tuning.

This is a solved problem and the solution is worth knowing exists. Under a maximal-update parameterisation, initialisation scales and per-layer learning rates are set so that the optimal rate is invariant to width. You tune once, small, and transfer. Part XVII treats it properly; it is mentioned here because it is the answer to a question this section otherwise leaves hanging.

§24 Regularization, and how little of it there is

The original transformer used dropout at 0.1 throughout and label smoothing at 0.1. Large modern language models frequently use neither, and the reason is Part VIII §1's definition applied honestly.

Why the regularization disappeared

Part VIII exists because models overfit. A model trained for a single pass over a corpus larger than it can memorise does not overfit: training and validation loss stay together, because no example is seen twice.

In that regime dropout is pure cost: it removes capacity from a model that is underfitting, and Part VIII §22's accumulation pitfall applies with nothing on the other side of the ledger. So it was dropped, and the pattern to take from it is Part VIII §22's ordering: diagnose before treating. Regularization treats variance. Single-epoch training on a vast corpus does not have a variance problem.

Where the data is small or repeated, dropout comes straight back, and fine-tuning, which is exactly that regime, uses it routinely.

Stochastic depth, dropping whole blocks at random, is the transformer-specific variant that survived in vision, and §16's residual-stream reading explains why it is coherent: if a block is an addend, omitting it is a smaller intervention than omitting a stage would be.

§25 What breaks

Three failures specific to this architecture, each with a measurable signature.

Pitfall: loss spikes

Training proceeds smoothly for thousands of steps and the loss jumps by a large factor in one step. Sometimes it recovers over hundreds of steps; sometimes it never does.

The mechanism is Part IX §9's cliff. The associated signature is a sudden growth in the attention logits (the scores before the softmax) which saturates it, which zeroes its gradient (§4), which leaves the layer stuck.

The standard mitigations are all about keeping the logits bounded: QK normalisation, which normalises queries and keys before the dot product; a lower $\beta_2$; and clipping. Log the maximum attention logit: it is one number per layer and it moves before the loss does.

Pitfall: attention entropy collapse

§4 showed the softmax saturating at initialisation if the scores are not scaled. The same thing can happen during training: the logits grow, the entropy of the attention distribution falls toward zero, and heads become hard pointers with no gradient.

It is cheap to watch. The entropy of each head's attention distribution is a scalar per head per layer, and a head whose entropy has collapsed to near zero has stopped learning where to look.

§26 Rank collapse, and what the skip connections are really for

The third failure is the most interesting, because it establishes that one of §14's components is not optional.

Pure self-attention, stacked, converges to a rank-one representation: every position's vector becomes the same vector, exponentially fast in depth. The model loses the ability to distinguish positions at all.

Why attention alone collapses, and why the residual stops it

Attention's output is a convex combination of the values: each output lies inside the convex hull of its inputs. Applying that repeatedly is an averaging process, and averaging applied over and over converges to a constant. This is Part XI §11's power method wearing different clothes: repeated application of a stochastic operator drives everything to its leading eigenvector.

The residual connection breaks it because $\mathbf{x} + f(\mathbf{x})$ is not a convex combination: the original vector is preserved in full and the averaged version is added on top. The differences between positions survive to every depth.

So skip connections in a transformer are doing two separate jobs, and only one of them was in Part X §23. They shorten the gradient path, as there. And they prevent the forward representation from degenerating, which is a statement about expressiveness rather than optimisation: a transformer without them does not merely train badly, it computes something trivial.

The feedforward layers help for the same reason: they are not convex combinations either, which is a second answer to §15's question about why two thirds of the model is the part nobody discusses.
