Part BStructural causal models

Movement A ended on a problem: whether to adjust for a variable depends on its causal role, and the data cannot tell you what that is. This movement builds the language for saying it, a model of the mechanism rather than the distribution, and derives from it exactly when an interventional quantity can be computed from observational data, and when it cannot.

§8 A model of the mechanism

The structural causal model

An SCM is a set of variables, and for each one an equation giving its value as a function of its direct causes and an independent noise term:

$$V_i := f_i(\mathrm{pa}_i, \varepsilon_i), \qquad \varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n \text{ independent}$$

The assignment symbol is doing real work. $V_i := f_i(\cdot)$ says this is how $V_i$ is produced, which is directional in a way that $=$ is not. Figure 1's system was three such equations, and knowing them is what made the true effect a thing the script could set rather than estimate.

What the model buys that a distribution does not

A joint distribution answers questions about the world as it is. An SCM additionally answers questions about worlds you can construct from it, because the equations are modular: you can replace one and leave the rest alone.

That modularity is the entire content of the causal assumption. It says the mechanism producing $Y$ from its parents keeps working the same way when you reach in and change how $X$ is produced. It is an assumption, it is often approximately true, and it is what everything downstream rests on.

§9 Graphs, and what an arrow means

Draw one node per variable and an arrow from each direct cause to its effect. The result is a directed acyclic graph, and it is a lossy but sufficient summary of the SCM for most of what follows: the identification results in §12 and §13 depend on the graph alone, not on the functional forms.

What the graph asserts, and what it does not

An arrow $X \rightarrow Y$ asserts that $X$ may directly affect $Y$. It says nothing about sign, size or shape.

A missing arrow is the strong claim. It asserts there is no direct effect. The graph's content is almost entirely in its absences, which is why drawing one is uncomfortable and why doing it is useful.

The acyclicity asserts no feedback loops at the timescale modelled. Systems with genuine feedback need a different treatment.

Draw it before you fit anything

Take a model you have built and draw the graph you believe generated the data. It will take ten minutes and it will be uncomfortable, because it forces you to commit to claims you have been leaving implicit. Then ask, for each variable in your feature set, whether it is a confounder, a mediator, a collider, or a descendant of the outcome. Part XIII §11's leakage is the last of these, and the graph makes it visible rather than something you discover in production.

§10 Reading dependence off the graph

The three-node vocabulary, and d-separation

Every path between two nodes is built from three pieces, and each behaves differently under conditioning.

Chain $X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$: open. Conditioning on $M$ closes it.

Fork $X \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y$: open. Conditioning on $U$ closes it.

Collider $X \rightarrow C \leftarrow Y$: closed. Conditioning on $C$, or on any descendant of it, opens it.

Two nodes are d-separated by a set $Z$ when every path between them is closed given $Z$. D-separation implies conditional independence in the distribution, which is what makes the graph testable.

The collider's reversal is the rule that catches people, and Figure 2 is it: the path $X \rightarrow C \leftarrow Y$ is closed, so $X$ and $Y$ are independent, until you condition on $C$ and open it. The measured correlation goes from −0.003 to −0.519 for that reason and no other.

§11 The do-operator

Intervention, defined by surgery on the model

$\mathrm{do}(X = x)$ means: delete the equation that produced $X$, replace it with the constant $x$, and leave every other equation untouched. The resulting distribution is $p(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x))$.

Deleting the equation is what cuts the confounding path. $U$ still causes $Y$, but it no longer causes $X$, because nothing does: you set it. That is precisely what a randomised experiment achieves physically, and §16 is the same statement from the practical end.

Why $p(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) \ne p(y \mid x)$ in general

Conditioning selects a subpopulation: the units that happened to have $X = x$. Those units differ systematically from the rest, because something made $X$ take that value, and that something may also affect $Y$.

Intervening creates a different population: everyone, with $X$ set. The two coincide exactly when nothing but $X$'s own noise determined $X$, which is what randomisation arranges and what observational data almost never has.

§12 The backdoor criterion

When adjustment works, exactly

A set $Z$ satisfies the backdoor criterion for $(X, Y)$ when

1. no node in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$, and
2. $Z$ blocks every path between $X$ and $Y$ that starts with an arrow into $X$.

Then the interventional distribution is computable from observational data:

$$p(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) = \sum_z p(y \mid x, z)\, p(z)$$

This is the adjustment formula, and it is what Figure 1's green line computes. Note what it is not: it is not $p(y \mid x, z)$ averaged over $p(z \mid x)$, which would give you back the conditional. The weights are the marginal distribution of $Z$: the population as a whole, not the population that received $x$.

What each condition rules out

Condition 1 excludes mediators and colliders downstream of the treatment. It is why "adjust for everything" fails: descendants of $X$ are exactly the variables that must not be adjusted for.

Condition 2 requires the confounding to be measured. Paths into $X$ are the backdoors, the routes by which association flows without causation, and every one must be blocked by something you observed.

The second condition is the one that fails in practice, and it fails silently. There is no test for an unmeasured confounder, which is why §22's sensitivity analysis exists and why §26's instruments are worth their cost.

§13 The front-door criterion

The backdoor criterion needs the confounder measured. Remarkably, that is not always necessary: if the effect travels entirely through a mediator you can measure, and nothing confounds that mediator, the effect is identified even with the confounder completely unobserved.

Front-door adjustment

For $X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$ with an unobserved $U$ confounding $X$ and $Y$ but not $M$:

$$p(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) = \sum_m p(m \mid x) \sum_{x'} p(y \mid m, x')\, p(x')$$

The logic is two backdoor arguments chained. The effect of $X$ on $M$ is unconfounded because $U$ does not touch $M$. The effect of $M$ on $Y$ is confounded by $U$, but $X$ blocks that backdoor. Multiply the two.

Pitfall: the assumption it needs is very strong

Front-door requires that all of the effect passes through the measured mediator, and that nothing confounds the mediator with the outcome. Both are untestable, and the first is usually false: most treatments have more than one pathway.

It is included here because it makes a conceptual point worth more than its practical use: identification is a property of the graph, not of which variables happen to be observed. A quantity can be recoverable without the confounder ever being measured, and unrecoverable with a great many things measured.

§14 Identifiability, and when the answer is no

Identifiable

A causal quantity is identifiable from a graph and an observational distribution when it can be written as a function of that distribution: when any two SCMs consistent with the graph that agree on the observables also agree on the quantity.

Backdoor and front-door are two sufficient conditions. The general question is settled: there is a complete algorithm (do-calculus, three rules) that returns an expression when one exists and proves none exists otherwise.

The result that matters most, and it is a negative one

For some graphs the answer is no, and this is a theorem, not a limitation of current methods. No estimator, no sample size and no amount of computation recovers the effect. The bivariate case with an unmeasured common cause, $X \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y$ with $X \rightarrow Y$, is the canonical example: nothing in the joint distribution of $X$ and $Y$ separates the effect from the confounding.

This is the most useful thing in the movement. It converts "we need better data" or "we need a bigger model", both of which are wrong, into a precise statement: you need a different design. That is what movement D is about.

Go and read

Pearl (2009), Causality, chapter 3. The do-calculus and the completeness results. Dense, and the three rules repay the effort because they turn "can this be estimated" from a judgement call into a decision procedure.

§15 What movement B establishes

Three things

Causal assumptions are made in a language, not extracted from data. The graph states what causes what; its content is in the arrows it omits; and everything downstream is a consequence of it.

Whether adjustment works is decidable from the graph. The backdoor criterion says exactly which sets suffice, and it explicitly forbids adjusting for descendants of the treatment: the formal version of §4's warning.

Sometimes the answer is no, provably. Identifiability is a property of the graph, and for some graphs no estimator exists. That is a design problem, and recognising it early is worth more than any modelling effort spent on an unidentifiable quantity.
