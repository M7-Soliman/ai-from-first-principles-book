Part AThe setting

§1 What changes

In 60 seconds

Supervised learning has a fixed dataset and a loss you can evaluate on any example. Here the learner acts, its actions determine what it observes next, and the reward for a decision may arrive a hundred steps later mixed with the consequences of everything else it did. The dataset is a consequence of the policy, and the policy is what you are trying to learn.

Four difficulties, and every method is a response to one

Credit assignment. A reward arrives; which of the preceding decisions earned it? Part VII §14's backpropagation solves this within a computation, where the graph is known. Here the graph runs through the environment and you do not have it.

Exploration. You only observe the outcome of what you actually did. Data about the alternative does not exist unless you go and get it, and getting it costs something.

Non-stationarity. As the policy improves, the distribution of states it visits changes. The training distribution moves because you moved it: Part XIV §26's distribution shift, caused by the learner rather than the world.

Delayed and sparse signal. One scalar per episode, sometimes, carrying information about thousands of decisions.

§2 The Markov decision process

The formalism

States $\mathcal{S}$, actions $\mathcal{A}$, a transition kernel $p(s' \mid s, a)$, a reward function $r(s, a)$, and a discount $\gamma \in [0, 1)$.

The Markov property is the whole content of the name: the next state depends on the current state and action alone, not on how you got there. It is not a claim about the world; it is a claim about your state representation, and it is satisfied by definition if the state contains everything relevant.

Which relocates the difficulty rather than removing it. When a problem is not Markov in your chosen state (a game where you cannot see the whole board, a robot with one camera), the honest description is a partially observable MDP, and the standard response is to make the state a summary of the history, which is Part XI's recurrence doing a job.

§3 Return, and why it is discounted

What is being maximised

$$G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^{k} r_{t+k}$$

Three reasons for $\gamma < 1$, and only one of them is about preference. It makes the sum finite for an unbounded horizon. It makes the Bellman operator a contraction, which is what §12 measures and what every convergence result rests on. And it expresses a genuine preference for sooner over later.

The effective horizon is about $1/(1-\gamma)$ steps: $\gamma = 0.99$ looks roughly a hundred steps ahead. That is the number to reason with, not $\gamma$ itself.

Pitfall: $\gamma$ is a hyperparameter pretending to be part of the problem

It is usually presented as a property of the task. In practice it is tuned, and it changes what the optimal policy is, not merely how fast you find it. A smaller $\gamma$ makes a genuinely long-horizon problem into a short-horizon one that is easier to solve and answers a different question.

So report it. A result at $\gamma = 0.9$ and one at $\gamma = 0.999$ are results about different problems, and the second is far harder for the reason §12 makes exact.

§4 Policies and value functions

Three objects

A policy $\pi(a \mid s)$: what to do. A state value $V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid s_t = s]$: how good it is to be here, following $\pi$. An action value $Q^\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid s_t = s, a_t = a]$: how good it is to do this here, then follow $\pi$.

$Q$ is the more useful of the two for control, and the reason is worth stating: given $Q$ you can act greedily by comparing actions, with no model. Given only $V$ you would need $p(s' \mid s,a)$ to know where each action leads. That single difference is why value-based control learns $Q$.

§5 The Bellman equations

Consistency, and then optimality

For a fixed policy, value decomposes into one step plus the rest:

$$V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\Big[r(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} p(s'\mid s,a) V^\pi(s')\Big]$$

This is a linear system in $V^\pi$, $|\mathcal{S}|$ equations in $|\mathcal{S}|$ unknowns, so it can be solved directly. That is worth noticing, because it means policy evaluation is not an approximation at all when the model is known.

The optimality equation replaces the average over actions with a maximum:

$$V^*(s) = \max_a \Big[r(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} p(s'\mid s,a) V^*(s')\Big]$$

and that max makes it nonlinear, which is why the optimal case needs iteration where the evaluation case does not.

Why one deterministic policy always suffices

A fact that saves a great deal of worry: for any finite MDP there exists an optimal policy that is deterministic and stationary: no randomisation, no dependence on time.

The argument is short. $V^*$ satisfies the optimality equation, and acting greedily with respect to it achieves $V^*$; a greedy policy is deterministic. So randomisation buys nothing at the optimum.

Which raises the obvious question: why does the rest of this part use stochastic policies constantly? Three reasons, all practical rather than theoretical: randomness is how you explore (§33), a stochastic policy is differentiable in its parameters where a greedy one is not (§24), and in partially observed or multi-agent settings the guarantee does not apply.

§6 The taxonomy

Three axes that organise everything after this

Model-based or model-free. Do you know, or learn, $p(s'\mid s,a)$? A model lets you plan without acting, and learning one accurately is often harder than learning the policy.

Value-based or policy-based. Learn $Q$ and act greedily, or parameterise $\pi$ and improve it directly. Movement C is the first, movement D the second, and actor–critic is both.

On-policy or off-policy. Does the data have to come from the policy being improved? Off-policy methods can reuse old data and learn from other agents; they are also where the divergence of §21 comes from.

§7 The unit of measurement

Pitfall: the reward is not the objective

Part XIV §33 said the loss is not the metric and the metric is not the goal. Reinforcement learning has the same gap with sharper consequences, because the agent optimises the reward with full freedom over its own data and will find whatever the reward literally says.

§39 measures a case. The general form: a reward function is a specification, it is written by a person, and any gap between it and what was wanted is a gap the optimiser is being paid to find.

§8 What this part will and will not cover

Scope

Covered: the MDP and its exact solution; learning values from samples; the policy gradient family; exploration; and the applications that made this part necessary: reward design, offline learning, learning from human feedback, and training for reasoning.

Not covered: multi-agent settings and game theory, control theory's continuous-time treatment, and the large literature on model-based planning. Each is a subject rather than a section, and pretending otherwise would produce a worse account than declining.
