Part AWhy depth

§1 The problem that stopped the field

In 60 seconds

A linear model draws one straight boundary. Some problems need more: the smallest is XOR, four points that no line separates. The fix is not a cleverer line but an extra layer: transform the input first, then draw the line in the transformed space. That single move is what "hidden layer" means.

Go and read

Rosenblatt (1958), The perceptron: a probabilistic model for information storage. Where trainable classifiers begin, and worth reading beside the press coverage it attracted. Then Minsky & Papert (1969), Perceptrons, the critique this section is about. Read enough of it to see that it is correct and carefully limited to single layers, which makes the fifteen years that followed a fact about how the field read it rather than about what it said.

Take four points and a rule: output 1 when exactly one input is on.

Try to separate the ones from the zeros with a straight line. You cannot; the two positive points sit on opposite corners, with a negative point on either side of any line you draw. A linear model of the form $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$ cannot represent this function at all, at any setting of its parameters.

History: the four points that caused a winter

Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron, from 1958, was a trainable linear classifier with a learning rule guaranteed to converge if the data was linearly separable. It generated enormous excitement, some of it wildly overstated in the press of the time.

In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published a careful mathematical analysis of what perceptrons could and could not do. Their central example was XOR: a function of two binary inputs, trivially simple, and provably beyond a single linear unit.

The critique was correct. What it did not establish (and what was widely taken from it) was that layered networks were equally limited. Minsky and Papert noted the multi-layer case was open; nobody had a training algorithm for it, which was the real obstacle.

Funding and interest collapsed regardless, and neural network research entered roughly fifteen lean years now called the first AI winter. The training algorithm that resolved it (backpropagation, §14) existed in other fields for much of that period, unconnected to the problem it would solve.

§2 What a hidden layer actually does

The resolution is visible in the middle panel of Figure 1, and it is worth being precise about.

The network computes

$$\mathbf{h} = g\big(W^{(1)}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)}\big), \qquad \hat{y} = \sigma\big(\mathbf{w}^{(2)\top}\mathbf{h} + b^{(2)}\big)$$

The second line is exactly logistic regression from Part VI §13, an ordinary linear classifier. The difference is that it does not act on $\mathbf{x}$. It acts on $\mathbf{h}$, a learned representation of $\mathbf{x}$.

Intuition: learn the features, then draw the line

Part VI §12 noted that linear models become nonlinear once you feed them the right features, and that the limitation was never curvature; it was that you had to choose the features.

A hidden layer chooses them for you. The first layer learns a transformation that makes the problem linearly separable; the last layer solves the now-easy problem. Depth is automated feature engineering, and that sentence is close to the whole idea.

Pitfall: without the nonlinearity, depth buys nothing

If $g$ were the identity, the network would compute $W^{(2)}W^{(1)}\mathbf{x} = W\mathbf{x}$, one matrix, by associativity. A stack of linear maps is a linear map, so a hundred layers would still only draw one straight boundary.

This was Part I's drill S6, and it is the formal reason the nonlinearity is not optional. Everything depth provides comes from $g$ being something other than the identity.

§3 Universal approximation

In 60 seconds

A network with one hidden layer, given enough units, can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region to any accuracy you like. That result sounds like it ends the discussion. It does not, and understanding why is more useful than the theorem itself.

Go and read

Cybenko (1989), Approximation by superpositions of a sigmoidal function. Short, and worth reading precisely to see how little it promises: that a sufficient width exists, with no bound on it, no procedure for finding the weights, and no claim that gradient descent could. Hornik (1991) generalises it beyond sigmoids. The gap between what these theorems say and what they are usually taken to say is the subject of the rest of this section.

Why this matters less than it appears

The theorem is an existence result, and it is silent on all three things you actually need.

How many units? Possibly exponentially many in the input dimension. "Finite" is a weak promise.

Can training find it? The theorem says a good setting of the weights exists. It does not say gradient descent will reach it, and the loss surface is not convex.

Will it generalise? A network that can represent any function can represent the noise too. Part VI §7 already established that unrestricted flexibility is not an asset.

So universal approximation rules out one worry (that networks are fundamentally too weak) and leaves every practical question open. It is a licence, not a method.

§4 Why depth rather than width

If one hidden layer suffices in principle, why does anyone use fifty?

Because "in principle" hides a cost. The same total budget of units, arranged deeper, buys far more structure than arranged wider.

Intuition: folding paper

Each layer can fold the input space, and a later layer folds the already-folded result. Fold a sheet of paper once and cut it, and you get two cuts; fold it ten times and one cut produces a thousand. The folds compose multiplicatively.

A wide shallow network must draw every region separately, so its capacity grows with the number of units. A deep one reuses the earlier folds, so its capacity grows with the product across layers. That is the same compositional prior Part VI §22 argued was necessary, expressed as geometry.

There is a second argument, and in practice it may matter more. Depth encourages reuse. A feature computed in an early layer (an edge, a phoneme, a syntactic relation) is available to every later layer and to every downstream computation. A shallow network must rediscover it independently wherever it is needed.

Pitfall: more depth is not free

Everything above is about what a deep network can represent. Whether you can train it is a separate question, and for a long time the answer was no.

Section 18 shows the mechanism: gradients passing through many layers are multiplied by one factor per layer, and products of many factors either vanish or explode. Depth was understood to be desirable well before it was achievable, and the intervening work (better activations, better initialisation, normalisation, residual connections) was about making the gradient survive the trip.

Try it

You have a budget of 64 hidden units. Sketch the argument for spending them as one layer of 64, versus four layers of 16. What does each buy, and what does each risk?

Answer. One layer of 64: guaranteed trainable, as gradients pass through a single layer, so nothing vanishes. Capacity grows linearly in the units, so the network can carve roughly 64 regions and must learn each independently.

Four layers of 16: far greater representational capacity for the same parameter count, because the folds compose, and features learned early are reused throughout. The risk is the gradient path: four layers of multiplication, and if the activations or the initialisation are poor, the early layers may receive almost nothing.

In practice the deeper arrangement wins, provided you use the activations of §7 and the initialisation of §11. Those two sections are what converted the depth argument from theory into practice, and before them the shallow answer was frequently correct.
