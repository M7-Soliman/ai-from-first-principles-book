Part FForecasting

A forecast is not a number. It is a distribution, at a horizon, over a population of series that usually have to add up, and the reporting conventions that make it useful are as specific as anything in Part XXI.

§39 The horizon is part of the question

One model per horizon, or one model recursed

Direct. Fit a separate model for each horizon, each predicting $y_{t+h}$ from information available at $t$. No error compounding, and $H$ models to fit and maintain.

Recursive. Fit one one-step model and feed its own predictions back. Cheap, and errors compound: the model was trained on true inputs and is run on its own outputs, which is Part XVI §12's exposure bias in a different costume.

Direct is usually better at long horizons and recursive at short ones. Reporting a single accuracy number without saying which horizon it refers to is meaningless, and it is common.

§40 What the intervals do along the horizon

What to do about it

The guarantee is unavailable, so the interval is an estimate like any other and has to be checked rather than assumed: Part XXIV §40's coverage measurement, run on a rolling basis and reported alongside the forecast.

Two partial repairs. Recalibrate on the most recent window rather than on all history, which tracks a slowly changing process. And widen deliberately: if measured coverage is 0.75 at a nominal 0.90, inflate the band until the measurement says 0.90 and report that you did.

What is not acceptable is quoting the nominal number. The measurement is a line of code and the gap here is fifteen points.

§41 Probabilistic forecasts, and how to score them

Beyond the point

Most forecasting decisions are asymmetric (a stockout costs differently from overstock, which is Part XXIV §33's threshold), so the useful output is a predictive distribution, usually as a set of quantiles.

Pinball loss scores a quantile forecast and is minimised by the true quantile, making it a proper scoring rule for that quantile. The continuous ranked probability score scores the whole distribution and reduces to absolute error when the forecast is a point, which makes point and distributional forecasts comparable on one scale.

Report one of these. Reporting only a mean absolute error on the median discards the part of the forecast the decision actually uses.

§42 Hierarchies

When the forecasts have to add up

Sales by store sum to sales by region, which sum to the total. Forecast each level independently and they will not reconcile, and an inconsistent set of numbers is unusable for planning even when each is individually good.

The naive repairs are to forecast the bottom and add up, or forecast the top and apportion. Both discard information. Optimal reconciliation projects the independent forecasts onto the space of coherent ones, and it typically improves accuracy at every level, because the levels' errors are informative about each other.

Worth knowing because the constraint is extremely common and the default handling is to notice the mismatch in a meeting.

§43 Change points and anomalies

Two different questions

Anomaly. This observation is unusual. Answerable against a predictive distribution: it is a point in the tail, and §41's machinery supplies the tail.

Change point. The process itself has changed and everything after this is from a different distribution. Answerable only with a delay, because a change is not distinguishable from a run of unusual observations until enough of them accumulate.

Conflating them is how monitoring produces either an alert per outlier or a regime change noticed a month late. Part XXIII §36's tracking regret is the formal version of the second, and Part XXI §35's monitoring is where both belong.

§44 When a neural forecaster earns its place

Go and read

Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos (2020), The M4 competition: 100,000 time series. What actually won, at scale, when 100,000 series were forecast by every method willing to compete: the top entry was a hybrid of exponential smoothing and a neural network, and combinations of simple statistical methods held their own against far more elaborate ones. The finding behind why §35's baselines are baselines rather than history.

Three conditions, and they are about the data

Many related series. A global model fitted across thousands of series can share structure that no single-series method can, and this is where the modern architectures win clearly.

Covariates that matter. Prices, promotions, weather, holidays: a classical model accommodates these awkwardly and a learned one naturally.

Long or non-linear dependence that a linear model cannot express.

Absent all three, a well-tuned exponential smoothing model is the thing to beat, and frequently is not beaten. Report it as a baseline and the comparison becomes informative rather than decorative.

Named: DeepAR, N-BEATS and the Temporal Fusion Transformer are the architectures usually meant by "modern" above; Prophet is the popular decomposition-based alternative, closer in spirit to §35's classical models than to these three.

§45 What to carry forward

Five things

The prior is what matters, and a table's prior is axis-aligned. Figure 7 splits four comparisons two–two by the shape of the generating function, not by the data's format. Trees represent thresholds exactly and hyperplanes as staircases; networks are the reverse.

Averaging removes variance and boosting removes bias, and they want opposite base learners: deep trees for one, shallow for the other. Figure 4 shows boosting's two hyperparameters are effectively one: fix the rate low, early-stop the count.

Calibration follows the loss. A model fitted to a proper scoring rule comes out calibrated; a vote share does not, and a monotone calibrator repairs it while moving no ranking.

The split decides more than the model. Figure 8's random split on a time series reports a model 18.8× better than the one you have, with every conventional diagnostic passing, because the estimand was wrong rather than the estimator.

A forecast is a distribution at a horizon. Figure 9's intervals under-cover at every horizon including the first, because a series is not exchangeable with its own past, so measure the coverage and widen until it holds, rather than quoting the nominal number.

Sixty seconds

Start with gradient boosting on any table: it handles mixed types, missing values and unscaled columns, and its two main hyperparameters collapse to one. Check whether your scores are probabilities before anyone acts on them, and calibrate on a split the model never saw. Then spend most of your care on the split itself: by time if there is a date, by entity if rows share one, with every encoder and scaler inside the fold. If the rows are ordered, nothing you learned about independent samples applies: a random split will hand you a number that is off by an order of magnitude and no diagnostic will object.
