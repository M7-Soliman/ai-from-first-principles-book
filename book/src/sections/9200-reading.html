Reading the literature

At some point the textbooks stop and the papers begin. That transition is where most self-taught people stall, not because the papers are too hard, but because nobody explains that they are read differently from everything else, and for different purposes.

§1 Papers are not read linearly

A paper is not a chapter. It is a compressed argument written for reviewers, under a page limit, by authors who assume you already know the field. Reading one from the first word to the last is the slowest possible approach and usually ends in abandonment.

The standard technique is three passes, each with a different goal. It is worth adopting deliberately.

The three-pass method
Pass one: five to ten minutes. Should I read this at all?
Title, abstract, section headings, every figure and its caption, the conclusion. Skip the body entirely. You are answering: what problem, what claim, does it concern me? Most papers stop here, and that is the correct outcome.
Pass two: about an hour. What did they do?
Read the body, but skip proofs and derivations. Look hard at the experiments: what was the baseline, what was held fixed, what does the main table actually compare? You should end able to summarise the method and its evidence to someone else.
Pass three: several hours. Could I have written this?
Reconstruct the work. Re-derive the key result, challenge every assumption, note what you would have done differently. Reserve this for papers you intend to build on, but do it properly a handful of times, because it is what turns reading into ability.

The three-pass framing is due to Srinivasan Keshav, whose short note How to Read a Paper (2007) is itself a ten-minute read and worth doing first.

§2 Read the figures before the text

In an empirical paper the figures and tables are the argument; the prose explains and defends them. Reading captions first tells you what the authors think they showed, in about ninety seconds.

It also protects you. Prose can imply more than the evidence supports: a phrase like "substantially outperforms" is doing rhetorical work that the table may not back. Looking at the numbers first means you form your own impression before being told what to think of it.

§3 The questions to ask

Everything in Part V applies to papers, and applying it is the difference between reading and evaluating. The questions below will feel mechanical the first few times; with practice they become automatic.

AskBecause
What exactly is the claim?Stated precisely, it is usually narrower than the title suggests.
What is the baseline, and was it tuned?An untuned baseline is the most common way to manufacture an improvement. Part VI §11.
Is the difference larger than the noise?Look for intervals or seed variation. If there are none, you cannot tell. Part V §16.
How many configurations were tried?The reported best of a large search is biased upward. Part V §14.
What is not compared?The omitted baseline is often the informative one.
What would falsify this?If nothing would, it is not an empirical claim.
Does the ablation isolate the contribution?A method with five changes and one aggregate number tells you nothing about which change mattered.
Pitfall: the failure modes to expect

Baseline neglect. The proposed method got a hyperparameter search; the baseline got defaults. This is common and frequently accounts for the entire reported gap.

Test set as validation. Architectural choices made by looking at test performance, then that same number reported. Part V §15.

Single seed. One run, no variance estimate. On many benchmarks the seed-to-seed spread exceeds the improvements being claimed.

Cherry-picked datasets. Strong results on four benchmarks, with no statement of how many were tried.

None of these mean the work is wrong. They mean the evidence is weaker than presented, and noticing is the skill.

§4 Read old papers

There is a strong pull toward reading whatever appeared last month. It is worth resisting, for three reasons.

Older papers are frequently clearer. They were written when the idea was new, so the authors had to explain it rather than assume it. Shannon's 1948 paper builds information theory from nothing and is more readable than most textbook treatments of it. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's 1997 paper derives the LSTM from first principles in a way that no subsequent survey reproduces.

They tell you what problem the idea solved. A technique encountered in a modern survey arrives as a component with no motivation. In the original it is the answer to a question someone urgently needed answered, which is the same argument the history boxes throughout this book make.

And they show you what was not obvious. Reading a foundational paper reveals the alternatives that were live at the time and the reasons they lost. That is the part textbooks compress out entirely, and it is where the most useful understanding lives.

§5 The reading list, gathered

Every paper below has already appeared in a Go and read panel at the point in the book where its idea is developed. That is the intended way to meet them: a paper read the week you first need it lands differently from one read off a list, because you already know the question it answers.

This is the index. One or two primary sources per major idea, arranged by part, so that you can find one again, survey the foundations of a part before starting it, or plan a stretch of reading without hunting through the chapters.

Part 0: Mathematics and automatic differentiation

Linnainmaa (1970): the general reverse-mode algorithm Part 0 §17
Automatic differentiation stated in general form, as a master's thesis, fifteen years before backpropagation was popularised. The chain rule of §17, carefully organised and analysed for error accumulation. Read it to see how fully the idea was worked out before the field knew it needed it.
Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986): Learning representations by back-propagating errors Part 0 §24
The paper that made backpropagation widely known and sparked the second connectionist era. Short, readable, and the citation most people mean when they say "backpropagation was invented in 1986," though as §24's history box notes, not the first derivation of the algorithm. Read it for the representations argument: the point is not the algorithm but what it learns.
Baydin, Pearlmutter, Radul & Siskind (2018): Automatic differentiation in machine learning: a survey Part VII §13
The clearest modern account of forward versus reverse mode and what frameworks actually do. Read before writing an autograd engine.
Goldberg (1991): What every computer scientist should know about floating-point arithmetic Part 0 §26
Long, thorough, and the definitive treatment of Part 0 §26 and Part III's Part A.

Part I: Linear algebra

Pearson (1901); Hotelling (1933) Part I §23
The two origins of principal components: Pearson's geometric derivation as best-fitting lines and planes, and Hotelling's formulation via the covariance eigenvectors, which is the derivation in §23. The thirty-two years between them reflect how long it took to see that the two approaches are the same thing.
Eckart & Young (1936) Part I §19
The optimality theorem: truncating the SVD gives the provably best low-rank approximation. Short, and the result everything in §19 rests on.
Golub & Kahan (1965) Part I §19
The first numerically stable SVD algorithm: the reason a theorem from 1873 became usable technology.
Hu et al. (2021): LoRA: Low-rank adaptation of large language models Part I §11
Rank, applied. The modern payoff of §11, and a good example of an old idea becoming load-bearing again.

Part II: Probability and information theory

Shannon (1948): A mathematical theory of communication Part II §23
Read this one. An entire field, arriving nearly complete in a single paper, written with unusual clarity. The proofs are elementary; the ideas are not. If you read one paper from this list, make it this one. The Bell System Technical Journal version is freely available and includes Warren Weaver's accessible companion essay.
Kullback & Leibler (1951): On information and sufficiency Part II §24
The origin of the divergence in §24, framed as discriminating between hypotheses.
Jaynes (1957): Information theory and statistical mechanics Part II §15
The maximum entropy principle, the second of §15's three justifications for the Gaussian, argued properly.
Metropolis & Ulam (1949): The Monte Carlo method Part II §10
The original statement of the technique, two pages, from the people who named it. The named it after Monaco's casino because Ulam's uncle gambled there. Short enough to read in the time it takes to decide whether to read it.
Tversky & Kahneman (1974): Judgment under uncertainty: heuristics and biases Part II §22
Why §22's base rate result fools nearly everyone, including experts, along with the cognitive mechanisms behind it. Not a mathematics paper; one of the most cited in psychology; and among the most practically useful things on this list, because the errors it describes are not corrected by knowing about them.

Part III: Numerical computation and optimisation

Turing (1948): Rounding-off errors in matrix processes Part III §5
Where the condition number was named, and where conditioning was first separated from algorithmic stability.
Robbins & Monro (1951): A stochastic approximation method Part III §14
The ancestor of SGD, written for the problem of finding a root of a function observed with noise (with no connection to machine learning). The step-size conditions proved here ($\sum\alpha_t = \infty$, $\sum\alpha_t^2 < \infty$) are the theoretical reason learning rate schedules decay rather than staying constant.
Polyak (1964); Nesterov (1983) Part III §15
Momentum, and its accelerated variant. The theory behind §15.
Duchi, Hazan & Singer (2011): AdaGrad; Kingma & Ba (2015): Adam Part III §16
The adaptive gradient methods of §16, in their original form. AdaGrad accumulates squared gradients without forgetting, which works for sparse problems and fails on non-convex ones. Adam corrects this with exponential decay. Adam's paper is unusually readable for an optimisation paper; the convergence proof in it, however, contains an error; see Reddi et al. below.
Reddi, Kale & Kumar (2018): On the convergence of Adam and beyond Part III §16
The paper that found the error in Adam's proof and constructed a counterexample. Read it alongside the original: the pair is an education in how this field actually operates.
Loshchilov & Hutter (2019): Decoupled weight decay regularization Part III §17
Why weight decay and $L^2$ regularization are not the same thing once an adaptive optimiser is involved. A precise, useful correction.

Part IV: The framework

Paszke et al. (2019): PyTorch: an imperative style, high-performance deep learning library Part IV §5
The design paper for the library this part describes. Worth reading for the argument rather than the API: it is an explicit defence of building the graph as the code runs, against the then-dominant define-then-run approach, and it is candid about what that choice costs in optimisation opportunities.
Abadi et al. (2016): TensorFlow: a system for large-scale machine learning Part IV §5
The other side of that argument, from the system that made the opposite choice. Read the two together and the design space becomes visible; read either alone and its decisions look inevitable.
Micikevicius et al. (2018): Mixed precision training Part IV §2
Why 16-bit training works and what has to be done to make it safe: loss scaling, and keeping a 32-bit copy of the weights. It is Part III §1's precision-versus-range trade worked out at production scale, and it explains why bfloat16 later displaced the float16 scheme this paper describes.

Part V: Statistics and estimation

Student [Gosset] (1908): The probable error of a mean Part V §7
The $t$-distribution, published under a pseudonym for the reasons in §7's history box.
Efron (1979): Bootstrap methods: another look at the jackknife Part V §10
The bootstrap, introduced. Notable for how simple the idea is once stated.
Benjamini & Hochberg (1995): controlling the false discovery rate Part V §14
The practical alternative to Bonferroni when screening many hypotheses. Drill S4.
Ioannidis (2005): Why most published research findings are false Part V §14
Multiple comparisons, publication incentives and low power, combined into an argument about an entire literature. Directly applicable to benchmark-driven fields.
Wasserstein & Lazar (2016): the ASA statement on p-values Part V §12
The American Statistical Association stating plainly, in an official statement, what a p-value is not. Two pages with six numbered principles. Worth reading because the misconceptions it corrects are universal and because the authority of the source makes it useful to cite when disagreeing with someone who should know better.
Dwork et al. (2015): The reusable holdout Part V §15
The formal treatment of §15's test-set problem, with an actual mechanism for surviving adaptive reuse.
Belkin, Hsu, Ma & Mandal (2019); Nakkiran et al. (2019) Part V §20
Double descent, named and then demonstrated across modern architectures. The papers behind §20's caveat.

Part VI: Machine learning

Wolpert (1996): The lack of a priori distinctions between learning algorithms Part VI §7
No free lunch, stated carefully. Worth reading because the theorem is more often invoked than understood.
Cover & Hart (1967): Nearest neighbor pattern classification Part VI §16
The surprising result that 1-NN's asymptotic error is at most twice the best possible.
Boser, Guyon & Vapnik (1992); Cortes & Vapnik (1995) Part VI §14
The kernel trick and support vector networks: the two papers behind §14 and §15.
Breiman (2001): Random forests Part VI §16
Ensembling as variance reduction, done properly.
Breiman (2001): Statistical modeling: the two cultures Part VI §16
An essay, not a method. On the split between modelling the data-generating process and predicting well without one. It predicted the shape of the following two decades and is the best single argument for why this field looks the way it does.
Domingos (2012): A few useful things to know about machine learning Part VI §7
Practical wisdom, densely packed. Much of Part VI's Section 7 and Section 21 in short form.
Zhang et al. (2017): Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization Part VI §6
Networks trained on randomly labelled data fit the training set perfectly, which means no classical capacity measure (VC dimension, Rademacher complexity, uniform stability) can explain why the same architectures generalise on real labels. The paper that made the puzzle explicit and forced the field to look for a different account.
Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux (2022): why tree-based models still outperform deep learning on tabular data Part VI §16
A careful, honest answer to the question §16 raises: when should you not use a neural network? The inductive biases that favour trees on tabular data are identified and tested individually, which is the right way to answer the question rather than the common way.
Sculley et al. (2015): Hidden technical debt in machine learning systems Part VI §20
What happens to these systems after the paper is written. Read once you have deployed something.

Part VII: Neural networks

Rosenblatt (1958): The perceptron Part VII §1
Where trainable linear classifiers begin, along with the enthusiasm (and the overclaims) that followed. Read it as a historical document and notice how much of the modern understanding of learning is already present, and how much is confidently wrong.
Minsky & Papert (1969): Perceptrons Part VII §1
The book that proved single-layer perceptrons cannot compute parity or XOR, and whose conclusion (that neural networks were fundamentally limited) was correct about single layers and wrong about networks in general. Widely blamed for the first AI winter. Read the actual argument rather than the reputation.
Cybenko (1989); Hornik (1991) Part VII §3
The universal approximation theorems of §3. Cybenko proved the result for sigmoid activations; Hornik extended it to a broad class of non-constant, bounded, continuous functions. Short, and worth reading precisely to see how little they promise: the guarantees say nothing about how many units are needed, whether gradient descent will find the solution, or whether it will generalise.
Glorot & Bengio (2010); He et al. (2015) Part VII §11
The initialisation scales of §11, derived from keeping variance constant across depth. The second adds the factor of two for ReLU.
Glorot, Bordes & Bengio (2011): deep sparse rectifier networks Part VII §7
The case for ReLU, made empirically at the moment it was still contentious.
Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton (2012): ImageNet classification with deep CNNs Part VII §7
The result that effectively ended the argument about whether deep learning was viable. Worth reading as an artefact: ReLU activations, dropout, data augmentation, GPU training and scale, each component present because it was measurably needed, combined and shown to work at a margin that made the result difficult to dispute.
He, Zhang, Ren & Sun (2016): deep residual learning Part VII §18
The skip connection. One structural change, and depth stopped being the limiting factor.

Part VIII: Regularization

Srivastava et al. (2014): Dropout: a simple way to prevent neural networks from overfitting Part VIII §19
The paper that made dropout standard. Read it for the ensemble interpretation: the argument that training with dropout is approximately training exponentially many weight-sharing subnetworks. Notice how much of it is empirical: the method was adopted on overwhelming evidence, and its theoretical account remains contested. A technique whose mechanism is still debated is not unusual in this field.
Tibshirani (1996): Regression shrinkage and selection via the lasso Part VIII §5
Where $L^1$ enters statistics, with the geometric argument for sparsity that Part I §15 and §6 of this part both use. Unusually readable for a methods paper.
Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy (2015): Explaining and harnessing adversarial examples Part VIII §20
The argument that adversarial examples come from linearity in high dimensions rather than from anything exotic about deep networks, plus the fast gradient sign method that Figure 8 uses. It is the paper that reframed the phenomenon.
Zhang et al. (2017): Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization Part VI §6
Already cited for Part VI. Appears here because it belongs alongside Part VIII as much as Part VI: a network that memorises random labels has not regularised in any meaningful sense, yet dropout and weight decay do not prevent it. Read it alongside Part V §20's double descent; together they are the honest state of what the field understands about why overparameterised networks generalise.
Loshchilov & Hutter (2019): Decoupled weight decay regularization Part III §17
Already cited for Part III, and it belongs here too: weight decay and $L^2$ stop being the same thing once an adaptive optimiser rescales the gradient.

Part IX: Optimization

Bottou & Bousquet (2008): The tradeoffs of large scale learning Part IX §13
The argument behind §13: once you account for the fact that generalisation error cannot improve faster than $O(1/k)$, pursuing an optimiser that converges faster stops being obviously worthwhile. Short, and it changes how you read convergence-rate claims.
Dauphin et al. (2014): Identifying and attacking the saddle point problem in high-dimensional non-convex optimization Part IX §8
The paper that moved the field off local minima. Contains both the evidence that real networks are covered in high-cost saddles and the saddle-free Newton method of §8. Read the eigenvalue-versus-cost result carefully; it is the part that explains why the many local minima are harmless.
Goodfellow, Vinyals & Saxe (2015): Qualitatively characterizing neural network optimization problems Part IX §12
Where the visualisations behind §12 come from. Its most useful contribution is negative: the projected cost surfaces of real networks show far fewer obstacles than everyone expected, and most of training is spent traversing a long arc rather than escaping anything.
Saxe, McClelland & Ganguli (2013): Exact solutions to the nonlinear dynamics of learning in deep linear networks Part IX §8
Deep linear networks have a non-convex loss and can be solved exactly, which makes them the best available model of what depth does to optimisation. The source of both the orthogonal initialisation of §16 and the no-bad-local-minima result of §8.
Sussillo & Abbott (2014): Random walks: training very deep nonlinear feed-forward networks with smart initialization Part IX §10
Trains a thousand-layer feedforward network with nothing but a correctly chosen gain, which is the cleanest evidence for §10's claim that depth with independent weights is a different problem from depth with a shared matrix.
Ioffe & Szegedy (2015): Batch normalization: accelerating deep network training by reducing internal covariate shift Part IX §23
The method, from its authors. The explanation in the title (internal covariate shift, the changing distribution of each layer's inputs as earlier layers are updated) is the account the authors give and the account that is probably wrong. The method itself works; derived in §23.
Santurkar et al. (2018): How does batch normalization help optimization? Part IX §23
Presents controlled experiments showing that internal covariate shift is not the mechanism, and argues that the real effect is smoothing the loss landscape. Read it directly after Ioffe and Szegedy: the pair is among the clearest examples in this book of a method that outlived its own explanation and kept working anyway.
Bengio et al. (2009): Curriculum learning Part IX §28
The paper that connected shaping to continuation methods. Worth reading alongside Zaremba and Sutskever's finding that only the stochastic curriculum helps: §28's pitfall, and the reason most curriculum implementations do nothing.
Romero et al. (2015): FitNets: hints for thin deep nets Part IX §26
The result of §26. Note what is being measured: without hints the deep thin student is poor on the training set, which is how you tell an optimisation obstacle from a representational one. Almost no paper reports that number, and it is the one that matters.
Martens (2010): Deep learning via Hessian-free optimization Part IX §21
The most serious attempt to make second-order methods work at scale, using Hessian-vector products and conjugate gradients rather than forming $H$, making §§20 and 21 practical. Also the source of the sparse initialisation of §16. It did not displace SGD, and the reasons are §5's batch-size result and §8's saddles.

Part X: Convolutional models

LeCun et al. (1998): Gradient-based learning applied to document recognition Part X §2
The convolutional network, complete, from the group that built it. No new mathematics and no trick, just a principled architecture, stated clearly, validated at production scale on a tenth of the cheques processed in the United States. The longest paper on this list, but repays reading end to end: section VII's graph transformer network is a reminder of how much was tried before the simple version won.
Luo et al. (2016): Understanding the effective receptive field in deep convolutional neural networks Part X §10
The $\sqrt{L}$ result of §10's Figure 4: the region actually influencing an output grows as $\sqrt{L}$, not $L$, because central pixels are reachable by many paths and edge pixels by one. Obvious once stated; unstated for twenty-five years during which everyone designed against the theoretical field of $1+2L$.
Springenberg et al. (2015): Striving for simplicity: the all convolutional net Part X §15
Removes pooling and finds the network no worse. One of the cleanest ablations in vision, and the beginning of the end for an operation that was in every diagram.
Zhang (2019): Making convolutional networks shift-invariant again Part X §15
Downsampling by any means aliases, and the fix is a low-pass filter, the classical answer, rediscovered here about thirty years late.
Simonyan & Zisserman (2015): Very deep convolutional networks Part X §21
One idea applied with total consistency: only $3\times3$ kernels, stacked. §2.3 has the receptive-field argument in full.
Szegedy et al. (2015): Going deeper with convolutions Part X §21
Inception: why pick a kernel size at all? Also the source of the $1\times1$ reduction that §19 generalises, and of the auxiliary heads of Part IX §27.
Liu et al. (2022): A ConvNet for the 2020s Part X §26
The method is the contribution: a ResNet modernised one measured change at a time, until it matches transformers. Read it as a model of how an ablation should be reported, then reread the papers whose claims it deflates.
Odena, Dumoulin & Olah (2016): Deconvolution and checkerboard artifacts Part X §27
Diagnoses an artefact everyone had seen and nobody had explained, and gives the arithmetic condition Figure 9 verifies.
Ronneberger et al. (2015): U-Net Part X §27
Why a segmentation decoder needs skip connections from the encoder rather than merely benefiting from them: the fine spatial information was destroyed on the way down.
Hubel & Wiesel (1962): Receptive fields, binocular interaction and functional architecture in the cat's visual cortex Part X §31
Two people, an electrode and bars of light. Simple cells, complex cells and the detector-then-pool hierarchy, all present in 1962.
Olshausen & Field (1996): Emergence of simple-cell receptive field properties by learning a sparse code for natural images Part X §31
A sparse coding objective on natural images recovers Gabor-like filters with no supervision. Read §31's pitfall before drawing the obvious conclusion.
Dosovitskiy et al. (2021): An image is worth 16×16 words: transformers for image recognition at scale Part X §32
Reopens the question of whether convolution is necessary for vision, and is honest about the condition on the answer: with limited data the transformer loses decisively, and wins only with very large datasets. The convolutional inductive bias has a price and a value, and the authors quantify both. §11's infinitely strong prior, costed by the people who removed it.

Part XI: Sequence models

Elman (1990): Finding structure in time Part XI §2
Where the recurrent network in its modern form begins. Elman's argument (that time should be represented by its effect on the internal state of a processor rather than as an explicit input dimension) is the founding idea of §2, and it was not obvious then. The simple recurrent network (SRN) he introduces is the architecture this book calls an RNN.
Bengio, Simard & Frasconi (1994): Learning long-term dependencies with gradient descent is difficult Part XI §11
The result Part C is about. §III has the argument of §12: storing memories robustly requires the region where gradients vanish, so the two are one condition rather than a defect.
Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio (2013): On the difficulty of training recurrent neural networks Part XI §11
The geometric picture of the cliffs, and norm clipping. The more practical of the pair.
Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (1997): Long short-term memory Part XI §18
Written as an error-flow analysis first and an architecture second: the gates are derived from what the gradient equations require, not proposed and tested. The most carefully motivated architecture in the sequence modelling literature. Note that the original LSTM has no forget gate: that came three years later, and the version everyone uses is Gers et al.'s modification.
Gers, Schmidhuber & Cummins (2000): Learning to forget: continual prediction with LSTM Part XI §18
Adds the forget gate to the original LSTM, and establishes the bias-of-1 initialisation that §20 measures. The version most implementations use is this paper's, not Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's. Read both in order.
Greff et al. (2017): LSTM: a search space odyssey Part XI §21
Eight variants, thousands of runs: none reliably beats the standard cell, and the forget gate is the component that matters.
Jozefowicz, Zaremba & Sutskever (2015): An empirical exploration of recurrent network architectures Part XI §21
Ten thousand architectures searched, and a bias of 1 on the forget gate makes the plain LSTM as strong as anything found. Two large studies whose finding is that an initialisation was doing the work.
Graves, Wayne & Danihelka (2014): Neural Turing machines Part XI §26
A memory the network addresses rather than one it is. Its addressing operation is attention, reached from computer architecture rather than translation.
Sutskever, Vinyals & Le (2014): Sequence to sequence learning with neural networks Part XI §27
The encoder–decoder architecture and the first competitive neural machine translation system. The key engineering insight (reversing the source sentence before encoding) is worth noting as a sign of how empirical the field was: nobody predicted it would help, but it did.
Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (2015): Neural machine translation by jointly learning to align and translate Part XI §27
Identifies the fixed-size context vector as the bottleneck and replaces it with a mechanism that lets the decoder query the encoder's hidden states directly. The moment attention enters deep learning, introduced as a repair to a specific architectural flaw, not as a revolution. Read it immediately after Sutskever et al.: the problem statement is in one paper and the solution in the next.

Part XII: Attention and transformers

Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (2015): Neural machine translation by jointly learning to align and translate Part XII §6
Attention, invented as a repair to the encoder–decoder bottleneck rather than as an architecture. Worth reading for how narrowly it is framed: nothing here suggests the component is about to replace everything around it.
Vaswani et al. (2017): Attention is all you need Part XII §7
The title is a joke about exactly that. Read §3.2 for the scaled dot-product and the footnote justifying the $\sqrt{d_k}$: it is the variance argument of §4, in two sentences.
Su et al. (2024): RoFormer: enhanced transformer with rotary position embedding Part XII §11
RoPE. The derivation is short and the property it establishes is exact rather than encouraged, which is what distinguishes it from everything before it.
Xiong et al. (2020): On layer normalization in the transformer architecture Part XII §17
Where pre-norm was analysed rather than merely adopted. Read it against Figure 7, which finds the usual one-line summary of this paper too strong.
Dao et al. (2022): FlashAttention: fast and memory-efficient exact attention with IO-awareness Part XII §29
The word exact in the title is the point. It computes the same function and made long context practical where a decade of approximations did not, because the resource that mattered was memory traffic rather than arithmetic.
Shazeer (2019): Fast transformer decoding: one write-head is all you need Part XII §30
Multi-query attention, two pages, and an unusually clean case of a deployment constraint reaching back into the architecture.
Shazeer et al. (2017): Outrageously large neural networks: the sparsely-gated mixture-of-experts layer Part XII §33
Conditional computation, and the balancing problem that comes with it. Note where the experts are placed: the feedforward layer, because §15 says that is where the parameters are.
Dosovitskiy et al. (2021): An image is worth 16x16 words Part XII §35
Already cited for Part X §32 and it belongs here too, because the honest reading needs both architectures derived: the transformer loses below about a hundred million images and wins above. Part X §11's prior, priced.
Jain & Wallace (2019): Attention is not explanation Part XII §36
Constructs substantially different attention distributions producing near-identical predictions. Read it before trusting any attention heatmap, including your own.
Elhage et al. (2021): A mathematical framework for transformer circuits Part XII §37
Where the residual-stream reading of §16 and the induction circuit of §37 come from. Long, and the first half (the one- and two-layer analysis) is the part to read; it is the clearest worked example of a mechanism identified from structure and then confirmed.
Brown et al. (2020): Language models are few-shot learners Part XII §38
In-context learning, reported. Read it for what it does not contain: no mechanism, no explanation, and a capability nobody set out to build. That absence is still the state of the question.
Gu & Dao (2024): Mamba: linear-time sequence modeling with selective state spaces Part XII §40
The recurrent end of §40's axis, made serious: a state large enough to be useful and an input-dependent transition, which is Part XI §18's gating promoted to the central mechanism.

Part XIII: Data

Aroyo & Welty (2015): Truth is a lie: crowd truth and the seven myths of human annotation Part XIII §5
Seven assumptions about annotation, dismantled, including that disagreement is noise. The argument that it is signal about the task is the one worth carrying.
Geirhos et al. (2020): Shortcut learning in deep neural networks Part XIII §8
Named the phenomenon and gave it a taxonomy. A shortcut is a correct solution to the problem you posed; the problem you posed is your dataset.
Kapoor & Narayanan (2023): Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in machine-learning-based science Part XIII §11
294 papers across seventeen fields, with leakage in a large fraction. The errors are ordinary and the work was reviewed, which is the uncomfortable part.
Broder (1997): On the resemblance and containment of documents Part XIII §15
MinHash, for the problem of duplicate web pages. The identity is derived in a paragraph.
Lee et al. (2022): Deduplicating training data makes language models better Part XIII §15
What deduplication does to a real corpus: better models from fewer tokens, and substantially less memorisation. One intervention improving several things at once.
Gebru et al. (2021): Datasheets for datasets Part XIII §19
The documentation template. Read it for the questions, especially what the dataset should not be used for, which is the section most often left blank.
Shumailov et al. (2024): AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data Part XIII §28
Named model collapse. Read alongside Figure 5, which finds the sampling-error mechanism much weaker than the summary suggests and locates the damage in filtering instead.
Gerstgrasser et al. (2024): Is model collapse inevitable? Part XIII §28
Collapse largely disappears when generated data accumulates alongside real data rather than replacing it. A difference in pipeline design, not in mathematics.
Carlini et al. (2021): Extracting training data from large language models Part XIII §33
Verbatim training text, including personal information, extracted from a deployed model.
Carlini et al. (2023): Quantifying memorization across neural language models Part XIII §33
Memorisation grows with model scale, with data duplication, and with prompt length. The relationship Figure 6 reproduces in miniature.

Part XIV: Practice and Diagnosis

Guo et al. (2017): On calibration of modern neural networks Part XIV §5
Modern networks are worse calibrated than the smaller ones they replaced, despite being more accurate. One scalar temperature, fitted on validation data, fixes most of it and beats the elaborate alternatives.
Squire & Trapp (1998): Using complex variables to estimate derivatives of real functions Part XIV §9
Four pages. Removes the subtraction from a finite difference and with it the entire cancellation problem, for any analytic function. Figure 3 is this result.
Bergstra & Bengio (2012): Random search for hyper-parameter optimization Part XIV §16
Simpler, cheaper and better, argued from one observation: few hyperparameters matter and you do not know which. Among the highest value-per-page results in the field.
Li et al. (2018): Hyperband: a novel bandit-based approach to hyperparameter optimization Part XIV §17
Successive halving with the aggressiveness hedged, framed as a bandit problem, which is the reframing that makes the analysis work.
Falkner, Klein & Hutter (2018): BOHB: robust and efficient hyperparameter optimization at scale Part XIV §18
A surrogate model with early stopping. The honest answer to the objection that Bayesian optimisation throws away the information a human uses most.
Dodge et al. (2019): Show your work: improved reporting of experimental results Part XIV §20
A score is meaningless without the compute budget that produced it. Report expected best performance as a function of trials: Figure 7's hazard, turned into a curve you publish. Largely ignored.
Recht et al. (2019): Do ImageNet classifiers generalize to ImageNet? Part XIV §22
A fresh test set built to the original protocol. Every model dropped substantially; the ranking survived almost perfectly. Both halves matter.
Zheng et al. (2023): Judging LLM-as-a-judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena Part XIV §24
Measures the model judge against human preference, and its biases (position, verbosity, self-preference) rather than assuming them away.

Part XV: Representation Learning

Vincent et al. (2010): Stacked denoising autoencoders Part XV §11
The paper that made denoising a representation-learning method rather than a way to clean images. Its framing (corruption forces the model to learn the data's structure) is the one the whole modern stack inherited.
Vincent (2011): A connection between score matching and denoising autoencoders Part XV §12
Four pages establishing the identity Figure 3 measures, and the single most consequential result in this part. Read it before Part XVI rather than during.
Olshausen & Field (1996): Emergence of simple-cell receptive field properties by learning a sparse code Part XV §10
Sparse coding, proposed as a claim about how natural signals are structured. Worth reading now because §34 applies the same penalty to a transformer's activations thirty years later, and it works for the reason this paper gives.
Rifai et al. (2011): Contractive auto-encoders Part XV §13
The Jacobian penalty, and the observation that its surviving singular directions are the manifold's tangent directions. The clearest worked instance of the manifold reading.
Bengio, Courville & Vincent (2013): Representation learning: a review and new perspectives Part XV §41
The survey the source chapter's prior list comes from. Read for the list of assumptions, and read it against §41's verdicts: several have since been settled, one negatively.
Chen et al. (2020): A simple framework for contrastive learning of visual representations Part XV §19
SimCLR. Its most valuable section is the augmentation ablation, which is Figure 4's argument on real images: the composition of augmentations is the design decision, and cropping plus colour distortion is doing nearly all of the work.
He et al. (2020): Momentum contrast for unsupervised visual representation learning Part XV §17
MoCo. Decouples the number of negatives from the batch size with a queue, and explains why the momentum encoder is required rather than convenient.
Chen & He (2021): Exploring simple Siamese representation learning Part XV §21
SimSiam. Strips BYOL to its essentials and shows the momentum encoder is not one of them. Its ablations are the evidence that the predictor and the stop-gradient are each load-bearing, and its hypothesis section is honest about how much remains unexplained.
He et al. (2022): Masked autoencoders are scalable vision learners Part XV §22
MAE, and the 75% mask ratio. Read §4's ablation for why the ratio is so much higher than BERT's: it is a measurement of image redundancy, not a tuning result.
Radford et al. (2021): Learning transferable visual models from natural language supervision Part XV §36
CLIP. The supervision is the pairing, which exists on the web without anyone labelling anything. Read the zero-shot evaluation with §38's caveat in hand: prompt wording is a hyperparameter, and the corpus is unpublished.
Hewitt & Liang (2019): Designing and interpreting probes with control tasks Part XV §32
The paper behind Figure 9, and the one that should have changed probing practice more than it did. Selectivity, not accuracy, and the demonstration that a sufficiently powerful probe learns anything you give it.
Locatello et al. (2019): Challenging common assumptions in the unsupervised learning of disentangled representations Part XV §39
The impossibility theorem plus 12,000 trained models. Read both halves: the proof settles what is achievable, and the study shows seeds mattered more than methods and that the good hyperparameters could not be chosen without the ground truth.
Elhage et al. (2022): Toy models of superposition Part XV §33
The model Figure 10 reproduces. Explains polysemantic neurons as a consequence of packing rather than a failure, and is unusually clear about what its toy setting does and does not establish.
Bricken et al. (2023): Towards monosemanticity: decomposing language models with dictionary learning Part XV §34
Sparse autoencoders on a real model's activations. Read the limitations section as carefully as the results: dead features, reconstruction error and feature-set instability are all named there, and are usually dropped when the work is cited.
Wang & Isola (2020): Understanding contrastive representation learning through alignment and uniformity on the hypersphere Part XV §18
Decomposes the contrastive objective into the two forces and shows they can be optimised directly. The cleanest account of what the negatives are actually doing.

Part XVI: Generative Models

Hyvärinen (2005): Estimation of non-normalized statistical models by score matching Part XVI §23
The integration by parts that removes both the partition function and the unknown data score. Read it for the derivation, which is three pages, and note the second-derivative cost that kept it unused for a decade.
Vincent (2011): A connection between score matching and denoising autoencoders Part XVI §25
The identity the whole movement turns on, and the paper to read before anything else here. Removes the second derivative and the coverage problem in one move.
Kingma & Welling (2014): Auto-encoding variational Bayes Part XVI §16
The VAE. Its contribution is narrower and more important than "a generative model": the reparameterisation trick, which turned an intractable expectation into an ordinary differentiable expression.
Burda, Grosse & Salakhutdinov (2016): Importance weighted autoencoders Part XVI §17
The tighter bound, and the one to report if you are reporting a likelihood at all. Read §3 for why it tightens with $k$.
Goodfellow et al. (2014): Generative adversarial networks Part XVI §34
The paper. Read §4's proof that the optimal discriminator turns the objective into Jensen–Shannon: it is the clearest statement of what a learned loss function is.
Arjovsky, Chintala & Bottou (2017): Wasserstein GAN Part XVI §37
Diagnoses the vanishing gradient as a consequence of using a divergence that is undefined when the supports do not overlap, and proposes a distance that is not. The diagnosis outlasted the specific fix.
Ho, Jain & Abbeel (2020): Denoising diffusion probabilistic models Part XVI §27
The paper that made diffusion work, largely by simplifying the loss to plain noise-prediction. Read §3 for the parameterisation argument, which is the part that mattered.
Song & Ermon (2019): Generative modeling by estimating gradients of the data distribution Part XVI §26
The multi-noise-level argument, stated directly: this is the paper that identifies coverage and mode weighting as the two failures and fixes both with a schedule. Figure 2 is its argument.
Song et al. (2021): Score-based generative modeling through stochastic differential equations Part XVI §29
Unifies the discrete-step and continuous views and produces the deterministic sampler as a consequence. Where the few-step samplers come from.
Ho & Salimans (2022): Classifier-free diffusion guidance Part XVI §30
One network, label dropout, and a weight at sampling time. Read it alongside Figure 7: the paper is clear that guidance changes the distribution being sampled, which is often lost in retelling.
Nichol & Dhariwal (2021): Improved denoising diffusion probabilistic models Part XVI §31
The cosine schedule, and the demonstration that the corruption process is part of the model. A rare case of a large gain from touching no architecture.
Lipman et al. (2023): Flow matching for generative modeling Part XVI §32
The same destination reached without a Markov chain or a noise schedule. Worth reading after the diffusion papers so the simplification is visible.
Rombach et al. (2022): High-resolution image synthesis with latent diffusion models Part XVI §20
Compress first, then diffuse in the latent space: Part XV's autoencoder and this part's density model, split. The architecture behind most deployed image generators.
Theis, van den Oord & Bethge (2016): A note on the evaluation of generative models Part XVI §41
Short, and the most useful thing written on the subject. Log-likelihood, sample quality and nearest-neighbour checks are largely independent, and a model can be excellent on any one while failing the others. Everything in movement F is downstream of this.
Kynkäänniemi et al. (2019): Improved precision and recall metric for assessing generative models Part XVI §43
The two-number replacement for a single distance, with a support estimate that works from samples. What should be reported by default and usually is not.

Part XVII: Scale

Kaplan et al. (2020): Scaling laws for neural language models Part XVII §10
The paper that made scaling a research programme. Read it for the method and read §6's allocation conclusion knowing it was corrected: the reasoning is sound and one procedural choice was not.
Hoffmann et al. (2022): Training compute-optimal large language models Part XVII §12
Chinchilla. Three independent estimation methods agreeing, and a model four times smaller than the previous generation beating it at equal compute. Read §3 for what the schedule error was and why it tilted the answer.
Schaeffer, Miranda & Koyejo (2023): Are emergent abilities of large language models a mirage? Part XVII §7
The deflation, and a clean piece of methodological work: the same runs produce a sharp curve or a smooth one depending on whether the metric is discontinuous.
Rajbhandari et al. (2020): ZeRO: memory optimizations toward training trillion parameter models Part XVII §18
The three sharding stages, with the memory arithmetic worked out explicitly. Read it with Figure 1 in hand and note which category is missing from the analysis.
Shoeybi et al. (2019): Megatron-LM: training multi-billion parameter language models using model parallelism Part XVII §23
Tensor parallelism, and the column-then-row split that needs only one all-reduce per block. Short, concrete, and the communication analysis is the part to read.
Narayanan et al. (2021): Efficient large-scale language model training on GPU clusters using Megatron-LM Part XVII §26
How the three forms of parallelism are combined in practice, with the cost model that decides the assignment. The clearest published account of choosing a configuration by arithmetic.
Micikevicius et al. (2018): Mixed precision training Part XVII §19
The fp32 master copy, loss scaling, and which operations must stay in higher precision. Predates bf16 hardware, which is why so much of it is about managing a range problem that bf16 removes.
Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer (2022): Switch transformers Part XVII §30
Top-1 routing, the auxiliary balancing loss, and the capacity factor. Read §2.2 on load balancing and note that the drop rate is discussed there and rarely reported elsewhere.
Yang et al. (2022): Tensor programs V: tuning large neural networks via zero-shot hyperparameter transfer Part XVII §34
μP. The derivation is long; the practical claim is short and checkable, and §7's transfer experiments are the part worth reading first.
McCandlish et al. (2018): An empirical model of large-batch training Part XVII §33
The gradient noise scale, and the prediction that it sets the batch size beyond which parallelism stops buying speed. One of the few results here with a derivation rather than a fit.

Part XVIII: Reinforcement Learning

Sutton & Barto (2018): Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction Part XVIII, throughout
The textbook, and unusually one worth reading rather than consulting. Chapters 3–6 are the exact case and the sampled case done properly; §11.3's account of the deadly triad is the clearest there is.
Mnih et al. (2015): Human-level control through deep reinforcement learning Part XVIII §22
DQN. Its contribution is not that it was best at any game but that one architecture and one hyperparameter setting worked across dozens, which is what made it convincing.
van Hasselt, Guez & Silver (2016): Deep reinforcement learning with double Q-learning Part XVIII §19
The maximisation bias measured on real value estimates, and a one-line restructuring that removes it. Read §3 for the demonstration that the learned values were systematically too high.
Schulman et al. (2015): Trust region policy optimization Part XVIII §31
The monotonic improvement argument and the KL constraint. Worth reading for why the constraint belongs in policy space, which is the idea PPO keeps.
Schulman et al. (2017): Proximal policy optimization algorithms Part XVIII §31
Short, and the clipped objective is the whole method. Read the $\min$ carefully: it is what makes a too-large step buy nothing rather than cost something.
Schulman et al. (2016): High-dimensional continuous control using generalized advantage estimation Part XVIII §29
GAE, and the clearest treatment of the bias–variance trade in the actor's target.
Engstrom et al. (2020): Implementation matters in deep RL: a case study on PPO and TRPO Part XVIII §32
The replication that found much of PPO's advantage in code-level details rather than in the clipping. An uncomfortable paper and a model of how to check a method.
Henderson et al. (2018): Deep reinforcement learning that matters Part XVIII §43
Seeds, implementations and reporting. Results varying more across seeds than across the algorithms being compared, with three seeds the norm. Read alongside Part XIV §19.
Ng, Harada & Russell (1999): Policy invariance under reward transformations Part XVIII §39
The proof that potential-based shaping leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and that essentially nothing else does. Short, and the telescoping argument is worth seeing once.
Christiano et al. (2017): Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences Part XVIII §41
The construction behind RLHF, several years before it was used that way. Comparisons rather than scores, a learned reward model, and policy optimisation against it.
Gao, Schulman & Hilton (2023): Scaling laws for reward model overoptimisation Part XVIII §41
Reward-model score rising while true quality peaks and falls, measured across model sizes. The quantitative version of why the KL penalty is the main knob.
Levine et al. (2020): Offline reinforcement learning: tutorial, review, and perspectives Part XVIII §40
Why learning from a fixed dataset is not simply supervised learning: the max in the Bellman target ranges over actions the data does not cover, and nothing corrects the extrapolation.

Part XIX: Efficiency and Deployment

Williams, Waterman & Patterson (2009): Roofline: an insightful visual performance model Part XIX §3
Predates all of this and explains most of it. Arithmetic intensity against a machine's ridge point, and the observation that an operation's bottleneck follows from its shape.
Dettmers et al. (2022): LLM.int8(): 8-bit matrix multiplication for transformers at scale Part XIX §10
The outlier finding: a small number of fixed feature dimensions carry very large activations, and keeping just those in higher precision rescues 8-bit inference. Read §3 for the structure, which is what the later methods exploit.
Frantar et al. (2023): GPTQ: accurate post-training quantization for generative pre-trained transformers Part XIX §11
Column-by-column quantization with second-order error compensation. The idea worth taking is that it fixes the error's downstream effect rather than rounding better.
Lin et al. (2024): AWQ: activation-aware weight quantization Part XIX §11
Not all weights matter equally: protect the ones multiplying large activations, by scaling rather than by mixed precision. Simpler than GPTQ and competitive.
Frankle & Carbin (2019): The lottery ticket hypothesis Part XIX §18
Sparse subnetworks that train to full accuracy from their original initialisation. Read the reset step carefully (it is the whole claim), and read the follow-up work on rewinding, which is what made it hold at scale.
Hinton, Vinyals & Dean (2015): Distilling the knowledge in a neural network Part XIX §25
Soft targets, temperature, and the dark knowledge in the wrong answers. Short, and its framing of what a teacher's distribution contains is the part that transfers.
Hu et al. (2022): LoRA: low-rank adaptation of large language models Part XIX §32
The method, and §7's analysis of the update's rank. Read that section against Figure 6 and note the difference between an update being low rank and a rank-$r$ constraint being sufficient.
Dettmers et al. (2023): QLoRA: efficient finetuning of quantized LLMs Part XIX §34
A 4-bit frozen base with 16-bit adapters, which is what put fine-tuning a large model on one device. The paged optimiser and the NF4 format are the engineering that makes it work.
Kwon et al. (2023): Efficient memory management for large language model serving with PagedAttention Part XIX §39
An operating-systems idea applied to the KV cache, and the clearest published account of why cache fragmentation, not compute, limits serving throughput.
Leviathan, Kalman & Matias (2023): Fast inference from transformers via speculative decoding Part XIX §40
The rejection rule and its proof that the output distribution is unchanged. Worth reading for the proof, because "exactly the same distribution" is a rare property in this part.
Salimans & Ho (2022): Progressive distillation for fast sampling of diffusion models Part XIX §29
Distilling a procedure rather than a function: halve the sampler's step count, repeatedly. The technique behind real-time image generation, and honest about the diversity it costs.

Part XX: Language Models

Sennrich, Haddow & Birch (2016): Neural machine translation of rare words with subword units Part XX §2
Byte-pair encoding brought to neural sequence models, and the argument for why the unknown token had to go. Short, and the algorithm is three lines.
Brown et al. (2020): Language models are few-shot learners Part XX §17
The paper that made in-context learning a subject. Read §3's task list for the breadth and §5's honest limitations, which are more careful than the reception was.
Olsson et al. (2022): In-context learning and induction heads Part XX §18
Ties the capability to a mechanism and to a moment in training. One of the few results in this part that explains rather than describes.
Min et al. (2022): Rethinking the role of demonstrations Part XX §19
The finding that randomising demonstration labels costs surprisingly little. Read it together with the work that finds the opposite on novel mappings: the disagreement is about whether the task was already learnable from the weights, and that is the transferable lesson.
Wei et al. (2022): Chain-of-thought prompting elicits reasoning in large language models Part XX §21
The observation, and the scaling behaviour that made it interesting. Read alongside the work on faithfulness, which establishes that the chain helps without being a transcript.
Ouyang et al. (2022): Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback Part XX §26
InstructGPT, and the three-stage pipeline as it is actually run. The striking number is how little preference data changes how usable the model is.
Rafailov et al. (2023): Direct preference optimization Part XX §29
The derivation that removes the reward model and the RL loop. Read §4 for what is given up (the online loop) because that is the part the simplification costs.
Liu et al. (2024): Lost in the middle: how language models use long contexts Part XX §12
The position effect, measured. The reason to report a depth profile rather than a window size.
Lewis et al. (2020): Retrieval-augmented generation Part XX §34
The construction, and the argument for why knowledge belongs in a document store rather than in weights: current, attributable, editable and access-controlled.
Zhao et al. (2021): Calibrate before use: improving few-shot performance of language models Part XX §22
How much performance moves with the order and formatting of demonstrations. The measurement that makes single-prompt evaluation indefensible.
Olah et al. (2020): Zoom in: an introduction to circuits Part XXI §11
The programme stated: features, circuits, and universality. Read it for what a circuit claim is supposed to consist of, then hold everything after it to that standard.
Adebayo et al. (2018): Sanity checks for saliency maps Part XXI §14
The control that ended a literature. Randomise the weights, re-run the method, and see whether the output changes: for several published methods it barely did. The most efficient paper in this list, and the one whose method generalises furthest beyond its subject.
Meng et al. (2022): Locating and editing factual associations in GPT Part XXI §10
Activation patching applied at scale, and then the editing that tests the localisation causally. The pairing is the point: a claim about where something lives, followed by an intervention that would fail if the claim were wrong.
Gao, Schulman & Hilton (2023): Scaling laws for reward model overoptimisation Part XXI §18
Read a second time here, for the shape rather than the coefficients: true reward rises, peaks and falls as the proxy is optimised harder. Figure 4 reproduces that shape in three lines of simulation and isolates the condition that produces it.
Burns et al. (2023): Weak-to-strong generalization Part XXI §22
Read for the experimental design rather than the headline. The analogy between "weak model supervising strong model" and "human supervising superhuman model" is the load-bearing assumption, the authors say so, and how much weight it carries is the thing to form a view on.
Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016): Inherent trade-offs in the fair determination of risk scores Part XXI §29
The impossibility theorem behind §29. Short, entirely elementary, and it converts an interminable argument into a choice that has to be made and defended.
Pearl (2009): Causality: models, reasoning and inference Part XXII §14
Chapter 3 is the one to read: the do-calculus, and the completeness results that turn "can this be estimated from observational data" from a judgement call into a decision procedure. Dense, and the three rules repay the effort.
Hernán & Robins: Causal Inference: What If Part XXII §12
The applied companion to Pearl, free online, written by people who have had to defend these assumptions to regulators. Read it for the estimand discipline and for how carefully it separates identification from estimation.
Angrist & Pischke (2009): Mostly Harmless Econometrics Part XXII §26
The design tradition: instruments, differences, discontinuities, and an unusually honest running commentary on when each one is not credible. Read it alongside Pearl rather than instead: the two fields solved the same problem in different languages and are still translating.
Peters, Janzing & Schölkopf (2017): Elements of Causal Inference Part XXII §34
Where causality meets machine learning: invariance, transfer, and causal discovery, with the identifiability results stated properly. The chapter on invariant prediction is the theory behind Figure 6.
Chernozhukov et al. (2018): Double/debiased machine learning Part XXII §21
How to use flexible models for the nuisance functions in a doubly robust estimator without their regularisation bias contaminating the effect estimate. Cross-fitting is the mechanism, and it is the same honest-splitting idea as the causal forest.
Athey & Imbens (2016): Recursive partitioning for heterogeneous causal effects Part XXII §33
Trees that split on effect heterogeneity rather than outcome variation, with honest sample splitting. Read §3 for why the same data cannot both choose a split and estimate within it, the problem Part XIV §20 measures in another setting.
Vapnik & Chervonenkis (1971): On the uniform convergence of relative frequencies of events to their probabilities Part XXIII §12
The paper the subject is built on. Worth reading for the shape of the argument (a combinatorial fact about labellings converted into a statistical rate), which is still the template every later bound follows.
Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David (2014): Understanding Machine Learning: From Theory to Algorithms Part XXIII §13
The standard modern treatment, and the place to go for the fundamental theorem stated properly with both directions. Chapters 3–6 cover this part's movements A and B at full rigour.
Zhang et al. (2017): Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization Part XXIII §23
The randomised-label experiment, run cleanly, with the consequences stated plainly. Short, and entirely readable after movement B: the contribution is noticing that an obvious experiment settles a question the field had assumed was open.
Belkin et al. (2019): Reconciling modern machine-learning practice and the classical bias–variance trade-off Part XXIII §25
Double descent, named and measured across several model families. Read it together with Nakkiran et al. (2019), which finds the same curve in the number of training steps as well as the number of parameters.
Bartlett, Long, Lugosi & Tsigler (2020): Benign overfitting in linear regression Part XXIII §26
The sharp characterisation for linear models: exactly which covariance spectra allow interpolation of noise without harm. The one place in this movement where the theory is complete.
Dziugaite & Roy (2017): Computing nonvacuous generalization bounds for deep networks Part XXIII §29
PAC-Bayes bounds that come in under 1 on a real network, the first ones that did. Note carefully which object is bounded: a stochastic network, not the deterministic one you would deploy.
Sagawa et al. (2020): Distributionally robust neural networks for group shifts Part XXIII §43
Group DRO, and the finding that strong regularization is what makes it work. Read it for the worst-group framing, which is more useful than the algorithm and is the right way to report almost any accuracy number.
Rasmussen & Williams (2006): Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning Part XXIV §9
Free online, and chapter 2 is the clearest derivation of the posterior anywhere. Read chapter 5 for the marginal likelihood as model selection: Occam's razor arriving as a log determinant rather than as a preference.
Angelopoulos & Bates (2023): A gentle introduction to conformal prediction Part XXIV §24
The best entry point to the subject: the guarantee, the proof in a paragraph, and the practical variants. Read §2 and then implement it: the whole method is a sort and an index, and writing it is what makes the guarantee believable.
Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel & Blundell (2017): Simple and scalable predictive uncertainty using deep ensembles Part XXIV §19
The paper that established ensembles as the practical baseline. Read it for the honesty of the framing: it does not claim a Bayesian derivation, and the measured results are what carried the argument.
Guo et al. (2017): On calibration of modern neural networks Part XXIV §6
That accuracy improved while calibration got worse, and that temperature scaling fixes most of it for one parameter. Read it alongside Part XIV §5, and note what it does not address, which is anything off the distribution it was measured on.
Kendall & Gal (2017): What uncertainties do we need in Bayesian deep learning for computer vision? Part XXIV §5
The aleatoric/epistemic split made operational, with the heteroscedastic loss written out. Read §3 for the loss and then look for the failure mode Part XXIV §5 describes, which the paper does not dwell on.
Tibshirani et al. (2019): Conformal prediction under covariate shift Part XXIV §29
How to restore the guarantee when exchangeability fails, by weighting the calibration scores with the likelihood ratio. The same positivity requirement as everywhere else in this book, and the paper is clear about it.
Breiman (2001): Random forests Part XXV §11
The variance identity of §9 is stated here and is the whole argument. Read it for that, and note that the strength-and-correlation bound it proves is an upper bound rather than a promise that subsampling pays on your data.
Friedman (2001): Greedy function approximation: a gradient boosting machine Part XXV §17
Boosting derived as gradient descent in function space, which is the derivation that makes the loss swappable. Section 4 introduces shrinkage and reports what Figure 4 measures.
Chen & Guestrin (2016): XGBoost: a scalable tree boosting system Part XXV §17
The second-order formulation with explicit penalties, and an unusually candid systems section. Read §2 for the gain expression that scores a split and sets the leaf in one computation.
Ke et al. (2017): LightGBM: a highly efficient gradient boosting decision tree Part XXV §23
Histogram splitting, leaf-wise growth and the sibling-subtraction trick. Most of the speed in modern boosting is here rather than in the statistics.
Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux (2022): Why do tree-based models still outperform deep learning on typical tabular data? Part XXV §28
Read a second time here, for the mechanism rather than the headline: the paper's ablations point at irregular target functions and uninformative features, which is a claim about the generating process. Figure 7 splits four such processes two–two.
Hyndman & Athanasopoulos: Forecasting: Principles and Practice Part XXV §35
Free online, and the best single source for movements E and F. Chapter 5 on evaluation is the one to read first, because the split is what decides a forecasting result.
Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos (2020): The M4 competition: 100,000 time series Part XXV §44
What actually wins forecasting competitions, measured at scale. The finding that combinations and simple statistical methods held their own is the reason §35's baselines are baselines rather than history.
Kipf & Welling (2017): Semi-supervised classification with graph convolutional networks Part XXVI §9
The paper that made graph learning ordinary, and short enough to read in one sitting. Note what it does not say: the symmetric normalisation is presented as a first-order approximation to a spectral filter, and the two-layer depth that everyone copied was a practical choice whose reason Part XXVI §13 measures.
Gilmer et al. (2017): Neural message passing for quantum chemistry Part XXVI §8
The unification. Read §2, which shows a dozen separately published architectures as the same loop with different messages and aggregators, the single most clarifying page in this area.
Xu et al. (2019): How powerful are graph neural networks? Part XXVI §16
The Weisfeiler–Leman bound, and the reason sum aggregation is an expressivity choice rather than a preference. The proof is short and the consequence is the only exact capability limit in this book.
Alon & Yahav (2021): On the bottleneck of graph neural networks and its practical implications Part XXVI §20
Over-squashing named and measured. Read it beside the over-smoothing literature, because the two failures pull in opposite directions and a paper addressing one usually worsens the other.
Bronstein et al. (2021): Geometric deep learning: grids, groups, graphs, geodesics and gauges Part XXVI §32
The general statement of the argument Part X made for translation and Part XXVI generalises. Long; read the first sixty pages for the blueprint and treat the rest as reference.
Satorras, Hoogeboom & Welling (2021): E(n) equivariant graph neural networks Part XXVI §35
The scalar-and-vector construction of §35, written out in about a page of arithmetic. Worth reading immediately after the higher-order literature, to see how much of the benefit is available without it.
Shchur et al. (2018): Pitfalls of graph neural network evaluation Part XXVI §41
The re-evaluation that found a well-tuned simple model matching most of what was published to beat it. Read it as a method paper about comparison, not as a result about graphs.
Shannon (1949): Communication in the presence of noise Part XXVII §2
The sampling theorem, stated and proved in a few pages of unusually clear prose. Read §II for the statement and the reconstruction formula; the rest is the founding paper of a different subject and worth the detour.
Cooley & Tukey (1965): An algorithm for the machine calculation of complex Fourier series Part XXVII §12
Four pages that changed what was computationally possible. Gauss had it around 1805 in an unpublished notebook, which makes it among the most rediscovered results in numerical mathematics.
Oppenheim & Schafer: Discrete-Time Signal Processing Part XXVII §17
The standard reference for movements A to C, and the place to go when you need a filter design rather than an explanation of what a filter is. Chapter 4 on sampling is the one to read first.
Oppenheim & Lim (1981): The importance of phase in signals Part XXVII §18
The magnitude-and-phase swap, done properly. Figure 4 reproduces it. Read it before designing any loss that compares spectra, because it says what such a loss cannot see.
Rahaman et al. (2019): On the spectral bias of neural networks Part XXVII §29
The phenomenon named and measured. Read §4 for the argument from the eigenvalues, then read it again beside Part XXIII's movement on infinite width, which is where the argument becomes a derivation.
Tancik et al. (2020): Fourier features let networks learn high frequency functions in low dimensional domains Part XXVII §36
The remedy, with the bandwidth made explicit as a hyperparameter. Unusually candid about the trade: the paper shows the failure at both ends of the range, which most method papers do not.
Zhang (2019): Making convolutional networks shift-invariant again Part XXVII §6
Anti-aliased downsampling in a real architecture. The diagnosis is sixty years old and the paper's contribution is noticing that nobody had applied it, which is worth remembering as a category of available result.
Su et al. (2021): RoFormer: enhanced transformer with rotary position embedding Part XXVII §34
Rotary embeddings, and the derivation showing the dot product depends only on relative position. Read §3.4, then read a context-extension paper and watch it edit the frequency bank.
Pearl (1988): Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems Part XXVIII §4
Where graphical models and d-separation come from. Chapter 3 is the one to read; the collider argument in it is the same one Part XXII needs and is stated more clearly here than in most causal-inference texts.
Rabiner (1989): A tutorial on hidden Markov models Part XXVIII §12
Still the clearest exposition of forward–backward, including the scaling that stops the recursion underflowing, which most modern treatments omit and every implementer rediscovers.
Dempster, Laird & Rubin (1977): Maximum likelihood from incomplete data via the EM algorithm Part XXVIII §15
The monotonicity proof, which is short and is the whole guarantee. Read it beside the ELBO derivation and notice they are the same inequality.
Blei, Kucukelbir & McAuliffe (2017): Variational inference: a review for statisticians Part XXVIII §18
The best single entry point to movement C. §2.2 states the mean-field underestimate that Figure 4 measures, and is unusually direct about it being a property of the objective.
Betancourt (2017): A conceptual introduction to Hamiltonian Monte Carlo Part XXVIII §29
Why the gradient helps, argued geometrically rather than algebraically. Also the best available discussion of what diagnostics can and cannot establish, which is Figure 6's subject.
Song et al. (2021): Score-based generative modeling through stochastic differential equations Part XXVIII §36
The paper that unified the diffusion literature by writing it as an SDE, and that introduced the probability-flow ODE. Read §3 and §4; everything Part XVI taught discretely is here continuously.
Peyré & Cuturi (2019): Computational Optimal Transport Part XXVIII §41
Free online, and the reference for movement F. Chapter 4 on Sinkhorn is what made transport usable inside a training loop.
Arjovsky, Chintala & Bottou (2017): Wasserstein GAN Part XXVIII §41
Read it for §2, which is Figure 8's argument made carefully: a worked example where the likelihood-ratio divergences give no gradient and the transport distance does.
Hartley & Zisserman: Multiple View Geometry in Computer Vision Part XXIX §25
The reference for movement D and one of the few textbooks in this list that has not been superseded. Chapters 6 and 9 are the ones to read; the rest is there when you need it. Nothing in it depends on what models are fashionable, which is the point.
Lin et al. (2017): Focal loss for dense object detection Part XXIX §14
The paper that closed the one-stage accuracy gap, and it is worth reading for the diagnosis rather than the fix: §3 shows the problem was the summed loss of easy negatives, not the hard ones, which is not what the name suggests.
Lin et al. (2017): Feature pyramid networks for object detection Part XXIX §15
Read beside Figure 4. The architecture is simple and the argument for it is arithmetic: small objects are not represented at the depth where the semantics live.
Carion et al. (2020): End-to-end object detection with transformers Part XXIX §11
Detection as set prediction, with a matching loss and no suppression step. The strongest argument in it is the one about removing a non-differentiable, untuned post-process from the middle of a system.
Zhang (1999): Flexible camera calibration by viewing a plane from unknown orientations Part XXIX §27
The checkerboard calibration everybody uses. Short, and reading it once removes the sense that calibration is a black box in somebody's library.
Lucas & Kanade (1981): An iterative image registration technique Part XXIX §21
Where the brightness-constancy formulation and the local-constancy assumption come from. The aperture problem is visible in the algebra on the second page, forty years before anyone measured it with a network.
Luiten et al. (2021): HOTA: a higher order metric for evaluating multi-object tracking Part XXIX §37
Why one tracking number is not enough, and how to decompose it into detection and association terms that separate teams can act on. A good example of a metric paper that is really an engineering paper.
Graves et al. (2006): Connectionist temporal classification Part XXX §11
The forward recursion, the blank, and the collapse rule. Short. Read §3 and notice that the independence assumption is stated plainly in the paper and is usually dropped from later summaries.
Graves (2012): Sequence transduction with recurrent neural networks Part XXX §15
The transducer, which is CTC with the coupling term put back. Read it directly after the above; the difference between the two papers is exactly what Figure 4 measures.
Griffin & Lim (1984): Signal estimation from modified short-time Fourier transform Part XXX §7
Alternating projection for phase estimation, and unusually clear about what it converges to. Figure 2 is this algorithm measured in both spaces at once.
van den Oord et al. (2017): Neural discrete representation learning Part XXX §20
Vector quantisation inside an autoencoder, with the commitment loss and the straight-through estimator. The codebook-collapse problem is visible in the appendix and is the first thing to check in any implementation.
Défossez et al. (2022): High fidelity neural audio compression Part XXX §22
A residual-quantiser codec written up carefully, including the parts that usually go unreported: codebook usage, the effect of stage count, and what the adversarial term buys.
Radford et al. (2021): Learning transferable visual models from natural language supervision Part XXX §25
The two-tower construction at scale. Read §3 for the training details and then read §7 on limitations, which is more candid than the paper is usually given credit for.
Liang et al. (2022): Mind the gap: understanding the modality gap in multi-modal contrastive representation learning Part XXX §35
Where the modality gap was named and traced to initialisation. Read it beside Figure 8, which is drawn as an illustration because this book's own setup could not elicit the phenomenon.
Åström & Murray: Feedback Systems Part XXXI §3
Free online, and the best entry point to movement A for someone arriving from machine learning. Chapter 1 makes the argument that feedback substitutes for knowledge better than any paper does.
Bertsekas: Dynamic Programming and Optimal Control Part XXXI §10
The reference for movement B, and the book that makes the identity between optimal control and reinforcement learning unavoidable. Read the LQR chapter beside Part XXVIII §13 and the duality stops being a curiosity.
Rawlings, Mayne & Diehl: Model Predictive Control Part XXXI §14
Including the parts about stability guarantees, which are the difference between MPC as an idea and MPC as something you can deploy. Chapter 2 is the one to read.
Ross, Gordon & Bagnell (2011): A reduction of imitation learning and structured prediction to no-regret online learning Part XXXI §19
Where the compounding-error argument is made precisely and the repair (labelling the states the learner reaches) is derived rather than proposed. Short, and the bound is the point.
Kalman (1960): A new approach to linear filtering and prediction problems Part XXXI §28
Worth reading in the original for how little it assumes. The covariance recursion is the part that matters here: it is what lets a system say it does not know.
LaValle: Planning Algorithms Part XXXI §29 · Part XXXIII §23
Free online, and the reference for movement D's planning. Chapter 5 on sampling-based methods explains what they give up as carefully as what they buy, which is rare.
Kalra & Paddock (2016): Driving to safety: how many miles of driving would it take to demonstrate autonomous vehicle reliability? Part XXXI §42
Figure 8's arithmetic, worked through for real fleets and real rates. The conclusion (that the required mileage is infeasible for the rates that matter) is why a safety case is an argument rather than an odometer reading.
Amodei et al. (2016): Concrete problems in AI safety Part XXXI §35
Read again here, for the sections on safe exploration and distributional shift. What Part XXI treats as an alignment question is, for a system with a body, an engineering one with a fallback controller in it.
Williams, Waterman & Patterson (2009): Roofline: an insightful visual performance model Part XXXII §4
Where the model comes from, and short. Its argument is that a single plot answers "what should I work on" better than a profile does, which is still true fifteen years later.
Hennessy & Patterson: Computer Architecture: A Quantitative Approach Part XXXII §2
The reference for movement A. Read the memory-hierarchy chapter; everything else in this part's first half is a consequence of it.
Thakur, Rabenseifner & Gropp (2005): Optimization of collective communication operations in MPICH Part XXXII §18
Why a library switches algorithm by message size, worked through for each collective. The volume-versus-latency trade Figure 4 leaves implicit is explicit here.
Dwork & Roth: The Algorithmic Foundations of Differential Privacy Part XXXII §26
Free online. Chapters 2 and 3 are the definition and composition, which is all most readers need, and reading the definition once removes the temptation to treat epsilon as a dial.
Abadi et al. (2016): Deep learning with differential privacy Part XXXII §27
Clipping, noise, and the accountant that makes a long training run's budget tractable. Read §3 for the mechanism and note how much of the paper is the accounting rather than the algorithm.
Shokri et al. (2017): Membership inference attacks against machine learning models Part XXXII §30
The attack, and the connection to overfitting. Worth reading for the experimental design: it is one of the few security papers where the ground truth is available and used.
Carlini et al. (2021): Extracting training data from large language models Part XXXII §31
The leak that needs no attack. Read it beside Part XIII §21 on deduplication, which turns out to be the most effective available mitigation.
Athalye, Carlini & Wagner (2018): Obfuscated gradients give a false sense of security Part XXXII §38
Seven defences broken in one paper, all by the same mechanism. The single most useful thing to read before proposing a defence, and the source of §38's rule: if a defence works because the attacker cannot compute something, assume it will be computed.
Russell & Norvig: Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach Part XXXIII §2
The reference for this whole part, and the book this one deliberately does not try to be. Chapters 3 to 5 cover movements A, B and E at far greater length. Read it for the breadth that was trimmed here (logic, constraint satisfaction, classical planning) rather than for the parts this part measures.
Hart, Nilsson & Raphael (1968): A formal basis for the heuristic determination of minimum cost paths Part XXXIII §8
A* and its optimality proof, in the original. Short, and the proof is the same three lines §9 gives. Worth reading to see how much of what is now standard vocabulary arrived at once.
Pohl (1970): Heuristic search viewed as path finding in a graph Part XXXIII §11
Where weighted A* and its bounded-suboptimality guarantee come from. Figure 2 is a measurement of how loose that bound is, which the paper does not claim otherwise.
Korf (1985): Depth-first iterative-deepening: an optimal admissible tree search Part XXXIII §21
IDA*, and the analysis of why re-expanding everything each round costs so little. The argument that memory rather than time is the binding constraint is made here more clearly than anywhere since.
Culberson & Schaeffer (1998): Pattern databases Part XXXIII §13
Heuristics as precomputed exact solutions to projections of the problem. The additive case and its disjointness condition are the part worth the attention: it is the reason adding heuristics is usually illegal and occasionally not.
Knuth & Moore (1975): An analysis of alpha-beta pruning Part XXXIII §36
The closed form Figure 7 lands on exactly. Read it for the demonstration that "optimal move ordering" has a precise cost rather than an asymptotic one, which is rarer than it sounds.
Auer, Cesa-Bianchi & Fischer (2002): Finite-time analysis of the multiarmed bandit problem Part XXXIII §30
UCB1 and its logarithmic regret bound. The source of the exploration term that reappears in UCT, in Part XVIII's exploration bonuses, and in most places anyone writes a square root over a visit count.
Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006): Bandit based Monte-Carlo planning Part XXXIII §31
UCT: applying the bandit rule at every node of a tree. The observation that this makes the estimate converge to the value under optimal play rather than under random play is the whole paper, and it is the difference between a search and an average.
Browne et al. (2012): A survey of Monte Carlo tree search methods Part XXXIII §31
The map of the variants, from before learned priors changed the subject. Useful for seeing how many of the tricks in modern systems were already named, and under what conditions each helps.
Silver et al. (2018): A general reinforcement learning algorithm that masters chess, shogi and Go through self-play Part XXXIII §33
Search and learning as one loop: the network supplies a prior and a value, the search improves on the prior, and training on the search closes it. Read it as a policy-improvement operator in Part XXII's sense rather than as a result about board games.
Kavraki, Švestka, Latombe & Overmars (1996): Probabilistic roadmaps for path planning in high-dimensional configuration spaces Part XXXIII §26
The paper that made motion planning practical by refusing to represent the free space. The narrow-passage problem is stated honestly here, which is more than most of the follow-up literature does.
LaValle & Kuffner (2001): Randomized kinodynamic planning Part XXXIII §27
RRTs, including the Voronoi-bias argument for why the tree explores without being told to. The kinodynamic half is the part that matters for anything with a body, and it is the half usually skipped.
Karaman & Frazzoli (2011): Sampling-based algorithms for optimal motion planning Part XXXIII §28
Proves that RRT converges to a suboptimal path with probability one, then fixes it. The shrinking connection radius and its $1/d$ exponent are where the dimension reappears after sampling seemed to have removed it.
Meister, Vieira & Cotterell (2020): If beam search is the answer, what was the question? Part XXXIII §18
Takes the observation behind Figure 4 seriously: a narrow beam is not an approximation that happens to work, it is an implicit regulariser, and the paper identifies what it is regularising toward. The honest treatment of a result usually stated as folklore.
Wang et al. (2023): Self-consistency improves chain of thought reasoning in language models Part XXXIII §39
Majority voting over sampled derivations, with no verifier anywhere. Read it beside Part XIII's treatment of ensembles: it removes variance and cannot remove bias, which predicts exactly where it stops helping.
Cobbe et al. (2021): Training verifiers to solve math word problems Part XXXIII §40
Where the generator-plus-verifier shape enters the language-model literature. The result that a verifier beats fine-tuning at equal compute is the one that made everything in movement F worth building.
Lightman et al. (2023): Let's verify step by step Part XXXIII §40
Process supervision against outcome supervision, measured. The reason to pay for step-level labels is not accuracy alone: only a process verifier has an opinion about an unfinished solution, which is what a tree search needs.
Snell, Lee, Xu & Kumar (2024): Scaling LLM test-time compute optimally can be more effective than scaling model parameters Part XXXIII §42
The budget-allocation question stated as a budget-allocation question. Its finding that the best way to spend test-time compute depends on the difficulty of the question is §44's fourth test in a different vocabulary.
Stroebl, Kapoor & Narayanan (2024): Inference scaling flaws: the limits of LLM resampling with imperfect verifiers Part XXXIII §41
Figure 8's result, argued from the other direction. The claim that resampling against an imperfect verifier cannot reach the ceiling (and past a point moves away from it) is the single most useful correction to the "just sample more" reflex.
Jacot, Gabriel & Hongler (2018): Neural tangent kernel: convergence and generalization in neural networks Part XXIII §47
The result itself: in the infinite-width limit the tangent kernel is deterministic and constant during training, so gradient descent on a network is kernel regression. Read it for the derivation and read §48 and §49 for what the limit costs.
Lee et al. (2019): Wide neural networks of any depth evolve as linear models under gradient descent Part XXIII §48
The empirical companion, and the more useful of the two here because it measures the approximation rather than only proving it. The finite-width corrections it reports are the same phenomenon Figure 8 measures.
Chizat, Oyallon & Bach (2019): On lazy training in differentiable programming Part XXIII §49
Where the word comes from, and the argument that laziness is a property of the output scale rather than of the architecture, which is what makes Figure 9's sweep possible at fixed width. The paper's point is that the lazy limit is a choice, and usually not the one you want.
Yang & Hu (2021): Tensor Programs IV: feature learning in infinite-width neural networks Part XXIII §50
Proves that the standard infinite-width limits cannot learn features, and constructs the parameterisation that can. The classification of what each choice of exponent gives you is the part to read; it is why μP is one solution to a system of constraints rather than a set of tricks.
Yang et al. (2022): Tensor Programs V: tuning large neural networks via zero-shot hyperparameter transfer Part XXIII §52
The result Part XVII §34 has been using. Figure 11 is its central claim at a scale a figure script can reach; the paper's version is at a scale where the saving is measured in millions of dollars. The coordinate-check procedure in its appendix is the practical contribution.
Poole et al. (2016): Exponential expressivity in deep neural networks through transient chaos Part XXIII §53
The order-to-chaos transition and the correlation map, derived. Read it beside Figure 12's first panel; the depth scale it defines is what initialisation is really choosing.
Schoenholz et al. (2017): Deep information propagation Part XXIII §54
Turns the phase diagram into a prediction about trainability. Worth reading alongside Figure 12's third panel, which reproduces its ordered-phase prediction and not its chaotic-phase one: the asymmetry being that the ordered phase destroys information while the chaotic phase only destroys scale.
Katharopoulos et al. (2020): Transformers are RNNs: fast autoregressive transformers with linear attention Part XI §31
The reassociation, and the observation in the title. Read it for the derivation and Figure 11 for what the substitution costs: the paper is clear that it is a substitution, and the follow-up literature is often less so.
Gu, Goel & Ré (2022): Efficiently modeling long sequences with structured state spaces Part XI §34
S4: the discretised linear system as a sequence layer, the convolutional view, and the HiPPO initialisation. The part to read is why the initialisation matters, which is §12's vanishing gradient restated as a choice about where the timescales go.
Gu & Dao (2024): Mamba: linear-time sequence modeling with selective state spaces Part XI §36
Selectivity, and the hardware-aware scan that makes it trainable. Figure 14 is its central motivation at a scale a figure script can reach. Note what is given up: input-dependence destroys time invariance, so the convolution is gone and the scan is all that is left.
Blelloch (1990): Prefix sums and their applications Part XI §33
Where the work-efficient scan comes from, decades before anyone applied it to a sequence model. Worth reading to see that the algorithmic fact modern architectures depend on was available the whole time and simply had nothing to be used on.
Arora et al. (2024): Zoology: measuring and improving recall in efficient language models Part XI §35
Isolates associative recall as the thing fixed-state models lose, and shows the state size controls it. Figure 13 measures the same relationship without any training, which is a cleaner demonstration that the limit belongs to the representation rather than to the optimiser.
Beltagy, Peters & Cohan (2020): Longformer: the long-document transformer Part XI §37
Sliding-window attention with a few global positions. The earliest widely used version of the hybrid argument in §37: keep exact attention where it is needed and bound the cache everywhere else.
French (1999): Catastrophic forgetting in connectionist networks Part XX §47
The problem stated clearly, long before anyone was doing sequential post-training on production models. Worth reading to see how little the diagnosis has changed and how much the setting has.
Kirkpatrick et al. (2017): Overcoming catastrophic forgetting in neural networks Part XX §48
Elastic weight consolidation: penalise movement in the directions the old task cared about. Read it as the principled version of §47's finding that forgetting is a distance: the Fisher information decides which directions the distance should be measured in.
Frankle et al. (2020): Linear mode connectivity and the lottery ticket hypothesis Part XX §49
Where the barrier in Figure 7 comes from, and the observation that sharing an early portion of training is what puts two models in the same basin. The precondition for everything in §49.
Wortsman et al. (2022): Model soups: averaging weights of multiple fine-tuned models Part XX §49
Averaging fine-tunes of one base model, at scale, and getting a better model for no extra inference cost. Note how carefully the setup satisfies the shared-ancestor condition; the result does not survive relaxing it.
Ilharco et al. (2023): Editing models with task arithmetic Part XX §50
Task vectors, added and negated. Figure 8 reproduces the effect and adds the control the claim needs: what each fine-tune already scores on the other task. The interference-versus-angle relationship is the part that generalises.
Meng et al. (2022): Locating and editing factual associations in GPT Part XX §51
ROME: the rank-one update Figure 9 abstracts. Read it with the follow-up work on sequential editing, which finds the accumulation problem the figure measures: a single edit is exact and a thousand are a slow deletion.
Cover & Thomas: Elements of Information Theory, chapter 10 Part XV §43
Rate–distortion theory, derived. The Gaussian case Figure 11 measures against is worked in full, and the chapter is unusually clear that the function belongs to the source rather than to any method.
Gersho & Gray: Vector Quantization and Signal Compression Part XV §43
Where the quarter-bit space-filling constant comes from, and the lattice results. Read it for the distinction Figure 11 turns into a finding: the quantiser that is optimal at a fixed number of levels is not the one that is optimal at a fixed entropy.
Rissanen (1978): Modeling by shortest data description Part XV §44
MDL in the original. Short, and the argument that a model is a code for its data is made more plainly here than in most of what followed.
Grünwald: The Minimum Description Length Principle Part XV §44
The modern treatment, including the part §47 flags: code lengths depend on the code, so MDL relocates arbitrariness rather than removing it. The chapters on universal codes are where that relocation is made rigorous.
Tishby, Pereira & Bialek (1999): The information bottleneck method Part XV §45
The objective and its fixed-point iteration, which is what Figure 13 runs. Read it as a statement of what a representation is for; the later claims about deep learning's compression phase are a separate and more contested matter.
Saxe et al. (2019): On the information bottleneck theory of deep learning Part XV §47
The rebuttal, and the reason §47 treats the bottleneck as a description rather than an explanation. Its central point is that mutual information in a deterministic network is either infinite or an artefact of the binning, so the compression phase depends on a choice nobody reports.

§6 Practical notes

Where to find them. Nearly everything published since 2012 is free on arXiv. Older papers are usually findable through an author's page or an institutional repository. When a paper is behind a paywall, the authors' own copy is very often a search away, and emailing an author for a copy works more often than people expect.

Preprints are not peer reviewed. arXiv is a posting service, not a journal. That is mostly good (it is why the field moves quickly), but it means the filtering is yours to do. §3's questions are how you do it, and the pitfall box is what to watch for.

Follow citations backwards, not forwards. When a paper uses a technique you do not know, find the paper it cites for that technique, and repeat. Two or three hops usually reaches something foundational and clear. Searching forwards through everything that has cited a paper is how you drown.

Reproduce one number. Nothing establishes whether you understood a paper like implementing its central experiment and obtaining roughly the reported result. When you cannot, the reason is informative: sometimes the problem is in your implementation, and sometimes the problem is in the paper.

Why this section exists

The material in this book has an end. The field does not.

Everything in the parts that follow was a paper before it was a chapter, and the papers being written now will be the chapters of whatever replaces this. Being able to read them (quickly, critically, without deference) is the difference between a practitioner who can only use what has been packaged and one who can evaluate what is new before it is packaged.

It is also, unlike most skills in this book, one that improves fastest by doing it badly at first. Pick a paper on something you already understand from these parts and read it this week. The first one is slow. The tenth is not.
