§0.2 What is actually new here

One idea, and it is the one that makes the subject a subject: performing well on data you have already seen is easy and worthless. The problem is data you have not seen.

That single distinction (between fitting and generalising) separates machine learning from curve fitting, from database lookup, and from optimisation as Part III described it. Part III's problem was reaching the minimum of a function. Here you will find that reaching that minimum is frequently the wrong thing to do.

Why this matters

Everything in the parts that follow (networks, convolution, attention, generative models) is an answer to a question posed in this part. What model class should we use? How do we stop it memorising? How do we make it work when the input has a million dimensions?

Sections 21 to 23 are the ones that matter most for what comes next. They explain, concretely and with measurement, why the classical algorithms in Sections 12–18 hit a wall on the problems this field cares about, and therefore why anyone bothered building something deeper.

§0.3 A note on the algorithms

Sections 12 to 18 cover the classical methods: linear and logistic regression, support vector machines, kernels, nearest neighbours, decision trees, k-means. They are not historical curiosities.

Each is still the right tool for some problems, each illustrates an idea that recurs in deeper models, and each is simple enough to understand completely, which makes them the right place to learn what "understanding a model completely" feels like. Linear regression is the whole of supervised learning with the difficulty removed, and everything true of it is true, in complicated form, of a transformer.

§0.4 The habit for this part

Always ask what the model cannot represent.

Every learning algorithm embeds assumptions about what patterns exist. Linear regression assumes a straight line. Nearest neighbours assumes nearby inputs have similar outputs. Trees assume axis-aligned splits matter. Those assumptions are not defects (Section 7 proves that learning is impossible without them), but they determine what the model can and cannot do, and they are usually invisible until they fail.

When a model performs badly, the question is rarely "is this model bad?" It is "what did this model assume, and is that assumption false here?"
