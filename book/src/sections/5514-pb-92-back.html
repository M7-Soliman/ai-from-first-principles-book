BuildThe project

Twelve to sixteen hours. You build an inference toolkit from the joint distribution upward (exact, variational and sampled) and use it to measure each method's error against an answer you can compute, which is a luxury this subject offers and almost no other does.

P.1 What you are building

joint: graphical models with d-separation, variable elimination, forward–backward, a Kalman filter, EM, mean-field and full-covariance variational inference, Metropolis–Hastings, Gibbs and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, and a diffusion sampler in both its forms.

The rule: numpy only, and every approximation compared against something exact. This part is the one place where that is always possible, and a method you have not compared is a method you are trusting.

P.2 Stage 1: structure

  1. Represent a directed model and build its full joint. Check it sums to one.
  2. Implement d-separation.
  3. Sample from a chain, a fork and a collider, and measure conditional correlations.
  4. Confirm the graph algorithm and the samples agree on every pair.

Deliverable: the agreement table. Step four is the point: d-separation is easy to implement subtly wrong and it does not complain when you do.

P.3 Stage 2: exact inference

  1. Implement variable elimination. Report the largest intermediate factor under three elimination orders.
  2. Implement forward–backward and check it against brute-force enumeration.
  3. Run the forward recursion unscaled on a long sequence and watch it underflow. Then fix it.
  4. Implement Viterbi and construct a case where the best path differs from the per-state argmax.
  5. Implement a Kalman filter and check it against the analytic posterior for the prior you gave it.

Deliverable: the two exactness checks and the elimination-order table. Step three is worth doing before you need it.

P.4 Stage 3: learning with latents

  1. Implement EM for a Gaussian mixture, asserting monotonicity every iteration.
  2. Sweep the component separation and measure how often restarts miss the true components.
  3. Choose your recovery criterion so that it discriminates at both ends of the sweep, and say why a fixed likelihood threshold does not.
  4. Check separately whether the best restart recovers them.

Deliverable: the sweep with error bars, and one paragraph distinguishing the separations where EM failed from the ones where the data could not answer.

P.5 Stage 4: variational inference

  1. Derive and implement mean field for a correlated Gaussian.
  2. Verify it independently by gradient ascent on the ELBO.
  3. Sweep the correlation and compare against √(1−ρ²).
  4. Fit a full-covariance family and confirm the error disappears.
  5. Fit a bimodal target with each direction of the KL.

Deliverable: Figure 4 reproduced, plus the full-covariance result. Step two matters because it separates a claim about the objective from a claim about the optimiser.

P.6 Stage 5: sampling

  1. Write down your predictions first, in a file, before running anything.
  2. Implement Metropolis–Hastings and sweep the proposal scale, reporting acceptance rate and effective sample size together.
  3. Implement Gibbs and run it on a strongly correlated target.
  4. Implement HMC with leapfrog, and verify energy conservation before trusting anything.
  5. Measure effective samples per evaluation for all three, across condition numbers.
  6. Reproduce Figure 6 both ways.
  7. Score your predictions.

Deliverable: the efficiency curves, the two R̂ values, and the scored predictions. Step four's energy check is what distinguishes a working leapfrog from one with a sign error, and the sign error produces samples.

P.7 Stage 6: continuous time

  1. Simulate the forward SDE and check its marginal against the closed form.
  2. On a mixture whose exact score you can compute, implement the reverse SDE and the probability-flow ODE.
  3. Sweep step count and compare both against the target.
  4. Run each twenty-four times from one starting point and measure the spread.
  5. Replace Euler with a second-order solver and re-run step three.

Deliverable: the step-count curves for both integrators and both solvers. Step five is where the folk claim about the ODE becomes true, and knowing which step made it true is the lesson.

P.8 Stage 7: transport

  1. Implement W₁ in one dimension by sorting.
  2. Compare it with KL as two distributions separate, through and past the point where their supports part.
  3. Implement Sinkhorn and watch the coupling sharpen as the regularisation falls.

Deliverable: Figure 8 reproduced, and the regularisation at which Sinkhorn stops converging.

P.9 Stretch

  • A junction tree, with the clique sizes reported.
  • Loopy belief propagation, against exact answers, including a case where it oscillates.
  • A structured variational family, with the recovered variance measured.
  • The amortisation gap, measured on a VAE you train.
  • A particle filter, with its degeneracy tracked against state dimension.
  • A recovery check on an inference method you actually rely on.

P.10 Done when

  • Your d-separation implementation agrees with sampled correlations on every pair.
  • You have seen an unscaled forward recursion underflow.
  • Forward–backward matches enumeration to machine precision.
  • Your Kalman filter matches the analytic posterior for the prior you gave it.
  • EM's monotonicity assertion has never fired.
  • You can say which of your EM failures were optimisation and which were identifiability.
  • Mean field's variance error matches √(1−ρ²) by two independent routes.
  • You have a full-covariance fit that removes the error entirely.
  • Your HMC conserves energy.
  • You report effective sample size rather than draws.
  • You have produced R̂ = 1.00 on an answer you know is wrong.
  • Your two diffusion samplers agree on the marginals and differ on determinism.
  • You know which solver order makes the ODE cheaper than the SDE.
  • You wrote predictions down before Stage 5 and scored them afterwards.
  • Your README reports one method whose intervals failed a recovery check.
Why this project

Every other part of this book compares methods against each other. Here you can compare them against the truth: the exact posterior, the analytic optimum, the closed-form variational solution, the target's own moments. A method's error stops being a matter of opinion.

Stage 5 step six is the one to do even if you skip the rest. Producing a perfect convergence diagnostic on an answer you know is wrong changes permanently how much weight you give one.

The framework, and the posterior

Dataset, model family, loss, optimiser. Part XXV said the family is a prior about the shape of the function; Part XXVI said the prior can be a symmetry; Part XXVII said a symmetry picks a basis. This part says what happens when you write the model down as a distribution rather than a function: the loss becomes a likelihood, fitting becomes inference, and the answer is a posterior rather than a point.

What you get is uncertainty that comes from the model rather than being bolted on, which is Part XXIV's subject arriving from underneath. What you pay is that the posterior is almost never computable, so you choose between an approximation whose error does not shrink and a sampler that cannot tell you when it has arrived. That choice, not the model, is usually what decides whether a probabilistic system works.
