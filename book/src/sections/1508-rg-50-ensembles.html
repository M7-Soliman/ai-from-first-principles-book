§18 Bagging, and why averaging works

In 60 seconds

Train several models on resampled copies of the data and average their predictions. Variance falls like $1/k$ and bias does not move, so the ensemble is strictly better than its members whenever the members are wrong in different ways. The catch is entirely in that word.

The argument is Part V §3's, and it is worth doing because the assumption it needs is exactly where the technique fails.

Worked: where the $1/k$ comes from, and what it assumes

Let $k$ models make errors $\epsilon_i$, each with mean zero and variance $v$, and let $c = \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_i \epsilon_j]$ be the average covariance between any two. The ensemble's error is the mean, and its expected squared value is

$$\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\tfrac1k\sum_i \epsilon_i\Big)^2\Big] = \frac{1}{k}v + \frac{k-1}{k}c$$

Read the two extremes.

Perfectly correlated errors ($c = v$): the expression collapses to $v$. The ensemble is exactly as bad as one member, and you have spent $k$ times the compute for nothing.

Uncorrelated errors ($c = 0$): it collapses to $v/k$. Error falls in proportion to the ensemble size, with no ceiling.

Everything in between interpolates. The benefit is not the averaging; it is the disagreement, and the second term is a floor you cannot get below however many models you add.

Why this is a variance-only instrument

Bagging is the one technique in this part that does not trade bias for variance. It removes variance and leaves bias alone, which sounds strictly better and comes with a matching restriction.

It can only help a model whose problem is variance. Averaging fifty underfitting models gives you one underfitting model, at fifty times the cost: every member is wrong in the same direction, so $c = v$ and the first term never appears.

Which makes Part VI §6's diagnosis a prerequisite rather than a nicety. Large gap, averaging helps. Both errors high and close, it does nothing. This is the clearest case in the book of a remedy that requires the diagnosis first.

Pitfall: the bootstrap is not free, and the comparison matters

The $1/k$ result compares an ensemble against one of its own members. It does not compare it against a model fitted to all your data, and those are different questions.

A bootstrap resample of $n$ points contains only about 63% unique ones (the rest are duplicates), so every member is trained on materially less information than you have. Measured on the same degree-6 fit setup, over 300 independent training sets: a single full-data fit had variance 0.0232, a single bootstrap member 0.1839, and a 20-member bootstrap ensemble 0.0362. These numbers are computed by the generator.

Read those three numbers carefully. Averaging did exactly what the algebra promised: it cut variance five-fold relative to its members. And the ensemble still did not beat the single full-data fit, because the resampling had cost more than the averaging recovered.

So bagging is a genuine win when the members are unstable for reasons other than having too little data (deep trees, high-degree fits on plentiful data) and can lose outright on small samples. When the data is scarce, ensembling over random seeds or initialisations, with every member seeing all of it, is usually the better trade.

The capacity-raising cousin

Bagging is worth contrasting with the ensemble method it is usually taught alongside, because the two point in opposite directions. Boosting builds its members sequentially rather than independently: each new member is trained to correct the errors the ensemble so far still makes, typically starting from members too weak to fit much of anything on their own. Where a bagged ensemble's capacity is $k$ copies of one model's capacity, a boosted ensemble's capacity grows with every member added, since each one targets whatever the combination has not yet explained.

That makes boosting the wrong entry for this part: it is closer to a way of buying capacity a member at a time than to a preference among equally-good fits, and by §1's definition a technique that reduces training error without the generalisation trade is not a regulariser at all. It shares a form with bagging (many models, one prediction) and almost nothing else, which is worth naming precisely because the shared form invites treating them as two flavours of the same idea, and they are not.

§19 Dropout

In 60 seconds

During training, delete each unit independently with probability $p$ and rescale what survives. At test time keep everything. It is bagging over $2^n$ networks that share weights, done at the cost of one, and it also stops units relying on each other, which may matter more.

Go and read

Srivastava et al. (2014), Dropout: a simple way to prevent neural networks from overfitting. The paper that made dropout standard. Read it for the ensemble argument, and notice how much of the case is empirical: the method was adopted on evidence, and the theoretical account has been argued over ever since without the practice changing. That pattern recurs often enough in this field to be worth naming.

The procedure is one line and its two explanations are quite different.

Explanation one: an ensemble you get for free

Each training step deletes a random subset of units, so each step trains a different subnetwork. With $n$ units there are $2^n$ possible subnetworks, and over a long run you have trained an enormous number of them; a hundred units gives more subnetworks than there are atoms in the observable universe.

Bagging with $2^{100}$ members is not otherwise available. Dropout gets it because the members share their weights: a weight trained inside one subnetwork is the same weight another subnetwork uses. The models are not independent, which by §18 means the variance reduction is less than $1/k$, but $k$ is astronomically large, and the ensemble is free.

Why the rescaling

A unit that survives with probability $1-p$ contributes, on average, $(1-p)$ times what it would contribute undropped. If nothing corrected for that, the sums arriving at the next layer would be systematically small during training and systematically large at test time, when nothing is dropped.

Inverted dropout (the standard) divides the surviving activations by $1-p$ during training, so the expected input to the next layer is unchanged and test time needs no adjustment at all. That is why model.eval() can simply switch dropout off rather than rescaling anything, and it is the arrangement Part IV §14 measured.

Explanation two: no unit may rely on any other

The second account may matter more in practice and is easier to state.

Without dropout, units can co-adapt: unit A learns to produce a broken feature that unit B learns to correct, and the pair is useful only together. That is a fragile arrangement and it is exactly the kind of thing that fits noise.

With dropout, B might be absent on any given step, so A cannot count on it. Every unit must be independently useful. The resulting features are more redundant and more robust, which is a preference in §1's sense, and a different one from "weights should be small".

Pitfall: three ways dropout is misapplied

Left on at evaluation. Part IV §14's silent bug: accuracy is depressed and irreproducible, and nothing raises. Figure 4 of that part measured it.

Applied to a small model. Dropout removes capacity, so a model that was already underfitting gets worse. The right panel above shows the far side of the U; a model with too little capacity starts there.

Stacked on everything else. Dropout, weight decay, augmentation and early stopping all at their usual defaults is a large total: see §12's Try it. Underfitting from accumulated regularization is common and looks like a model that "just is not learning".

§20 Adversarial training

Go and read

Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy (2015), Explaining and harnessing adversarial examples. The paper that reframed the phenomenon: adversarial examples come from linearity in high dimensions, not from anything exotic about depth, which is the argument the intuition box below reproduces, and which the linear classifier in Figure 8 is enough to demonstrate. It also introduces the fast gradient sign method that figure uses.

The last technique starts from an unsettling observation rather than a preference.

Take a trained classifier and an input it gets right. Compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the input (not the weights) and take a small step in the direction that increases the loss. The perturbation can be far too small to matter, and the classifier is now confidently wrong.

Intuition: why a linear model is enough to see it

The figure uses a linear classifier, which is worth noticing: adversarial examples are often presented as a mystery about deep networks, and the core of the effect needs no depth at all.

The output is $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}$. Perturb the input by $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ and the output moves by $\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\eta}$. Choose $\boldsymbol{\eta} = \epsilon\,\text{sign}(\mathbf{w})$ and every coordinate contributes with the same sign, so the total change is $\epsilon\|\mathbf{w}\|_1$, which grows with the dimension even though each coordinate moved by only $\epsilon$.

That is the whole phenomenon. In high dimensions, many individually negligible changes aligned with the weights add up to something decisive, while the same changes in random directions cancel (Part V §3's $\sqrt{n}$ against the alignment's $n$). It is a fact about dimension, not about depth.

Adversarial training generates such perturbed examples during training and includes them, which is augmentation from §10 with the transformations chosen by the model's own weaknesses rather than by domain knowledge. It improves robustness measurably. It is also expensive (each step needs an extra forward and backward pass to build the attack) and it typically costs a little clean accuracy, which is §1's trade appearing yet again.

A variant worth naming ties this section back to §17. Virtual adversarial training needs no label at all: instead of perturbing toward the direction that increases the loss against the true $y$, it perturbs toward the direction that changes the model's own predicted distribution the most, then trains the model to agree with itself across that perturbation. Nothing in that recipe uses a label, so it runs on unlabelled data exactly where §17's argument lives, and it is the same trade seen once more under a new name: a local-smoothness preference, this time bought with a gradient rather than with domain knowledge or a cluster assumption.
