Part CBoosting

Bagging attacks variance by averaging independent fits. Boosting attacks bias by fitting each model to what the previous ones left behind: a completely different mechanism that happens to use the same base learner. It is the method that wins tabular competitions, and the reason is as much engineering as statistics.

§16 Additive modelling

Build the function in stages

Rather than fitting one model, build $F(x) = \sum_{m} \nu\, f_m(x)$ by adding one term at a time, each chosen to reduce the loss given everything already added.

Nothing here is specific to trees. What makes trees the right base learner is that they are cheap, they need no feature scaling, they handle mixed types, and, most importantly, they are high bias when shallow, which is what a stagewise procedure wants. A boosted ensemble of deep trees works far less well, and the reason is that each term is supposed to be a small correction.

§17 Gradient boosting, derived

Go and read

Friedman (2001), Greedy function approximation: a gradient boosting machine. The derivation this section follows, generalising boosting to gradient descent where the parameter being optimised is the function itself. Read §4 for shrinkage: exactly what Figure 4 measures below, in the paper that introduced it. Then Chen & Guestrin (2016), XGBoost: a scalable tree boosting system, the engineering realisation fifteen years later: read §2 for the second-order gain expression that scores a split and sets the leaf in one computation, and the unusually candid systems section on why it is fast.

Gradient descent in function space

Treat the loss as a function of the predictions $F(x_i)$ rather than of parameters. The negative gradient at each training point,

$$g_i = -\left.\frac{\partial \ell(y_i, F)}{\partial F}\right|_{F = F(x_i)}$$

is the direction each prediction should move. That direction exists only at the training points, so fit a tree to it (the tree is how the step generalises to inputs you have not seen) and take a step of size $\nu$ along the result.

For squared loss $g_i$ is the residual, which recovers the intuitive "fit what is left over". For logistic loss it is $y_i - p_i$. Part IX's gradient descent, with the function itself as the parameter.

The second-order refinement

Using the curvature as well gives the leaf value in closed form. With gradients $g_i$, curvatures $h_i$ and an $L^2$ penalty $\lambda$ on the leaf,

$$w^{*} = \frac{\sum_{i \in \text{leaf}} g_i}{\sum_{i \in \text{leaf}} h_i + \lambda}, \qquad \text{gain} = \frac{\left(\sum g_i\right)^2}{\sum h_i + \lambda}$$

The same expression scores a candidate split and sets the leaf, which is what makes the split search and the leaf fit one computation. This is the form modern implementations use, and Part IX §19's damped Newton method is what it is.

The $\text{gain}$ above is the per-child objective term. A full split score, as in Chen & Guestrin (2016) Eq. 7, takes this term for the left child plus this term for the right child, subtracts the parent's version of the same term, subtracts a per-leaf penalty $\gamma$ for the extra leaf created, and carries an overall factor of $\tfrac{1}{2}$. None of the three changes which split maximises the score (the parent term and $\gamma$ are the same additive constant for every candidate at a node, and $\tfrac{1}{2}$ scales every candidate alike), so the simpler expression above is what to carry in your head, and the fuller one is what §19's penalties act on.

§18 Shrinkage

Two things the figure settles

Boosting can overfit. The folk claim that it does not comes from AdaBoost's margin behaviour on separable classification, and it does not transfer. With enough trees and a large step, test error turns around.

The number of trees and the learning rate are one hyperparameter, not two. Halving $\nu$ roughly doubles the trees needed. The practical procedure is to fix $\nu$ as small as the compute budget tolerates and choose the number of trees by early stopping on a validation set, which turns a two-dimensional search into a one-dimensional one, done for free during fitting.

History: from a reweighting trick to a gradient

1997 Freund & Schapire's AdaBoost reweighted the training set after every round, upweighting whatever the previous classifier had got wrong, and came with a proof that training error fell toward zero. It was presented as a reweighting scheme, not as an optimisation.

1999 Breiman's Prediction games and arcing algorithms recast AdaBoost as a form of gradient descent in a space of functions. 2000 Friedman, Hastie & Tibshirani's Additive logistic regression showed, from a different direction, that AdaBoost was already fitting an additive model by stagewise optimisation of one specific exponential loss.

2001 Friedman's Greedy function approximation generalised that observation to gradient descent in function space for any differentiable loss, the derivation above. AdaBoost's margin-maximising behaviour on separable data, the source of the "boosting doesn't overfit" folklore, is a property of the exponential loss on that kind of problem, and it was carried across to the general method by reputation rather than by proof. Figure 4 is that carry-over failing.

2016 Chen & Guestrin's XGBoost added the second-order refinement above and, as much as any single idea, an implementation fast enough to iterate on from a laptop, which is most of why gradient boosting displaced AdaBoost in practice, and it was an engineering advance rather than a statistical one.

§19 The other regularisers

Four, and what each restrains

Tree depth or leaf count. Caps the order of interaction each term can express: depth $k$ allows interactions of order $k$. This is the most consequential structural choice and 3 to 6 covers most problems.

Minimum child weight. Refuses splits whose child carries too little curvature, which prevents leaves fitted to a handful of points.

Row and column subsampling. Bagging's randomness inside boosting. Cheap, and it regularises and accelerates at once.

Explicit penalties: $\lambda$ on the leaf values, $\gamma$ per additional leaf. These enter the gain expression directly, so a split is not taken unless it pays for itself.

Also available: a monotonic constraint forces a leaf's prediction to move in one direction as a chosen feature increases, encoding a known sign directly rather than hoping the data implies it, supported by XGBoost and LightGBM, and worth reaching for whenever domain knowledge already gives you the sign for free.

§20 Categorical features

Three encodings, and the trap in the best one

One-hot. Safe, and it makes a high-cardinality column into hundreds of nearly-empty binary features that trees split on badly.

Ordered by target statistic. Sort the categories by their mean target and treat the result as ordinal. For squared loss this is provably the optimal split of the categories, and it is what good implementations do.

Target encoding. Replace the category with its target mean outright.

The last two share a leak: the target mean of a category is computed from rows that include the row being encoded, so the feature carries the answer. On a rare category with one row it carries it exactly. The repair is to compute the statistic out-of-fold or on preceding rows only, and omitting it is one of the most common ways a tabular model looks excellent in validation and fails in production: Part XIII §11's leakage, in the encoder.

§21 Missing values

A tree can learn where to send them

Rather than imputing, send missing values to whichever child gives the better gain, and learn that direction at each split. It costs one extra evaluation per split and needs no assumption about what the missingness means.

This is a genuine advantage over the alternatives, and it has a consequence worth stating: the model can exploit missingness that is informative. If a field is blank precisely when the outcome is unusual, the tree will find it, which is useful when the pattern will persist and is a trap when it is an artefact of how the training data was collected.

§22 Histogram splitting, measured

Why the coarser grid can win

A finer grid offers more thresholds, and more thresholds means more chances for one of them to fit noise. Part XIV §20's biased maximum again: the best of 255 candidates is more optimistically biased than the best of 32.

So binning is doing two jobs. The one it is famous for is speed. The one it is not is regularisation of the split choice, and on this data that is worth as much.

§23 What the implementations actually differ on

Go and read

Ke et al. (2017), LightGBM: a highly efficient gradient boosting decision tree. Histogram splitting, leaf-wise growth and the sibling-subtraction trick of §4, all argued and measured in one paper. Read it for the case that most of what separates a fast implementation from a slow one is engineering rather than statistics, which is the claim this section makes about all three libraries below.

Three libraries, three choices

XGBoost introduced the second-order formulation of §17 with explicit $\lambda$ and $\gamma$ penalties, and grows trees level by level.

LightGBM grows leaf-wise, always splitting the leaf with the highest gain, which reaches a given loss with fewer leaves and overfits more readily, so it needs its leaf count capped. Its histogram implementation and the sibling-subtraction trick of §4 are the source of most of its speed.

CatBoost attacks §20's leak directly with ordered target statistics, computing each row's encoding from rows preceding it in a random permutation.

None of these differences is large next to getting the validation right, which is §29.

§24 What movement C establishes

Three things

Boosting is gradient descent with the function as the parameter, and the tree is how a step defined at the training points generalises off them.

It overfits, and its two main hyperparameters are one. Figure 4 shows the turn at $\nu = 1$ after 46 trees, and small rates still improving at the budget, so fix the rate low and early-stop the count.

The engineering is not incidental. Figure 5's binning buys a factor of 125 in candidate splits and improves accuracy up to a point, and the categorical encoding that works best is the one that leaks unless computed out-of-fold.
