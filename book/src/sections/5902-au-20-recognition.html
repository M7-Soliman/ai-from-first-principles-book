Part BRecognition

Turning audio into text is an alignment problem before it is a classification problem: the input has ten times as many frames as the output has symbols, and nobody labelled which frame is which. Three answers exist, and the oldest one is still deployed because of what it costs rather than what it assumes.

§9 The alignment problem

Why this is not sequence-to-sequence classification

A second of speech is a hundred frames and perhaps a dozen characters. The mapping is monotone (audio and text advance together) and unaligned: no annotation says where each character begins.

You could label the alignment by hand, and the field did for years. The alternative is to treat the alignment as a latent variable and sum over every one of them, which is Part XXVIII §12 arriving in a new place: the same forward recursion, for the same reason.

§10 The hybrid system, and why it persisted

Acoustic model, pronunciation dictionary, language model

The classical stack scores acoustic frames against phone states with a hidden Markov model, maps phones to words with a hand-built dictionary, and rescores with a separate language model. Three components, three training procedures, one decoder combining them.

It survived long after end-to-end systems were competitive, and the reason is worth knowing: the language model is separate, so a new vocabulary is a dictionary edit rather than a retraining, and the decoder can be constrained to a grammar exactly. Both matter in production and neither is visible in a word-error-rate comparison.

Summing over alignments

Emit a distribution over the vocabulary plus a blank at every frame. A frame sequence maps to an output by deleting repeats and then deleting blanks, so a a ∅ a b becomes a a b. The probability of a target is the sum over every frame sequence that collapses to it, and that sum has the same forward recursion as an HMM's.

The blank is the load-bearing part of the design: without it, repeated symbols could not be expressed at all, because consecutive identical frames collapse.

§13 What CTC assumes

Conditional independence, stated precisely

CTC's probability of a frame sequence is a product of per-frame terms given the input. There is no term coupling one output symbol to another: given the audio, what the model emits at frame 40 is independent of what it emitted at frame 39.

That is what makes the forward recursion possible and it is a strong claim about language. Text is not conditionally independent given audio: "recognise speech" and "wreck a nice beach" differ by a language prior, not by an acoustic one.

§14 What that assumption costs

Where the mass goes, which is not where you would guess

The leaked probability lands on a and b, the single-symbol outputs, and not on aa or bb. The reason is the collapse rule: the frame sequence a a becomes a, not aa, so producing a repeated symbol requires a blank between and the "obvious" wrong answers are unreachable.

The practical consequence is the familiar one: a CTC model needs an external language model at decode time, and that is not a refinement. It is supplying the term the factorisation removed.

§15 Transducers and attention

Go and read

Graves (2012), Sequence transduction with recurrent neural networks. The prediction network and the joint network in the box below are this paper's construction: an RNN driven by the model's own emitted history, combined with the acoustic encoder through a small feedforward join, trained by summing over alignments the same way CTC is: over a lattice with one more branch at every step, for "emit" against "move forward." Read the forward–backward recursion over that lattice to see that the fix for CTC's independence assumption lives entirely in what the network conditions on, not in how the alignment sum itself is computed.

Two ways to put the missing term back

The transducer. Add a prediction network over the output history and combine it with the acoustic encoder at every step. The output is no longer conditionally independent, the alignment is still summed over, and streaming still works because the model never looks ahead. This is what most deployed on-device recognisers are.

Attention encoder–decoder. Drop the monotonicity assumption and let a decoder attend anywhere. The most flexible, the best on long-form audio with enough data, and it cannot stream without modification; and it can loop or truncate, because nothing enforces that the output advances with the input.

Note the shape of the trade: CTC assumes the most and is cheapest to decode; attention assumes the least and can fail in ways CTC structurally cannot.
