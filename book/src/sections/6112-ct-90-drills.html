PracticeDrills

Forty-three drills. The harness in parts/31-control/drills/ covers twenty of them with twenty-eight tests. Numpy only, and every plant written down, which is why several of these assert an exact optimum rather than a tolerance.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · What a safety claim costs

You want to claim fewer than one serious failure per 107 miles, at 95% confidence, from a fleet seeing no failures at all. Before computing anything, write down the miles required. Then write down how many vehicles driving 30,000 miles a year for one year that is, and how much longer it takes to estimate the rate rather than bound it.

Answer. 3×107 miles from the rule of three, which is 1,000 vehicles for a year. Estimating the rate to within 20% needs about 96 events rather than zero, so roughly 109 miles, thirty times more, and about 32,000 vehicle-years. Almost everyone underestimates the second by an order of magnitude, and the gap between bounding and estimating is what makes a safety case hard. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

Warm-upW1 · Three things that change

What is different about a model whose output acts on the world?

Answer. The data distribution is a consequence of the policy; errors accumulate rather than being independent; and being right too late is being wrong. The third is the one machine-learning training omits entirely.
Warm-upW2 · What feedback buys

Why does a thermostat need no thermodynamics?

Answer. The closed-loop response depends on the plant gain only through kpg/(1+kpg), which tends to one however large or small g is. High loop gain makes the system's own parameters nearly irrelevant.
Warm-upW3 · Read Figure 1

At what gain is feedback worse than no feedback, and why?

Answer. At kp = 1 the closed loop errs by 0.455 against open loop's 0.167. A proportional loop leaves an offset of 1/(1+kpg) even with a perfect model, and at low gain that offset exceeds the model error it was meant to suppress.
Warm-upW4 · What the integral term does

Why does integral action remove the offset exactly rather than reducing it?

Answer. At equilibrium the accumulated error must be constant, which requires the error itself to be zero. Any steady offset keeps driving the input until it disappears.
Warm-upW5 · Read Figure 2

What happens above ki = 110, and is that the plant's real margin?

Answer. The loop diverges, but 110 is where the figure's dt = 0.02 forward-Euler simulation diverges, not the continuous plant's own boundary. That boundary has a closed form: the characteristic polynomial 0.15s³ + 1.15s² + 9s + ki gives a Routh–Hurwitz crossing at ki ≈ 69. Exact tracking and stability margin are still traded by one number, and the margin exists only because the actuator cannot move instantly, but measure it against the closed form, not the discretisation.
Warm-upW6 · Latency is stability

Why is a 200 ms perception stack a control problem and not only a performance one?

Answer. It is 200 ms of dead time in the loop. A correction computed from an old measurement can be pushing the wrong way by the time it lands, and past a point the loop oscillates and then diverges. No amount of accuracy compensates.
Warm-upW7 · When not to learn

You know the dynamics exactly. What should you do?

Answer. Solve for the controller. Learning a policy for a system you have written down is spending samples to rediscover something six lines of linear algebra produce optimally.
Warm-upW8 · Read Figure 3

How far does hand tuning get you, as the state grows?

Answer. Within 0.1% on a double integrator, 78× worse on a triple, and on five integrators no PD gain stabilises it at all. The intuition that works on position and velocity gives nothing on five coupled states.
Warm-upW9 · A clip is not a constraint

Why is clipping an optimal controller's output different from constraining it?

Answer. The controller keeps planning as though the limit were absent, so it never chooses the differently shaped trajectory that stays inside. For a state limit clipping is not even available: by the time the state is out of bounds, the decision was several steps ago.
Warm-upW10 · Why cloning drifts

A cloned policy has a small per-step error. Why does its trajectory error grow faster than that suggests?

Answer. Its own errors take it to states the expert never visited, where it is worse, which takes it further off. More expert data does not fix it; data from the states the learner reaches does.
Warm-upW11 · Read Figure 6

The same model error on a stable and an unstable plant. What happens to the rollout?

Answer. It compounds at 1.0667× per step on the unstable one and shrinks at 0.9651× on the stable one. The usable horizon is set by the plant, not by the model, so stabilise before you learn.
Warm-upW12 · Read Figure 7

Where does exhaustive planning stop?

Answer. About six degrees of freedom: 12× more cells per joint, 4.3×108 at eight. A sampling planner needs 1.32× per joint and gives up optimality and any bound on time.
Warm-upW13 · Read Figure 8

A million miles with no failures. What does that establish?

Answer. That the rate is below one per 3×105 miles, at 95% confidence. Three hundred times weaker than a million miles sounds, and the rule of three is the whole calculation.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Open loop against closed

Simulate a first-order plant with a mis-estimated gain, both ways, and sweep the loop gain.

Answer. Figure 1's crossing. The residual offset is exactly 1/(1+kpg), which you should check against the simulation rather than assume.
CoreC2 · Add the integral term

Implement PI control and find the gain at which it diverges.

Answer. There is no such gain without lag: a PI loop on a pure first-order plant is unconditionally stable. Add an actuator lag and the threshold appears, which is why every real loop has one.
CoreC3 · Add dead time

Delay the measurement by k steps and find the gain at which the loop oscillates.

Answer. It falls fast with delay. Then convert your perception latency into steps and read off what loop gain you are allowed, a calculation almost nobody in a machine-learning team performs.
CoreC4 · Solve the Riccati recursion

Implement the LQR iteration and verify it converges.

Answer. Six lines. Check that the closed-loop eigenvalues of A − BK are inside the unit circle: if they are not, your Q, R or sign convention is wrong, and the cost will still look finite over a short rollout.
CoreC5 · Nothing beats LQR

Search over gains of a restricted form and confirm none costs less.

Answer. None can, for that cost, by construction. If your search finds one, your rollout is too short, your cost is not the one LQR was given, or your recursion has not converged.
CoreC6 · The tuning cliff

Reproduce Figure 3 on integrator chains of increasing length.

Answer. Fine on two, 78× on three, nothing stabilises five. Note also what happens if you tune the LQR direction instead: it recovers LQR exactly and the comparison becomes vacuous, which is a trap worth falling into once.
CoreC7 · Duality

Write the Kalman filter's recursion and the LQR's next to each other.

Answer. The same equations with the roles transposed. Estimation and control are dual problems, which is why one implementation with a flag can serve both and why understanding either gives you the other.
CoreC8 · A receding-horizon loop

Implement MPC: optimise over a horizon, apply one step, re-solve.

Answer. Note where the feedback is: in the re-solving, not in the optimisation. Fix the plan and apply it open-loop and the model error is uncorrected, which is the experiment that makes the point.
CoreC9 · Horizon length

Shorten the MPC horizon until the controller cannot see a constraint coming.

Answer. It commits to a trajectory it cannot exit. The required horizon is set by how fast the system can stop, not by how far ahead you want to plan.
CoreC10 · Compose transforms

Implement rigid transforms and compose a chain of them, then invert one.

Answer. The inverse is not the transpose of the whole matrix: rotation transposes, translation becomes −RTt. Getting that wrong produces poses that are plausible and wrong, which is the most common bug in robotics code.
CoreC11 · Rotations, three ways

Convert between Euler angles, rotation matrices and quaternions, and find gimbal lock.

Answer. At a pitch of 90° two of the three Euler axes coincide and a degree of freedom disappears. Also check that q and −q give the same rotation: interpolating between them without handling that produces a full extra revolution.
CoreC12 · The Jacobian near a singularity

Build a two-link arm, compute its Jacobian, and command a motion at full extension.

Answer. The joint velocity explodes as the determinant goes to zero. Damped least squares bounds it at the cost of tracking error, which is the trade every production inverse-kinematics routine makes.
CoreC13 · Gravity compensation

Simulate a two-link arm with and without cancelling the gravity term.

Answer. Without it the controller spends most of its effort holding the arm up and the tracking gains have to be large. With it the arm is nearly weightless and small gains suffice, the highest-value single term in the dynamics.
CoreC14 · Dead reckoning drifts

Integrate a noisy velocity measurement and plot position error against time.

Answer. Error grows without bound: as t for a bias and √t for zero-mean noise. That difference is why a bias term must be estimated rather than assumed small.
CoreC15 · Fuse two sensors

Combine a drifting fast sensor with a noisy slow one in a filter.

Answer. Better than either, and the covariance tells you when it is not confident. Then drop one sensor mid-run and watch the covariance grow, which a point estimate cannot express.
CoreC16 · Count the cells

Compute the grid size for a planner at fixed resolution as degrees of freedom grow.

Answer. 12× per joint at the resolution in Figure 7. Arithmetic rather than measurement, and it settles the architecture before any code is written.
CoreC17 · Build an RRT

Implement one and measure samples to solution against dimension.

Answer. Nearly flat where the grid is exponential. Then look at the path: jerky and far from optimal, so the gain is feasibility rather than quality and a smoothing pass is a second algorithm.
CoreC18 · Rollout divergence

Reproduce Figure 6 on stable, marginal and unstable plants.

Answer. Measure absolute divergence. The relative version is scale-free (it depends only on the ratio of model error to system gain), so all three curves land on top of each other and the experiment cannot test what it is asking.
CoreC19 · The rule of three

Derive the 95% upper bound on a rate from zero events in n trials.

Answer. P(zero) = (1−p)n ≈ e−pn, set to 0.05, giving p < 3/n. Then compute the miles your fleet needs for the claim you want to make, and notice it is larger than the fleet.
CoreC20 · Bounding against estimating

Compute both requirements across target rates.

Answer. Bounding needs about 3/p; estimating to 20% needs about 96/p, thirty times more. Conflating the two is how a safety claim becomes an order of magnitude cheaper than it is.
CoreC21 · Correlated trials

Your fleet drives a million miles, all in one city over one month. How many independent trials is that?

Answer. Far fewer than the mileage suggests, because failures cluster by weather, location and time. The rule of three assumes independence, and a bound computed from correlated miles is optimistic by a factor nobody can state precisely, which is why the operational design domain has to be part of the claim.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · Iterative LQR

Linearise about a trajectory, solve, roll out, repeat, on a pendulum swing-up.

Answer. It finds a locally optimal swing-up, and which local optimum depends on the initial trajectory, which is Part XXVIII's EM finding in a different subject.
StretchS2 · Sampling-based MPC

Perturb a nominal input sequence, roll out, take a weighted average.

Answer. No derivatives, embarrassingly parallel, and competitive. The weighting temperature is Part XXX §27's parameter doing the same job: deciding how many samples actually contribute.
StretchS3 · Clone, then correct

Train a policy on expert states, roll it out, label the states it reached, retrain.

Answer. The drift shrinks with each round, and the improvement comes entirely from the new states rather than the new data volume, which you can check by adding the same amount of extra expert data instead.
StretchS4 · Offline extrapolation

Fit a value function to a fixed log and query it off the log's support.

Answer. It over-estimates, confidently, and the policy moves toward exactly those actions. Then add a pessimism term and watch the failure disappear along with some of the performance.
StretchS5 · A shield

Build a verified fallback controller and a region it can recover from, and let a learned policy act only inside it.

Answer. The only construction in this part that yields a statement for a safety case. Establishing the region is the work, and it cannot itself be learned.
StretchS6 · Self-play, and its blind spot

Train two agents against each other and then against a frozen earlier version.

Answer. The current agent can lose to an ancestor. Beating your own lineage is not the same as being good, which is why populations and past-version pools exist.
StretchS7 · Decompose a safety case

Bound perception, planning and control separately, then compose.

Answer. The composition needs independence, which does not hold: fog degrades perception and prediction together. Stating that dependence explicitly is where the argument does its work, and it requires the intermediate representations to exist.
StretchS8 · Audit a system that acts

Take something you deploy that takes actions (a robot, a controller, a tool-using agent) and check its loop latency, its recovery behaviour and its stopping condition.

Answer. The most common findings are a loop whose latency nobody has measured and no defined behaviour for "this is not working". Both are control questions and neither shows up in an accuracy metric.
