Part ATwo different questions

A model that predicts perfectly can still be useless for deciding what to do, and the reason is not statistical noise or insufficient data. It is that prediction and intervention are different quantities, computed from different distributions. This movement establishes the difference and measures how large it gets.

§1 The question a predictor cannot answer

Two sentences that sound the same and are not

"Patients given this drug recover more often." A statement about the world as it is, about the joint distribution you sampled. A predictor estimates it, and Part VI's whole machinery applies.

"Giving this drug makes patients recover more often." A statement about a world you have not sampled: the one where the drug is assigned rather than chosen. No amount of data from the first world determines the second, and no amount of predictive accuracy closes the gap.

Every part of this book before this one has been about the first sentence. Part VI §3 stated the learning problem as estimating $p(y \mid \mathbf{x})$; Parts VII to XX built increasingly effective ways to do it; Part XIV measured how well it was done. That is a complete and useful programme, and it answers the wrong question whenever the point of the model is to decide something.

Why this is not a corner case

Almost every deployed model is used to make a decision. A model that predicts which customers will churn is used to decide who to call. A model that predicts which patients will deteriorate is used to decide who to treat. A model that predicts loan default is used to decide who gets a loan.

In each case the model was fitted on data generated under one policy and is being used to change the policy. The quantity that governs the outcome is the causal effect of the action, and the quantity the model estimates is an association under the old regime. When those differ, the model is confidently answering a question nobody asked.

Part XVIII saw the same problem from the other side and called it what it is: you caused your own data. Reinforcement learning solves it by acting, which is expensive. This part is about what you can recover without acting, and what it costs.

§2 Three rungs

A hierarchy, and it does not collapse

Association. $p(y \mid x)$: what I see when I observe $x$. Estimable from data alone. Everything in Parts VI to XX.

Intervention. $p(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x))$: what I see when I set $x$. Estimable from observational data only under assumptions, and §12 gives the exact ones.

Counterfactual. $p(y_{x'} \mid x, y)$: what would have happened to this unit had it been treated differently, given what actually happened to it. Requires more than the interventional distribution: a fully specified SCM, so that the unit's own noise terms can be inferred from what actually happened to it (abduction), the equation for $x$ replaced by the counterfactual value (action), and the outcome recomputed from the updated model (prediction). This part does not derive that machinery in full: §37's counterfactual fairness criterion is the one place rung three is put to use.

The hierarchy is strict: knowing every quantity on a rung does not determine the rung above. Two systems can agree on every observable association and disagree about every intervention.

The test to apply to any claim

Ask which rung it lives on, and which rung the evidence supports. A great deal of published work, in machine learning especially, estimates rung one and writes its conclusions in the language of rung two. The word to watch is any verb that implies action: improves, increases, causes, leads to, drives. If the study observed rather than assigned, those verbs are unlicensed.

§3 Confounding, measured

Confounding

A confounder is a common cause of the treatment and the outcome. It creates an association between them that is not an effect of one on the other, and no amount of data reduces it: Figure 1's naive estimate is computed on forty thousand points and is wrong by more than the effect itself.

This is the first thing to understand about causal inference: the error is not variance. It does not shrink with $n$, it is not visible in a confidence interval, and a model with excellent held-out accuracy has it in full.

Why the right panel is the one to remember

Both slopes in the right panel are computed correctly from the same data. The stratified slopes answer "among people alike in $U$, how does $Y$ vary with $X$", which is the causal question when $U$ is the only confounder. The pooled slope answers "across everyone, how does $Y$ vary with $X$", which mixes the effect with the confounding path.

When these two disagree in sign the pattern has a name, Simpson's paradox, and the name has done damage, because it suggests a paradox. There is none. The two numbers are answers to two different questions, and which one you want is not a statistical question at all. It is determined by what you intend to do next.

§4 The collider, and why more controls is not safer

The lesson people take from §3 is usually "adjust for everything you can measure." That is exactly wrong, and this section is the reason.

Collider

A collider is a common effect of two variables. Conditioning on it (by adjusting for it, stratifying on it, or simply by having sampled on it) makes its causes dependent.

The intuition is elimination. If a high value of $C$ requires $X$ or $Y$ to be large, then among units with high $C$, learning that $X$ was small tells you $Y$ must have been large. Nothing causal has happened. You conditioned on the outcome of a race and discovered that the runners' speeds are negatively related.

Pitfall: "control for everything available"

Adding a variable to a regression is not a neutral act with a small cost. If it is a confounder, adding it removes bias. If it is a collider, adding it creates bias. If it is a mediator, adding it removes part of the effect you were trying to measure (§5).

Which one it is cannot be determined from the data. Confounders, colliders and mediators produce the same correlational signatures; they differ in what causes what, and that is information you bring to the analysis rather than extract from it. This is the single most important practical consequence of the whole part, and it is why §8's graphs are a working tool rather than notation.

Where this has bitten, repeatedly

Selection into a study is conditioning on a collider whenever the selection depends on more than one cause. Hospital-based studies condition on being hospitalised; a study of two conditions among inpatients finds them negatively associated because either one alone was enough to get admitted. This is Berkson (1946), and it is still being rediscovered.

The machine-learning version: any dataset assembled by filtering on a quality score that depends on several attributes has colliders built into it by construction. Part XIII §17's filtering and §7's selection bias are this mechanism, and this part supplies the account of why they distort what they distort.

§5 Mediators, and the effect you did not mean to remove

Mediator

A mediator lies on the causal path from treatment to outcome: $X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$. Adjusting for it removes the part of the effect that travels through it, leaving the direct effect rather than the total effect.

That is sometimes exactly what you want and usually not. If a drug works by lowering blood pressure, adjusting for blood pressure will show the drug does nothing, which is true of its direct effect and false as a summary of whether the drug works.

Three variables, three opposite prescriptions

Confounder ($X \leftarrow U \rightarrow Y$): adjust. Not adjusting leaves bias.

Collider ($X \rightarrow C \leftarrow Y$): do not adjust. Adjusting creates bias.

Mediator ($X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$): depends on the question. Adjust for the direct effect, do not for the total effect.

Three structures, three different actions, and no way to tell them apart by looking at the correlations. The structure has to come from knowledge of the domain, and stating it explicitly is what §9's graphs are for.

§6 Selection, and the sample you actually have

Part XIII §7 named selection bias and described its effects. With colliders in hand it can be stated exactly: selection is conditioning, and it carries the same consequences.

Three ways a sample gets selected, and what each does

Selection on the treatment. Harmless for estimating the effect: you have fewer units in one arm, and precision suffers, but nothing is biased.

Selection on the outcome. Biases everything. You are conditioning on a descendant of the thing you are trying to explain.

Selection on a collider of the two. The dangerous one, because it looks like neither. Recruiting participants in a way that depends on both treatment and outcome induces an association with no causal content, exactly as in Figure 2.

Note that a model trained on such a sample will have excellent held-out accuracy on that sample. Part XIV's entire diagnostic apparatus is silent here, because nothing is overfitting and nothing is miscalibrated. The sample is a faithful record of a population that was assembled by conditioning.

§7 What movement A establishes

Three things

Prediction and intervention are different quantities, and no accuracy on the first bounds the error on the second. Figure 1's naive estimate is computed correctly on forty thousand points and reports the wrong sign.

The error does not shrink with data. Confounding is bias, not variance. It is invisible to every diagnostic in Part XIV, because nothing has gone wrong with the estimation: the estimand was wrong.

Adjusting for more variables is not safer. Figure 2 manufactures a correlation of −0.519 between two independent variables purely by conditioning. Whether to adjust for something depends on its causal role, which is not recoverable from the data, which is why the next movement builds the language for saying it out loud.

Sixty seconds

Data tells you what goes with what. Deciding requires knowing what changes what. The gap between them is not noise and does not close with more data: it is a matter of which distribution your estimate refers to. A common cause makes an association that is not an effect; a common effect makes one appear where none exists when you condition on it. Which variables to control for is a question about causal structure, and causal structure is something you assert and defend, not something you fit.
