Part DGeometry

A camera is a measuring instrument with known equations. This movement is the part of perception where the answer is constrained by algebra rather than by data, which means some errors cannot be trained away, and some accuracies cannot be exceeded however good the network is.

§25 The pinhole camera

Go and read

Hartley & Zisserman, Multiple View Geometry in Computer Vision. The reference for this movement, and one of the few textbooks in this list that has not been superseded. Chapters 6 and 9 are the ones to read; the rest is there when you need it. Nothing in it depends on what models are fashionable, which is the point.

The projection

A point $(X, Y, Z)$ in front of the camera lands at

$$u = f_x \frac{X}{Z} + c_x, \qquad v = f_y \frac{Y}{Z} + c_y$$

Everything in this movement follows from the $1/Z$. Depth is divided out, so it is not recoverable from one view; equal displacements at different depths produce unequal image motion; and the derivative of image position with respect to depth falls as $1/Z^2$, which is §29's error growth before any noise is mentioned.

§26 Intrinsics and extrinsics

Two matrices, and which one moves

Intrinsics $K$: focal lengths, principal point, skew. Properties of the camera and lens. Fixed unless the lens is changed or the image is resized, and resizing an image without rescaling $K$ is a bug that produces plausible, wrong geometry.

Extrinsics $[R \mid t]$: where the camera is and where it is pointing. Change every frame on a moving platform, and are what a calibration between sensors establishes.

Together: $\mathbf{x} \sim K[R \mid t]\mathbf{X}$. A large share of multi-sensor bugs are one of these being stale, in the wrong frame, or in the wrong units.

§27 Distortion, and calibration

Go and read

Zhang (1999), Flexible camera calibration by viewing a plane from unknown orientations. The checkerboard calibration everybody uses. Short, and reading it once removes the sense that calibration is a black box in somebody's library.

Pitfall: the centre of the image lies to you

Every distortion effect vanishes at the principal point and grows outward. So a system developed and debugged on centred objects looks calibrated, and fails at the edges of the frame, which is where the merging vehicle is.

Two practical consequences. Check reprojection error as a function of radius, never as a single average. And re-calibrate: mounts shift, temperature changes focal length slightly, and a calibration from the factory is a claim about a camera that no longer exists.

§28 Two views, and the epipolar constraint

The one-dimensional search

A point in one image constrains its match in another to a line, the epipolar line, because the point, its two projections and the two camera centres are coplanar. Algebraically $\mathbf{x}'^\top F \mathbf{x} = 0$.

This turns matching from a two-dimensional search into a one-dimensional one, which is a large reduction and the reason stereo is tractable. Rectification warps both images so the epipolar lines are horizontal rows, after which the search is along a scanline and the disparity is a single number per pixel.

§29 Triangulation, and its error

Depth from disparity

$$Z = \frac{f b}{d} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \left|\frac{\partial Z}{\partial d}\right| = \frac{fb}{d^2} = \frac{Z^2}{fb}$$

So a fixed disparity error becomes a depth error growing with the square of range, and shrinking with the product of focal length and baseline. Both halves of that are design levers and both are bounded by the vehicle.

What this decides about a sensor suite

Sub-pixel disparity estimation is worth a great deal, because the error is proportional to it. Halving the disparity error is exactly as valuable as doubling the baseline, and usually cheaper.

Beyond some range, stereo stops being a depth sensor and becomes a direction sensor. That range is computable from the figure before any data is collected, and it is the argument for a time-of-flight sensor, for motion parallax over a long baseline in time, or for accepting that distant objects are tracked rather than ranged.

§30 Structure from motion, and bundle adjustment

Solving for everything at once

Given many views of many points, recover the camera poses and the 3-D points together by minimising total reprojection error. This is a large sparse nonlinear least-squares problem, and the sparsity (each point appears in few views) is what makes it solvable at scale.

It is Part XXVIII's maximum likelihood with a Gaussian noise model, and the Jacobian structure is a graphical model's. What it needs from the front end is not accuracy so much as correct correspondences: a few wrong matches will drag the solution anywhere, which is why robust losses and consensus estimation belong here rather than being an afterthought.

§31 Bird's-eye view, and what the transform assumes

The representation a planner wants

A planner reasons in a top-down metric frame, not in image space. Getting there from cameras is the central architectural problem of a modern driving stack, and there are three approaches.

Homography. Warp the image plane to the ground plane directly. Exact if the world is flat and the extrinsics are right, and wrong in proportion to how untrue that is, so everything above the road surface is smeared.

Lift and splat. Predict a depth distribution per pixel, project the features into 3-D, and pool into top-down cells. Handles height, and inherits monocular depth's ambiguity from §20.

Query the images. Give each top-down cell a query that attends to the image features that project into it. No explicit depth, geometry used only to decide where to look, and the approach current multi-camera systems have converged on.

Left out on purpose: neural radiance fields and neural rendering (optimising a volumetric scene representation so that it reproduces a set of training views, then querying it from a new viewpoint) answer a different question from the rest of this movement. Everywhere else here the camera is fixed and the question is where something is; view synthesis fixes the scene and asks what a camera would see from somewhere it never stood. The machinery it needs (differentiable volume rendering, an implicit scene function trained by gradient descent) has more in common with Part XVI's generative models than with the projective geometry this movement develops, and is not covered here.

§32 What movement D establishes

Three things

Some errors are geometric and no model fixes them. Figure 6 measures 164 px of reprojection error from uncorrected distortion, which is two orders of magnitude beyond any detector's localisation accuracy.

Stereo depth error grows as the square of range, measured at an exponent of 2.000, so a sensor's usable range is computable before it is built.

Calibration is a live quantity, not a constant, and it must be checked against radius rather than averaged over the image.
