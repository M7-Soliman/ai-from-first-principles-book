Part EGeometry

Permutation is one symmetry group. Rotation is another, and a molecule has both: its atoms have no canonical order and its energy does not depend on which way it is pointing. This movement generalises movement A's argument to continuous groups and measures what a symmetry is worth against paying data for it.

§32 Groups, and what an architecture declares

Go and read

Bronstein et al. (2021), Geometric deep learning: grids, groups, graphs, geodesics and gauges. The general statement of the argument Part X made for translation and this part generalises. Long; read the first sixty pages for the blueprint and treat the rest as reference.

The general statement

A group is a set of transformations closed under composition and inverse. The ones that matter here: $S_n$, permutations of $n$ objects; $SO(3)$, rotations in space; $SE(3)$, rotations and translations; $E(3)$, those plus reflection.

An architecture declares a group and a type of behaviour under it. Part X declared translation and equivariance. Movement A declared permutation. A molecular network declares $SE(3)$ for the geometry and $S_n$ for the atoms simultaneously, and must respect both.

Reflection is a real decision, not a technicality

$E(3)$ includes reflection and $SE(3)$ does not, and the difference decides whether your model can tell a molecule from its mirror image. For chirality (where two mirror-image molecules have different biological effects), declaring the larger group makes the model provably unable to represent the distinction that matters.

Which is the cleanest example in this part of the general point: a symmetry is an assumption, and asserting one the problem does not have is as damaging as missing one it does.

§33 Building invariance from distances

The cheap and correct construction

Pairwise distances are unchanged by any rotation, translation or reflection. So a network that reads only distances is $E(3)$-invariant by construction, with no constraint on the network itself.

This is what Figure 5's invariant model does and it is the first thing to try: it costs nothing, it is exact, and it handles a large fraction of real problems. Its limitation is that distances discard orientation, so it cannot express anything chiral, and it cannot produce a vector output: a force has a direction, and a distance-only network has no way to point.

§34 What a symmetry is worth

Two readings, and the second is the useful one

The gap's shape says the symmetry is learnable, once there is enough data for the augmented model to have something to generalise from. Below a few hundred examples both models are weak and the gap between them is small for the wrong reason; above that, the augmented model starts to catch up, which is why augmentation is a reasonable default at scale and why large models trained on enormous datasets can dispense with architectural symmetry entirely.

What the gap does not do is close. At every size measured the invariant model is ahead, and it is ahead exactly: it cannot violate the symmetry on an input nobody tested, and the augmented model can. When the cost of an inconsistent answer is high, that difference matters more than the accuracy.

§35 Equivariant message passing

Go and read

Satorras, Hoogeboom & Welling (2021), E(n) equivariant graph neural networks. The scalar-and-vector construction below, written out in about a page of arithmetic. Worth reading before the higher-order features of §36, to see how much of the benefit is available without them.

Keeping a direction

To predict a vector (a force, a displacement), the network must be equivariant rather than invariant: rotate the input and the output rotates with it.

The construction that works is to keep two kinds of quantity at each node. Scalars, which are invariant, and vectors, which rotate. Update scalars from distances and other scalars; update vectors as scalar-weighted sums of relative position vectors:

$$\mathbf{v}_i \;\leftarrow\; \mathbf{v}_i + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} w\!\left(\lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j \rVert, h_i, h_j\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\right)$$

Every term on the right rotates the same way, so the sum does. The weights are an arbitrary learned function of invariant arguments, so nothing is given up in flexibility: the constraint costs the network only the requirement that it never mix a scalar into a vector slot.

§36 Higher-order features

Beyond scalars and vectors

Scalars and vectors are the first two of a family: the irreducible representations of the rotation group, indexed by a degree. Higher degrees carry finer angular structure, and combining them requires a specific product that respects the group.

The practical position is worth stating plainly. Higher-degree networks are more expressive and substantially more expensive and more difficult to implement. The scalar-and-vector construction of §35 is enough for a large fraction of molecular work, and the gains from going further are real, task-dependent, and smaller than the framework's sophistication suggests.

§37 Where geometry meets graphs

Three settings, three groups

Point clouds. A set with coordinates and no edges. Build a graph by distance cutoff or nearest neighbours, then everything above applies, and note the graph is now a modelling choice with a hyperparameter, not given data.

Molecules. A graph with coordinates. Bonds give edges and geometry gives distances, so both symmetries act at once.

Meshes. A graph with faces, where the geometry is intrinsic. The relevant invariance may be to bending rather than to rotation, which is a different group and a different construction.

§38 What movement E establishes

Three things

The permutation argument generalises to any group, and an architecture is the declaration of one. Getting the group wrong in either direction is a modelling error: too small wastes data, too large (declaring reflection symmetry on a chiral problem) makes the needed distinction unrepresentable.

Invariance from distances is free and limited. Exact, trivial to implement, and unable to express chirality or produce a direction.

The symmetry is learnable and building it in is still better. Figure 5 measures the gap rising from 0.004 to 0.073 as both models leave the weak-data regime, then narrowing to 0.039 by 3,200 examples, never closing, and the built-in version cannot be violated on an input nobody checked.
