Part BQuantization

Store the weights in fewer bits. The most effective single technique in this part, because it attacks the quantity §3 says is binding, and because it costs less than it has any right to.

§8 The idea

Integers with a scale

Represent a group of weights by integers and one scale factor:

$$w \approx s \cdot q, \qquad q = \mathrm{round}(w / s) \in [-2^{b-1}, 2^{b-1}-1]$$

with $s = \max|w| / (2^{b-1}-1)$ for the symmetric case. Asymmetric quantization adds a zero point so the range need not be centred, which matters for activations after a ReLU and rarely for weights.

Going from 16 bits to 4 divides the bytes moved by four, and §3 says that is the number that sets decode speed. The arithmetic saving is a bonus and often not realised at all, because the values are typically dequantized before the multiply.

§9 Granularity, and what it buys

One scale for how many weights

Per-tensor: one scale for the whole matrix. Cheapest, and every weight shares a range set by the largest.

Per-channel: one per output row. Almost free and substantially better.

Per-group: one per block of 64 or 128 along the input dimension. Best, and the extra scales are themselves storage: at group 128 in 4-bit, the scales add about 3% overhead, which is worth counting when comparing "4-bit" schemes.

§10 Outliers

Go and read

Dettmers et al. (2022), LLM.int8(): 8-bit matrix multiplication for transformers at scale. The outlier finding, and the reason 8-bit inference works at all. Read §3 for the structure (the large activations concentrate in a small number of fixed feature dimensions, consistently across inputs) because that structure is what every later method exploits.

Why a handful of channels dominates

Quantization error is set by the step size, which is set by the range, which is set by the largest value in the group. A few weights a hundred times larger than the rest widen the range for everyone, and every ordinary weight loses precision to accommodate them.

The figure measures it: six columns of 512 account for a sixfold difference in error. In real transformers the effect is stronger and structured: the large activations concentrate in a small number of fixed feature dimensions, consistently across inputs.

That structure is what the remedies exploit. Mixed precision keeps the outlier channels in 16 bits and quantizes the rest, which was the original LLM.int8 observation. Scaling divides outlier channels down and folds a compensating factor into the adjacent layer, which is SmoothQuant's approach; AWQ instead scales the weight channels that multiply large activations, using activation statistics to decide which weights are salient rather than moving the difficulty into the activations themselves (§11). Both work because the outliers are few and identifiable rather than scattered.

§11 Post-training quantization

Go and read

Frantar et al. (2023), GPTQ: accurate post-training quantization for generative pre-trained transformers. Column-by-column quantization that adjusts the remaining weights to compensate for the error just introduced. The transferable idea is the reframing: accept the rounding and fix its downstream effect, rather than trying to round better. Then Lin et al. (2024) on AWQ, which is simpler and competitive.

Quantize a trained model, with a little calibration

The cheapest route: take the weights, choose scales, done. A calibration set of a few hundred examples is used to pick activation ranges and, in the better methods, to choose which rounding minimises the error that actually propagates.

GPTQ quantizes column by column and, after each, adjusts the remaining unquantized weights to compensate for the error just introduced, using second-order information from the calibration set. AWQ observes that not all weights matter equally and protects the ones that multiply large activations.

Both are error-compensating rather than error-minimising: they accept the rounding and fix its downstream effect, which is why they beat naive rounding at the same bit width.

§12 Quantization-aware training

Train with the rounding in the loop

Simulate quantization in the forward pass and let the model adapt. The gradient does not exist; rounding has zero derivative almost everywhere, so the straight-through estimator is used: quantize forward, pass the gradient back as though the operation were the identity.

It is a lie about the derivative and it works, for a reason worth stating: the quantity being optimised is not the rounding but the weights, and the identity gradient points in approximately the right direction for them.

Better than post-training quantization at low bit widths, and it costs a training run, so it is used when 4 bits or below is required and post-training methods have failed.

§13 Activations, and why they are harder

Pitfall: weight-only quantization is not the same as quantized inference

Most deployed quantization is weight-only: weights in 4 bits, dequantized to 16 for the multiply. This gets the memory-bandwidth win of §3 and none of the arithmetic win.

Quantizing activations too would let the multiply run in integer arithmetic, which is several times faster on hardware that supports it. It is much harder, because activations are data-dependent: their range varies per input, so the scale must be computed on the fly or calibrated and hoped for, and the outliers of §10 are worse in activations than in weights.

So when a system reports "4-bit", ask which. The two have different speedups and very different accuracy risks.

§14 What it actually costs

Measure the loss, not the weight error

§9's figure measures the error in the weights, which is the wrong quantity: it is not what anyone deploys against. §15's figure measures the model's loss, which is.

The two disagree in an important way: weight error grows smoothly as bits fall, and loss stays flat and then falls off a cliff. Choosing a bit width by weight error will pick the wrong one.

§15 Where the cliff is

Why the shape matters more than the numbers

Compare this to §9's figure, which measured the error in the weights. That error grows smoothly as bits fall. The loss does not: it is flat, and then it collapses.

Two consequences. Choosing a bit width by weight error will pick the wrong one, because a smooth quantity cannot tell you where a cliff is. And there is usually a bit width that is nearly free, going below it is expensive and stopping above it wastes memory, so the useful thing to measure is the location of the edge rather than the error at any point.

Where the edge sits depends on the granularity more than on the method. That is worth knowing before reaching for a sophisticated post-training algorithm: per-group scales are cheap, and they buy about the same bit that GPTQ does.

§16 Quantizing the cache

Where the other half of the memory is

§4 showed the KV cache overtaking the weights past about 26,000 tokens. Quantizing the weights and not the cache therefore stops helping exactly in the long-context regime that motivated the work.

Cache quantization to 8 or 4 bits is standard, and it has a property the weights do not: the cache is written once and read many times, so quantization error accumulates over the generation rather than being applied once. Keys are more sensitive than values, because they enter a softmax; the common asymmetry is to keep keys at higher precision than values.
