Part CMore data, and pretending you have it

§9 The best regulariser is more data

Before any technique: Part VI §6's learning curves established that a high-variance model improves with data and a high-bias one does not. Where the gap is large, more data dominates every method in this part: it reduces variance without buying any bias at all, which is a trade none of the penalties can offer.

The difficulty is that data is the expensive thing. So the field asks a narrower question: can we manufacture examples we do not have? Sometimes, and the conditions under which it works are precise.

§10 Dataset augmentation

In 60 seconds

Transform your training examples in ways that should not change the label (rotate an image, crop it, alter its colour) and train on the results as if they were new data. It is regularization because it states a preference: functions invariant to those transformations. It works exactly to the extent that the invariance is true.

The mechanism is §1's definition made literal. You are not adding information; you are adding a constraint, and the constraint is that the model's output should not change under transformations you have declared irrelevant.

Why it is the strongest regulariser available, when it is available

Every other technique in this part expresses a generic preference: smaller weights, fewer weights, less dependence on any one unit. None of them knows anything about your problem.

Augmentation is different: it encodes a fact about the domain. A rotated 7 is a 7. A photograph with the brightness raised is the same photograph. That is knowledge, and a regulariser carrying real knowledge is worth more than one carrying a preference for tidiness.

Which is why augmentation is close to mandatory in vision and much rarer in tabular problems: images come with a large group of transformations everyone agrees are label-preserving, and a table of customer records does not.

Pitfall: an invariance that is not true

The whole method rests on the transformation preserving the label, and the standard counterexample is worth remembering because it is so easy to get wrong.

Horizontal flips are a staple of image augmentation, and they are correct for cats and disastrous for digits: a flipped 2 is not a 2, and a flipped b is a d. Rotation by 180° turns a 6 into a 9. Aggressive cropping can remove the object you are trying to classify while keeping its label.

Each of these teaches the model something false, and the symptom is not obvious: the model trains fine and is simply worse, having spent capacity learning an invariance the task does not have.

The check is not statistical. It is to look at the augmented examples and ask whether a human would still assign the original label.

§11 Encoding an invariance without generating data

Augmentation states an invariance by example: here are a thousand rotated digits, infer that rotation does not matter. That is effective and indirect; you are hoping the model draws the general conclusion from the instances.

A family of older methods states it directly instead, and they are worth knowing because of what they reveal rather than because you are likely to use them.

Tangent propagation

If the label is unchanged along some transformation, then the model's output should not change along it either, so its directional derivative in that direction should be zero.

Write $\mathbf{v}^{(i)}$ for the direction in input space corresponding to an infinitesimal rotation, translation or scaling. Then penalise

$$\Omega(f) = \sum_i \big(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x})^\top \mathbf{v}^{(i)}\big)^2$$

which is zero exactly when the output is flat along every transformation you named.

Compare the two approaches. Augmentation samples the transformation and lets the model work out the rule; tangent prop differentiates it and imposes the rule. The second is more direct and needs you to write down the tangent vectors, which requires knowing the transformation analytically, easy for rotation and translation but hopeless for "different lighting" or "a different person's handwriting".

That practical limit is why augmentation won. But the idea has an afterlife worth noticing.

Why this connects to the manifold hypothesis

Part VI §23 argued that real data concentrates near a low-dimensional manifold, and that representation learning is the search for coordinates on it. Tangent methods are that claim used as a regulariser.

The transformations you can apply to an image without changing its label (rotate, translate, scale, adjust lighting) trace out directions along the manifold. The tangent vectors $\mathbf{v}^{(i)}$ are literally a basis for its tangent space, which is where the name comes from.

So the penalty says: be flat along the manifold, and free to vary across it. That is a remarkably precise statement of what a good classifier should do, and it is the same instinct behind the manifold tangent classifier, which estimates the tangent directions from data with an autoencoder rather than requiring you to supply them.

Which is the honest reason these methods are in this book despite being little used: they are the clearest statement anyone has made of what augmentation is for, and they connect it to the argument in Part VI that motivates the whole enterprise.

A closely related method, tangent distance, uses the same idea for nearest neighbours: measure the distance between two examples' manifolds rather than between the points, so a digit and its rotated copy are correctly judged close. It predates deep learning by decades and was the strongest MNIST method of its era.

§12 Noise, and why it is a penalty

A particular kind of augmentation deserves its own treatment, because it turns out to be one of the penalties from Part B in disguise.

Add Gaussian noise to the inputs during training. It is a reasonable thing to try: the model should not care about small perturbations, and there is no obvious connection to weight decay. There is an exact one.

Worked: why they coincide

Take squared error on a linear model and perturb the input, $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ with $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}] = \mathbf{0}$ and covariance $\sigma^2 I$. The expected loss is

$\mathbb{E}\big[(\mathbf{w}^\top(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}) - y)^2\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} - y)^2\big] + 2\,\mathbb{E}\big[(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}-y)\,\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\big] + \mathbb{E}\big[(\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\epsilon})^2\big]$

The middle term vanishes, because $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ has mean zero and is independent of everything else in it. The last one is a quadratic form in the noise covariance, which for $\sigma^2 I$ collapses to $\sigma^2\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$. So:

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{loss with noise}] = \text{loss without noise} + \sigma^2\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$$

That is the per-example result. Summing over all $n$ training examples gives a total extra cost of $n\sigma^2\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$, which is an $L^2$ penalty with strength $\alpha = n\sigma^2$, the value the figure confirms.

Note what the derivation used: linearity of the model and zero-mean noise, and nothing else. For a nonlinear model the equivalence is approximate rather than exact, but the mechanism survives, which is why noise injection behaves like a penalty in networks too.

Noise elsewhere

The same idea is applied in three other places, and each expresses a different preference:

Noise on the weights
Perturb the parameters rather than the inputs. For squared error this works out to a penalty on the gradient of the output with respect to the weights, a preference for regions of parameter space where small weight changes do not change the function much. Flat minima, in other words, and that is the same preference Part III §14 noted small-batch noise appears to express.
Noise on the labels
Label smoothing: replace a hard target of 1 with $1-\epsilon$, and spread $\epsilon$ over the other classes. This exists because of Part II §25's loss curve: cross-entropy drives the correct logit toward infinity to reach a target of exactly 1, and it never gets there. A softened target is attainable, so the weights stop growing.
Noise on the activations
Which is dropout, and it is different enough to need §19.
Try it

You add input noise with $\sigma = 0.1$ and also weight decay with $\alpha = 0.5$, on 1,000 examples. Roughly what total penalty are you applying, and what should you do before tuning either?

Answer. The noise contributes an effective $\alpha_{\text{noise}} = n\sigma^2 = 1000 \times 0.01 = 10$, which swamps the explicit $\alpha = 0.5$ by a factor of twenty. The total is about $10.5$, and essentially all of it is coming from the noise you may not have thought of as regularization at all.

What to do first: recognise that you have one knob, not two. Tuning $\alpha$ over a range of 0.1 to 1 while $\sigma$ silently contributes 10 will look like weight decay having no effect, and the natural conclusion (that regularization does not help here) is wrong. Sweep the dominant term, or fold them into a single effective strength and sweep that.

The general lesson is worth more than the arithmetic: regularisers compose, and their strengths add. Several mild-looking ones can total something severe, which is one of the most common causes of unexplained underfitting.
