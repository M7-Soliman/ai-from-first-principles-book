BuildThe project

Ten to fourteen hours. You write the transform from the definition upward, then use it to audit a model you already have, and find, in an architecture this book taught you in Part X, a defect that has a textbook remedy nobody applied.

P.1 What you are building

spectra: the discrete transform, a radix-2 FFT, filters with their responses, a windowed short-time transform, a calibrated spectral estimator, and a shift-consistency audit that runs against any model you can call.

The rule: numpy only, and every spectral routine checked against a signal whose answer you know. A transform that is silently off by a factor of $n$ produces plausible plots forever.

P.2 Stage 1: the transform

  1. Build the DFT matrix and apply it. Verify on a tone landing exactly on a bin: one non-zero magnitude, everything else at rounding.
  2. Check Parseval. Then break the normalisation on purpose and confirm the check catches it.
  3. Implement radix-2 Cooley–Tukey and verify it against the matrix.
  4. Time both against length and fit exponents. Fit over the whole range and over the largest sizes only, and report both.

Deliverable: the two fits and the crossover. Step four is where you find out that a complexity claim and a wall clock are different measurements, and which one your reader will reproduce.

P.3 Stage 2: aliasing

  1. Sample a sinusoid above Nyquist, transform, and read the peak. Confirm it lands where §3 says.
  2. Sample two different tones that alias onto each other and show the sample vectors are equal. That is what "unrecoverable" means, stated as an equality.
  3. Build a low-pass filter and confirm its response is what you intended.
  4. Filter, then sample, and confirm the peak is where it should be.

Deliverable: the two spectra and the equality from step two. Most treatments assert aliasing; step two demonstrates it.

P.4 Stage 3: the audit

  1. Write the shift-consistency test: pool a model's output, shift the input by one pixel, measure the relative change.
  2. Run it on a random convolutional stack with one, two, three and four strided stages.
  3. Insert a blur before each stride and re-run.
  4. Run it on a trained classifier you already have, over shifts one through eight.

Deliverable: Figure 1 reproduced, plus the number for your own model. Then say whether that number is larger or smaller than the accuracy differences you have been reporting.

P.5 Stage 4: filters and phase

  1. Build low-pass, high-pass and band-pass kernels; verify each by its frequency response rather than by its name.
  2. Check the centred difference [−1, 0, 1] and find its two zeros.
  3. Swap magnitude and phase between two images and measure the correlations.
  4. Reconstruct an image from magnitude alone with random phase.

Deliverable: the response plots and the correlation table. Step two is a label the response contradicts; step four is why a magnitude-only loss is a bad idea.

P.6 Stage 5: windows and the spectrogram

  1. Transform a tone that falls between bins, with and without a window, and measure the leakage.
  2. Implement the short-time transform.
  3. Build a signal with a steady tone and a transient. Sweep the window length and measure both resolutions.
  4. Report the product across window lengths.

Deliverable: the resolution table. If the product is not constant, your two measurements are not measuring what you think.

P.7 Stage 6: spectra, calibrated

  1. Write down your predictions first, in a file, before running anything.
  2. Implement the radially averaged power spectrum.
  3. Calibrate it on synthetic images built to have a chosen exponent. Do not proceed until it recovers what you set.
  4. Measure the exponent on real images from at least three sources, at native resolution, and report the spread across tiles.
  5. Downsample fourfold with and without filtering and compare spectra.
  6. Score your predictions.

Deliverable: the calibration, the measured exponent with its spread, and the scored predictions. Step three is the difference between a number about the world and a number about your code, and step four is where you find out that "natural images" is a claim about a sample.

P.8 Stage 7: spectral bias

  1. Fit a small network to a sum of sinusoids and track each component's recovered amplitude.
  2. Reverse the amplitudes so the highest frequency is loudest, and re-run.
  3. Replace the input with a bank of Fourier features and re-run again.
  4. Raise the bank's bandwidth until the network fits noise on the first epoch.

Deliverable: the three convergence plots and the bandwidth at which step four happens. That last number is the cutoff you took away from the optimiser and are now responsible for.

P.9 Stretch

  • Anti-alias a real classifier throughout, retrain, and measure accuracy alongside consistency.
  • A long-convolution sequence layer computed through the FFT.
  • Rotary embeddings, then three context-extension methods compared beyond the training length.
  • A learned audio front end against a mel spectrogram, at matched compute.
  • The graph Laplacian's eigenbasis, with Part XXVI's over-smoothing expressed in it.
  • An audit of every resampling step in a pipeline you run.

P.10 Done when

  • Your transform puts a bin-aligned tone in exactly one bin.
  • Parseval holds, and you have seen your own check catch a broken normalisation.
  • Your FFT agrees with the definition and you have both timing fits.
  • You have two tones whose sample vectors are equal, and can say why that ends the argument.
  • You have Figure 1's curve, and the same number for a model you actually use.
  • You know which of your kernels are band-pass despite being called something else.
  • You have measured what phase carries and what magnitude does not.
  • Your time–frequency product is constant across window lengths.
  • Your spectral estimator recovers an exponent you set before you quote one you did not.
  • You have a natural-image exponent with a spread beside it, from more than one source.
  • You wrote predictions down before Stage 6 and scored them afterwards.
  • You know the Fourier-feature bandwidth at which your network starts fitting noise.
  • Your README names one resampling step in your pipeline that does not filter first.
Why this project

Stage 3 is the one that pays for the rest. Every reader of this book has trained a network with strided downsampling, and almost none has measured what it does to shift-consistency. The measurement takes twenty minutes once the transform exists, the number is usually larger than the effects being reported around it, and the remedy is sixty years old.

Stage 6 step three is the second. Fitting a power law is easy and quoting one is easy; knowing that your estimator recovers an exponent you set is what separates a measurement from a plausible number, and it is why the failure recorded against Figure 6 is honest rather than embarrassing.

The framework, and the basis

Dataset, model family, loss, optimiser. Part XXV said the family is a prior about the shape of the function; Part XXVI said that prior can be a symmetry group. This part says what the choice looks like from underneath: a symmetry picks a basis, and the basis decides what is easy.

Translation picks sinusoids, which is why convolution is multiplication and why a filter has a one-line description. Permutation on a graph picks the Laplacian's eigenvectors, which is the same construction with a different notion of neighbour. And the choice reaches further than the architecture: gradient descent's order of learning is a statement in that basis (low frequencies first, measured at 50 steps against never) so the basis decides not only what the model can express cheaply, but what it will find first.
