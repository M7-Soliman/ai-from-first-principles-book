Part CBeyond counting

VC dimension counts labellings and ignores everything else: the distribution, the scale of the weights, the margin, the algorithm. Each of those can be brought in, and each buys a tighter bound at the cost of some of the generality. This movement is the four standard ways, and they are the ones movement D finds still insufficient.

§16 Rademacher complexity

How well the class fits noise

Draw signs $\sigma_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ uniformly at random. The empirical Rademacher complexity of $\mathcal{F}$ on a sample is

$$\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{F}) = \mathbb{E}_\sigma \left[ \sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i f(x_i) \right]$$

It measures how well the class can correlate with pure noise on the points you have. A class that can fit any random labelling has complexity near its maximum; a restricted class cannot chase the noise and has small complexity.

The bound that follows is $R(f) \leq \hat{R}_S(f) + 2\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{F}) + 3\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)/2n}$, and it is proved with McDiarmid rather than Hoeffding, because the supremum is not an average.

Two advantages over VC dimension

It is data-dependent. The quantity is computed on the sample you actually have, so a class that is enormous in principle but constrained on your data gets a small number. VC dimension cannot express that, because it quantifies over all point sets.

It is computable. Estimate it by sampling signs and fitting, which is exactly the randomisation test of Part XIV, and the same procedure as the randomised-label experiments of movement D. The bound and the diagnostic are the same measurement.

§17 Covering numbers

Counting at a resolution

The covering number $N(\mathcal{F}, \varepsilon)$ is the fewest functions needed so that every $f \in \mathcal{F}$ is within $\varepsilon$ of one of them.

This recovers §4's finite-class argument for infinite classes: apply the union bound over the cover, then pay $\varepsilon$ for the approximation. Chaining, summing the argument over a sequence of resolutions, gives the sharpest of the classical bounds and connects covering numbers to Rademacher complexity directly.

The reason to know this exists: it is the route by which the scale of the weights enters. A class of networks with bounded weight norms has a covering number that depends on those norms, which is how the norm-based bounds of §29 are built.

§18 Margins

Confident correctness is worth more than bare correctness

Two classifiers with identical training error are not equally trustworthy if one scrapes past every point and the other clears them all comfortably. The margin makes that precise, and the margin bound replaces the training error with the fraction of points classified correctly by at least $\gamma$, plus a term that shrinks as $\gamma$ grows.

The consequence is a bound that does not depend on the dimension at all: only on the margin and the data's scale. This is why a support vector machine with a kernel mapping into an infinite-dimensional space is not vacuous: the VC dimension is infinite and the margin bound does not care.

Part VI §14's maximum-margin objective was presented as a geometric choice. This is the theorem that makes it a statistical one.

§19 Algorithmic stability

Bounding the algorithm instead of the class

An algorithm is uniformly stable with parameter $\beta$ if replacing one training point changes its loss on any input by at most $\beta$. A stable algorithm generalises, with expected gap at most $\beta$, and no hypothesis class appears anywhere in the statement.

This is a genuinely different move. Uniform convergence bounds every hypothesis the class contains; stability bounds the one the algorithm returns. Since the algorithm may never visit most of the class, the second can be far smaller.

Known results: strongly convex empirical risk minimisation is stable with $\beta = O(1/n)$, and stochastic gradient descent is stable for a limited number of steps. Both are the shape movement D needs, properties of the procedure, and neither yet reaches the regime where deep networks are trained.

§20 Structural risk minimisation

Choosing the class as part of the problem

Order a nested family $\mathcal{H}_1 \subset \mathcal{H}_2 \subset \cdots$ by complexity, and minimise empirical risk plus the complexity penalty for the class you chose:

$$\hat{h} = \arg\min_{k,\; h \in \mathcal{H}_k} \; \hat{R}_S(h) + \mathrm{pen}(k, n)$$

The resulting guarantee is competitive with the best class in the family, chosen with hindsight. It is the theoretical statement of what Part VIII called regularization and Part XIV called model selection, and the reason both work.

Where the penalty comes from matters

Derived from a bound, the penalty is a guarantee. Chosen by cross-validation, it is a hyperparameter, better in practice, and it carries Part XIV §20's selection bias, because you picked the winner of a search.

Almost all real work does the second and reports it as though it were the first.

§21 What all four have in common

The same trade, four times

VC dimension: assumes nothing, applies to every distribution and algorithm, gives the loosest bound.

Rademacher: assumes the distribution you sampled from, tightens accordingly.

Margin and covering: assume something about scale and separation.

Stability: assumes a property of the algorithm and drops the class entirely.

Each step down the list buys tightness with an assumption. That is the only currency available, and it is why §13's lower bound is not a wall you can climb by being cleverer: a distribution-free, algorithm-free bound has a floor, and beating it requires giving up one of those freedoms.

§22 What movement C establishes

Three things

Tightness is bought with assumptions, and the four standard tools are four different purchases: the distribution, the scale, the margin, the algorithm.

Rademacher complexity is measurable, and the measurement is the randomised-label experiment: the bound and the diagnostic are the same procedure run for different purposes.

Stability is the shape that survives into movement D, because it bounds the hypothesis the algorithm returns rather than every hypothesis the class contains, and the gap between those two is exactly where deep learning lives.
