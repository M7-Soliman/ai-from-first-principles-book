Part CTurning inference into optimisation

When the posterior cannot be computed, pick a family of distributions you can compute with and find the member closest to it. That converts inference into optimisation, which this book already knows how to do, and it commits you to a specific, measurable kind of wrongness.

§17 The idea

Approximate by choosing

Choose a family $\mathcal{Q}$ and solve

$$q^* \;=\; \arg\min_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \; \mathrm{KL}\!\left(q \,\|\, p(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x})\right)$$

The trade is explicit and it is the only trade in the movement: a richer family is closer to the posterior and more expensive to optimise. And unlike the sampling methods of movement D, the answer is biased and does not become unbiased with more compute: running longer converges to the best member of the family, which is not the posterior.

§18 The evidence lower bound

Go and read

Blei, Kucukelbir & McAuliffe (2017), Variational inference: a review for statisticians. The best single entry point to this movement. §2.2 states the mean-field underestimate that Figure 4 measures, and is unusually direct about it being a property of the objective rather than a failure of the optimiser.

Why the objective is computable when the KL is not

The KL above contains $\log p(\mathbf{x})$, which is the quantity you could not compute in the first place. But it is a constant in $q$, and rearranging gives

$$\log p(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})\right] + \mathbb{H}[q]}_{\text{ELBO}(q)} \;+\; \mathrm{KL}\!\left(q \,\|\, p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\right)$$

Both terms on the right are what you want: the KL is non-negative, so the ELBO is a lower bound on the evidence, and maximising it is exactly minimising the KL. The gap between them is the KL, so the bound's looseness is the approximation error, which sounds useful and is not, because you cannot measure the gap without the thing you were approximating.

The same inequality, three times in this book

EM's guarantee (§15) is this bound with an exact E step. Part XVI §15's derivation of the VAE is this bound with a neural encoder. And the importance-weighted variants of §26 are this bound with a tighter estimator. One inequality, and recognising it is most of what makes the literature navigable.

§19 Mean field

The simplest family, and the standard one

Assume the approximate posterior factorises completely: $q(\mathbf{z}) = \prod_i q_i(z_i)$. Then coordinate ascent has a closed form (each factor's optimum is the exponentiated expected log-joint under the others), and the updates cycle until the ELBO stops moving.

It is cheap, it is what "variational inference" means without qualification in most papers, and the assumption it makes is precisely the one the posterior usually violates: that the latent variables are independent given the data.

§20 What it gets wrong

Not a bug, and not fixable by optimising harder

The mean is right. The variance is too small, by a factor that has a closed form and is severe long before the correlation looks extreme: at $\rho = 0.9$ the reported uncertainty is 44% of the truth.

It is a property of the objective, not of the optimiser. Figure 4's gradient ascent finds the same answer as the closed form, so there is no better solution being missed. A variational posterior is systematically overconfident, and any decision made from its credible intervals inherits that, which is why Part XXIV's calibration check belongs on the output of one.

§21 The direction of the KL

Two divergences, two failure modes

Reverse KL, $\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)$, is what the ELBO minimises. It is infinite wherever $q$ has mass and $p$ does not, so $q$ avoids $p$'s low-probability regions: it is mode-seeking and under-disperses.

Forward KL, $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$, is infinite wherever $p$ has mass and $q$ does not, so $q$ must cover everything: it is mass-covering and over-disperses across modes. It is also usually not computable, because it needs expectations under $p$.

One correction, because the folk version of this is too strong. For a Gaussian target with a factorised Gaussian family the forward KL recovers the true marginals exactly: it does not over-disperse. Over-dispersion needs a target the family cannot represent, a multimodal one being the standard case. The asymmetry is real; where it shows up is narrower than usually stated.

§22 Amortisation, and the VAE recalled

Fitting one network instead of one posterior each

Classical variational inference optimises free parameters per data point. Amortised inference trains a network mapping each observation to its approximate posterior's parameters, so a new point costs a forward pass instead of an optimisation.

That is Part XVI §18's encoder, and this movement is what it was doing. The reparameterisation trick of Part XVI §16 exists because the ELBO's gradient with respect to the encoder needs to pass through a sampling step.

The cost has a name: the amortisation gap, the difference between the network's output and the best member of the family for that point. It adds to §20's gap rather than replacing it, so a VAE's posterior is overconfident twice over.

§23 Beyond mean field

Four ways to buy back the dependence, and what each costs

Structured approximations keep some of the posterior's graph (a chain, say) and factorise the rest. Cheap, and the gain is exactly the dependence you kept.

Full-covariance Gaussians fix Figure 4's problem entirely for a Gaussian posterior, at quadratic parameters in the latent dimension.

Normalising flows transform a simple distribution through invertible maps, giving an expressive family with a tractable density. Part XVI §21.

Tighter bounds from importance weighting: averaging several weighted samples inside the logarithm gives a bound that approaches the true evidence as the sample count grows, at proportional cost.

Two more standard approximations sit outside that list because they are not enrichments of the mean-field family: they are different procedures for building an approximate posterior, and each buys accuracy in a different currency: one trades the coordinate-ascent loop for a single optimisation, the other trades the ELBO's one global objective for many small local ones.

Laplace and expectation propagation

The Laplace approximation fits a Gaussian at the mode instead of anywhere else. Find $z^\star = \arg\max_z \log p(z \mid \mathbf{x})$ by gradient ascent (Part 0 §24's algorithm, run uphill), then match the curvature there:

$$q(z) \;=\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(z^\star,\, H^{-1}\right), \qquad H \;=\; -\nabla^2_z \log p(z \mid \mathbf{x})\big|_{z^\star}$$

which is the second-order Taylor expansion of the log-posterior about its peak, exponentiated. One mode-finding optimisation and one Hessian replace mean field's coordinate-ascent loop entirely, cheaper than CAVI, and exact whenever the true posterior already is Gaussian. It is also blind by construction to anything curvature at a single point cannot see: a second mode, or a skew the quadratic cannot represent. Part XXIV §18 runs the identical construction on a network's weight posterior instead of a latent one (same Hessian, same blindness), which is why Laplace is usually the first uncertainty a fitted model gets, before any heavier machinery from this movement is built.

Expectation propagation keeps full-covariance mean field's global-Gaussian ambition but changes how the fit is found. Rather than one KL against the whole posterior, it works factor by factor: hold the current approximation fixed everywhere except one factor, substitute the true factor back in, and moment-match: set the approximation's mean and covariance to match this "tilted" distribution exactly, which is a local, computable instance of the forward KL's mass-covering behaviour from §21, not the ELBO's reverse KL. Cycle over factors until the messages stop moving. There is no global objective EP is guaranteed to decrease, so unlike CAVI it can oscillate rather than converge, but where it does converge it typically recovers the marginal variance mean field under-reports, because no single factor is ever asked to cover the whole posterior's mass at once. It is §12's forward–backward generalised: once §11's treewidth rules out exact message passing, EP passes moment-matched messages instead of exact ones.

§24 What movement C establishes

Three things

Inference becomes optimisation by choosing a family, and one inequality (the ELBO) underlies EM, the VAE and every variational method in the literature.

The error does not go away with compute. More iterations converge to the best member of a family that does not contain the posterior.

The wrongness has a direction and it is overconfidence. Figure 4 measures a standard deviation of 0.436 against a true 1.000, exactly $\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$, and the optimiser is not the problem.
