BuildThe project

Ten to fourteen hours. You write a message-passing library from the scatter-add upward, then use it to reproduce this part's two structural limits and the one exact impossibility theorem in the book, and to find out whether the graph you were handed was carrying anything.

P.1 What you are building

looplib: a message-passing implementation on edge lists, with the three aggregators, the standard normalisations, a colour-refinement checker, and a validation harness that knows the difference between a transductive and an inductive split.

The rule: numpy only, and every model checked for invariance before it is trained. The permutation test is three lines and it catches a class of bug that trains cleanly and is silently wrong.

P.2 Stage 1: the loop

  1. Implement gather-and-scatter aggregation on an edge list, with sum, mean and max.
  2. Build one layer: transform self, add transformed aggregate, squash.
  3. Write the permutation test and run it on your layer.
  4. Deliberately break invariance (index a node by position somewhere), and confirm the test catches it.

Deliverable: the layer, the test, and the broken version that fails it. Step four is the one that makes step three a check rather than a ritual.

P.3 Stage 2: what the aggregator decides

  1. Build two tasks: one whose label depends on a node's degree, one whose does not.
  2. Fit all three aggregators on both, several seeds each.
  3. Report paired differences with spreads.

Deliverable: a six-cell table with error bars. The claim that sum is injective on multisets and the others are not is a theorem; what it is worth on your task is a measurement.

P.4 Stage 3: the collapse

  1. Sweep depth from one to twenty-four, measuring accuracy and the mean pairwise distance between representations.
  2. Reproduce Figure 2's shape, then repeat with the symmetric normalisation and state precisely what differs.
  3. Add residual connections, then initial-feature re-injection, and re-measure both curves.
  4. Report the paired accuracy difference between the peak depth and the deepest, with its standard error.

Deliverable: the two curves for each variant, and the paired comparison. Say for each repair whether it delayed the collapse or removed it.

P.5 Stage 4: the ceiling

  1. Implement colour refinement and use it to test a pair of graphs for distinguishability.
  2. Construct the two 2-regular graphs and confirm your trained model gives them identical outputs, to zero, not to a tolerance.
  3. Add a structural feature the loop cannot compute and confirm they separate.
  4. Add random node identifiers instead, and measure both the accuracy gain and the variance across draws of the identifiers on a fixed input.

Deliverable: the zero, the separation, and the variance in step four. That last number is the price of the expressivity, and it is usually left unquoted.

P.6 Stage 5: distance

  1. Measure influence share along a path and across a joined pair of trees, reproducing Figure 4.
  2. Build a task requiring a six-hop dependency and measure accuracy against depth.
  3. Add a virtual node, then full attention with a structural encoding, and re-measure, including memory.

Deliverable: the accuracy-against-depth curve with its peak, and the two rewiring results with their costs. Then answer in a paragraph whether the rewired graph is still the object the question was about.

P.7 Stage 6: the honest evaluation

  1. Write down your predictions first, in a file, before running anything.
  2. Take a node-classification dataset. Measure homophily. Fit a features-only model, label propagation, and a two-layer network.
  3. Evaluate under a random node split and under a genuinely inductive one, and report both.
  4. Repeat everything over ten seeds and report the spread beside every number.
  5. For link prediction, score against uniform and against distance-two negatives.
  6. Score your predictions against the outcomes.

Deliverable: the full table with spreads, and the scored predictions. If the features-only baseline matches the graph model, say so plainly: that is the most useful result this stage can produce and the one least often reported.

P.8 Stage 7: geometry

  1. Build a rotation-invariant target on point clouds. Fit a distance-only model and a coordinate model with rotation augmentation, across training sizes.
  2. Reproduce Figure 5's shape and report where the gap sits at each size.
  3. Implement the scalar-and-vector update and verify equivariance by rotating an input.
  4. Build a chiral target and confirm the distance-only model cannot represent it.

Deliverable: the gap-against-size curve and the two symmetry checks. Step four is the cleanest demonstration in this part that a symmetry is an assumption and can be asserted wrongly.

P.9 Stretch

  • Neighbour sampling on a graph too large for memory, with the variance it introduces measured against depth.
  • A heterophilous benchmark, with and without separating self from neighbours.
  • Higher-order message passing on pairs, with its cost curve.
  • A knowledge-graph scorer, evaluated filtered and unfiltered.
  • Temporal graphs, split by time, against Part XXV §36's result.
  • An audit of a graph system you actually run.

P.10 Done when

  • Your layer passes the permutation test and your deliberately broken one fails it.
  • You have a paired measurement of what the aggregator choice is worth on two tasks.
  • You have an accuracy-against-depth curve with a peak, and the distance curve beside it.
  • You can say for each over-smoothing repair whether it delayed the collapse or removed it.
  • Your two regular graphs receive identical outputs to exactly zero.
  • You have quoted the variance random identifiers introduce, not only the accuracy they buy.
  • You have measured influence share falling with distance on two topologies.
  • You have a homophily number for your dataset.
  • You have a features-only baseline and a label-propagation baseline, tuned as hard as the model.
  • You have both a transductive and an inductive number for the same dataset.
  • You have link-prediction scores against two different negative samplers.
  • Every number you report has a spread beside it.
  • You wrote predictions down before Stage 6 and scored them afterwards.
  • Your README says whether the edges were carrying anything.
Why this project

Graph learning is the area of this book where published comparisons are least reliable, and the causes are all things this project makes you measure: absent baselines, leaked splits, benchmark noise larger than the effect, and a metric that moves with how the negatives were drawn.

Stage 4 is here for a different reason. It is the one place in the book where you can watch a proved impossibility happen in a running program: not a tendency, not a bound that turns out vacuous, but a difference that is exactly zero because the two computations are the same computation.

The framework, and the symmetry

Dataset, model family, loss, optimiser. Part XXV said the family is a prior about the shape of the function; this part says what kind of prior that can be. An architecture is the declaration of a group: the transformations under which the answer is required not to change.

Convolution declares translation. Message passing declares permutation. A molecular network declares permutation and rigid motion together. The declaration is worth samples, because a constraint is information that does not have to be learned, and Figure 5 measures the exchange rate: the gap widens from 0.004 to 0.073 as both models leave the weak-data regime, then narrows to 0.039 by 3,200 examples, and does not close. Which leaves the choice where it belongs: not on which architecture is fashionable, but on which transformations genuinely leave your answer alone.
