Part CSummarising

A full distribution is a lot of information. Often you want a couple of numbers that capture the gist: where it sits and how far it spreads. That is what this part is for.

§10 Expectation

Go and read

Metropolis & Ulam (1949), The Monte Carlo method. Estimating an expectation by sampling from it, stated by the people who named the technique, and who needed it because the integrals arising from neutron diffusion had no closed form. Every minibatch you will ever compute is an instance of this paper.

Definition: expectation

The expected value of a function $f$ under a distribution is the average of $f$, weighted by how likely each outcome is:

$$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[f(x)] = \sum_x P(x) f(x) \qquad \text{or} \qquad \int p(x) f(x)\,dx$$

The subscript $x \sim P$ reads "$x$ drawn from $P$", and it names which distribution the averaging happens under. When it is obvious, people drop it and write $\mathbb{E}[X]$.

Taking $f(x) = x$ gives the mean, the ordinary notion of average, but weighted by probability rather than treating every outcome as equally likely.

Intuition: expectation is a weighted centre of mass

This is not a metaphor; it is literally the physics formula for centre of mass with probability playing the role of mass. That is why an expectation can fall where no outcome lives, in the same way a doughnut's centre of mass sits in the hole. "The average family has 1.9 children" is not a claim about any family.

Linearity, the property that does all the work

$$\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]$$

This holds always, for any random variables, dependent or not, related or not. It is the most useful identity in the chapter, and it makes many otherwise difficult calculations collapse into arithmetic.

The "dependent or not" is the surprising part and worth dwelling on, because almost nothing else in probability is so forgiving. $X$ and $Y$ may be entangled in any way you like ($Y$ could be $X$ itself, or $-X$, or some baroque function of it) and the identity does not care. Expectations add. Always.

The reason is that expectation never looks at the two variables separately. It is a single weighted sum over the joint outcomes, and in each outcome $X$ and $Y$ take definite values whose sum is just a number. You are adding up $(x + y)$ across outcomes, and any sum can be split into $\sum x$ plus $\sum y$ by regrouping the terms, which is rearrangement, not an assumption. Dependence would matter if we needed to know how $X$ and $Y$ vary together, and for a plain sum we never do.

Why deep learning cares: this is why minibatches work

Linearity is what licenses nearly every "average over the batch" step you will ever write. A loss over $m$ examples is a sum, so its expectation is the sum of the individual expectations, so the average gradient over a random minibatch is an unbiased estimate of the gradient over the whole dataset. That single fact is what makes stochastic gradient descent legitimate rather than a hopeful approximation, and Part III §14 leans on it entirely.

Note what is not being claimed. The minibatch gradient is correct on average; it is not close to the true gradient on any particular step, and how far it strays is a variance question rather than a mean one. Linearity gives you the first for free and says nothing at all about the second.

Pitfall: linearity does not extend to products or functions

$\mathbb{E}[XY] \neq \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y]$ in general; equality requires independence, and §12 measures exactly how far apart the two sides are.

More broadly, $\mathbb{E}[f(X)] \neq f(\mathbb{E}[X])$ for nonlinear $f$. The average of the squares is not the square of the average. This gap is not a technicality: it is precisely what variance measures in §11, and forgetting it produces a large fraction of all wrong probability arguments.

§11 Variance and standard deviation

Definition: variance

$$\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\big]$$ the average squared distance from the mean. Its square root, $\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}$, is the standard deviation.

Why squared? Two reasons, and both matter. Without squaring, the deviations above and below the mean cancel exactly, giving zero every time: useless. Squaring also punishes large deviations disproportionately, which is usually what you want when you care about outliers. Absolute value would fix the cancellation too, but as Part I §14 noted, it is not differentiable at zero and is much less pleasant to optimise.

A computational identity worth knowing, since it lets you compute variance in a single pass over data:

$$\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2$$

"The mean of the square minus the square of the mean", which is exactly the gap the pitfall above warned about, now given a name.

Pitfall: that identity is numerically dangerous

When the mean is large and the variance small, $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ and $(\mathbb{E}[X])^2$ are two big, nearly equal numbers, and subtracting them destroys precision, a phenomenon called catastrophic cancellation. It can even return a small negative variance, which is mathematically impossible.

It is fine on paper and risky in code. Prefer np.var, which uses a numerically stable algorithm. This is the same lesson as Part I's §13: an identity being true does not make it safe to compute.

Standard deviation is usually the more interpretable of the two, because it carries the same units as $X$. If heights are in centimetres, the variance is in centimetres squared (which is not a quantity anyone has intuition about) while the standard deviation is in centimetres and can be compared directly against a height.

§12 Covariance, correlation, and a myth

Definition: covariance

$$\text{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])\big]$$

Read the sign, which is where the meaning is. When $X$ is above its mean, is $Y$ usually above its mean too? Then the product of deviations is usually positive, and so is the covariance. If $Y$ tends to be below its mean when $X$ is above, the products are negative and so is the covariance.

  • Positive: they tend to move together.
  • Negative: they tend to move oppositely.
  • Zero: no linear relationship. Note that word carefully.

The magnitude is hard to interpret because it depends on the units of both variables. Dividing it out gives the correlation, which always lands in $[-1, 1]$:

$$\rho(X,Y) = \frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$$
Pitfall, the myth: "zero covariance means independent"

This is false, and the fourth panel of Figure 6 is the counterexample. Independence is a much stronger claim than zero covariance.

The implication runs one way only:

independent $\Rightarrow$ zero covariance,   but   zero covariance $\not\Rightarrow$ independent

Covariance detects linear association. A relationship that is curved, cyclic, or otherwise symmetric can be perfectly deterministic and still register zero. The one important exception: for jointly Gaussian variables the two notions do coincide, which is a special property of the Gaussian and not a general fact.

§13 The covariance matrix

Symmetric and PSD is exactly the hypothesis of the spectral theorem (I §17). That is not a coincidence; it is why PCA exists.

With many variables at once, collect all the pairwise covariances into a matrix:

$$\Sigma_{i,j} = \text{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$$

The diagonal holds each variable's own variance, since $\text{Cov}(X_i, X_i) = \text{Var}(X_i)$. The off-diagonal entries hold the pairwise relationships.

Two structural facts follow immediately, and both connect back to Part I:

  • It is symmetric, because $\text{Cov}(X,Y) = \text{Cov}(Y,X)$: the definition is unchanged by swapping the two factors.
  • It is positive semidefinite. Intuitively: any weighted combination of the variables has a variance, and variances cannot be negative. That is precisely the condition $\mathbf{a}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{a} \geq 0$ from Part I §18.
Why deep learning cares: this is where Part I pays off

Symmetric and positive semidefinite is exactly the hypothesis of the spectral theorem. So a covariance matrix always has real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors, which is what made PCA work in Part I §23.

Read across the two chapters: the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ are the directions the data varies along, and the eigenvalues are how much variance sits in each. PCA is the eigendecomposition of this object. Part I gave you the machinery; Part II tells you what the matrix being decomposed actually means.

Try it

You have three features. What shape is the covariance matrix, how many distinct numbers does it contain, and what sits on its diagonal?

Answer. Shape $3 \times 3$, so nine entries, but symmetry means $\Sigma_{1,2} = \Sigma_{2,1}$ and so on, leaving only 6 distinct values: the 3 diagonal entries plus 3 independent off-diagonal ones. In general an $n \times n$ covariance matrix has $n(n+1)/2$ free parameters. The diagonal holds the three individual variances.

That parameter count is not trivia. A full covariance matrix over 1,000 features needs about 500,000 numbers to estimate, which requires an enormous amount of data, which is exactly why models frequently assume a diagonal covariance and accept the loss of fidelity. You will meet that trade in §16.
