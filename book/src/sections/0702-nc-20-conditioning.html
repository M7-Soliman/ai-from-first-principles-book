§5 The condition number

In 60 seconds

The condition number is an amplification factor: how much a small error in the input becomes a large error in the output. It is a property of the problem, not of your code; a badly conditioned problem cannot be rescued by a better algorithm. For a matrix it is $\kappa(A) = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$, the ratio of largest to smallest singular value.

Go and read

Turing (1948), Rounding-off errors in matrix processes. Where the condition number was named, and (more importantly) where the property of the problem was first separated from the behaviour of the algorithm. That distinction is §6's whole subject, and it is worth seeing it being drawn for the first time by someone who had no prior vocabulary for it.

Part I §13 introduced this and moved on quickly. Here it gets the treatment it deserves, because it is the concept that explains most of what goes wrong in numerical work.

Definition: condition number

For solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, the condition number is

$$\kappa(A) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)}$$

and it bounds how badly a relative error in $\mathbf{b}$ can be amplified into a relative error in $\mathbf{x}$:

$$\frac{\|\delta\mathbf{x}\|}{\|\mathbf{x}\|} \;\leq\; \kappa(A)\, \frac{\|\delta\mathbf{b}\|}{\|\mathbf{b}\|}$$

Read the inequality as a budget. You start with about 16 good digits. Multiply the input error by $\kappa$ and you find how many you keep: a condition number of $10^{k}$ costs you roughly $k$ digits. At $\kappa = 10^{16}$ you have none left, and the computed answer carries no information about the true one.

Intuition: geometrically, how squashed is the transformation?

Part I §19 showed that every matrix maps the unit sphere to an ellipsoid, and the singular values are its semi-axis lengths. So $\kappa$ is the ratio of the longest axis to the shortest, or how far from spherical the ellipsoid is.

A near-spherical map is well conditioned: every direction is treated comparably, and inverting it is stable. A very flat ellipsoid means some direction was nearly collapsed, and undoing that collapse means multiplying by a huge number, which multiplies the error in that direction by the same huge number.

An infinitely flat ellipsoid is a singular matrix, with $\kappa = \infty$. Ill conditioning is simply proximity to singular, measured.

§6 Conditioning versus stability

Two different things that are routinely conflated, and separating them tells you where to look when an answer is wrong.

Examplenearly parallel columnsforming $A^\top A$ rather than using QR

The combination you want is a stable algorithm on a well-conditioned problem. A stable algorithm on a badly conditioned problem returns a poor answer and there is nothing wrong with your code; the question itself was fragile. An unstable algorithm on a well-conditioned problem is the case worth catching, because it is entirely your fault and entirely fixable.

Backward stability

An algorithm is backward stable if its computed answer is the exact answer to a slightly perturbed version of your problem.

This is the standard the good algorithms meet, and it is a subtle idea worth sitting with. It does not promise the answer is close to correct; it promises the algorithm introduced no more error than a tiny wobble in the input would have. Combined with the condition number, that gives the complete accuracy story: error $\approx$ stability $\times$ conditioning.

History: Turing named it, in a paper about rounding errors

In 1948 Alan Turing published a paper on rounding errors in matrix computation, motivated by the very practical question of whether the new electronic machines could be trusted to solve linear systems.

In it he introduced the term condition number and the idea it names: that some systems are intrinsically sensitive, so that no amount of care in the calculation can produce a trustworthy answer. He distinguished this from errors introduced by the method itself, the conditioning-versus-stability split above, drawn at the very beginning of the subject.

The framing was developed substantially by James Wilkinson in the 1960s, whose backward error analysis gave the field its modern vocabulary. But the concept, and the word, are Turing's, from the same few years as the Bayesian codebreaking in Part II §21 and the machine that bears his name.

§7 Where conditioning bites in learning

This is not a numerical-analysis footnote. Conditioning explains several things about machine learning that otherwise look like folklore.

1. Correlated features

Two features that are nearly duplicates make the data matrix have two nearly parallel columns. Nearly parallel columns mean a tiny smallest singular value, which means a huge $\kappa$, which means the fitted coefficients are wildly sensitive to noise. The model may predict well and its coefficients are meaningless, with one large positive weight cancelling one large negative weight, with the split between them decided by rounding.

This is why regularization stabilises coefficients. Adding $\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ adds $\lambda$ to every eigenvalue of the relevant matrix, which lifts the smallest one off the floor and cuts $\kappa$ directly. The statistical story about overfitting and the numerical story about conditioning are the same fact.

2. Unscaled features

One feature in metres and another in milligrams gives columns of wildly different magnitude, and hence a large $\kappa$ before any real structure is involved. This is the numerical reason for standardising inputs, and not merely because it "helps convergence"; it removes an artificial ill-conditioning you introduced by choosing units.

3. The optimisation landscape

The most important case, and the subject of §10. The curvature of a loss surface is described by a matrix, and the condition number of that matrix controls how badly gradient descent zigzags. A ratio of 1 means circular contours and a direct path to the minimum. A ratio of 1000 means a long narrow valley, in which the gradient points mostly across the valley rather than along it, and progress is agonising.

Why this matters: it is the reason modern optimisers exist

Momentum, RMSProp, Adam and every other method in §15–17 is, from one angle, a device for coping with bad conditioning. They do different things (accumulate velocity, rescale per parameter, estimate curvature) but the problem they are all solving is the one this section describes.

Likewise batch normalisation, careful initialisation, and residual connections all improve conditioning of the optimisation problem, whatever else they also do. Once you see this, a large chunk of deep learning practice stops looking like a bag of tricks and starts looking like one idea applied repeatedly.

Try it

A matrix has singular values $\{4, 2, 10^{-7}\}$. What is $\kappa$, roughly how many digits of accuracy do you expect to lose, and what does that tiny singular value mean geometrically?

Answer. $\kappa = 4 / 10^{-7} = 4 \times 10^{7}$, so expect to lose about seven or eight digits, leaving perhaps eight of the original sixteen. Usable, but you should not trust the result past that.

Geometrically, the matrix squashes one direction by a factor of ten million. It is very nearly rank-deficient: it almost destroys that direction entirely, and recovering anything along it requires multiplying by $10^7$, which multiplies whatever error is there by $10^7$ too. If the smallest singular value were exactly zero the matrix would be singular; $10^{-7}$ means it is close enough that the distinction barely matters in practice.
