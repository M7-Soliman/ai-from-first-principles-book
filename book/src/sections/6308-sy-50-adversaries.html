Part EAdversaries

Every measurement in this book has assumed the inputs were not chosen to defeat it. Drop that assumption and the worst case replaces the average, which is a different question with different answers, and one that no accuracy metric addresses.

§33 Stating a threat model

Three questions before any defence

What can the adversary do? Query the model, see its probabilities, modify the input, poison the training data, read the weights.

What are they trying to achieve? A specific wrong answer, any wrong answer, recovering data, cloning the model.

What do they know? The architecture, the weights, the training data, the defence.

A defence is only meaningful against a stated model, and the assumption that the adversary does not know the defence is the one that fails, repeatedly, and always in the same way. A large fraction of published defences were broken by an attack adapted to them.

§34 Adversarial examples

What the gap means for a claim

An empirical robustness number is an upper bound on the attack you tried; it says nothing about the attack you did not. A certified number is a lower bound on safety and is conservative by a large factor.

So the two numbers answer different questions and neither is "how robust is this model". Reporting empirical robustness without naming the attack is the most common error in this literature, and the fix is a sentence rather than a technique.

Randomised smoothing: how a certified number is actually produced

Take any classifier $f$ and build a new one from it: the smoothed classifier $g$ answers with whichever class $f$ returns most often on the input plus Gaussian noise,

$$g(x) = \arg\max_c \Pr_{\delta \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)}\big[f(x+\delta) = c\big].$$

$g$ can be certified even though $f$ cannot, because the guarantee only has to reason about the noise distribution, not about $f$'s internals. Let $p_A$ be the probability the winning class gets under the noise, estimated by sampling with a confidence correction rather than assumed exact. The Neyman–Pearson lemma, applied to two shifted Gaussians, gives the largest perturbation $g$ is guaranteed to survive without changing its answer:

$$R = \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(p_A)$$

where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the standard normal quantile. At $\sigma = 0.5$ and $p_A = 0.9$, $\Phi^{-1}(0.9) \approx 1.28$, so $R \approx 0.641$: a number, not the shape Figure 7 draws, and it is what "certification proves safety only for a much smaller budget" in that figure's caption is standing in for. The cost is exactly what buys the guarantee: raising $\sigma$ raises the certifiable radius and lowers clean accuracy, because a classifier that must stay right under heavy noise is a worse classifier on an unperturbed input: the same trade §28's privacy-utility curve makes, with noise doing the work in both places.

§35 Poisoning and backdoors

Attacking the training set rather than the input

Poisoning degrades a model by corrupting its data. A backdoor is worse and quieter: the model behaves normally except on inputs carrying a trigger the attacker chose, so every ordinary evaluation passes.

The reason this is not theoretical is the supply chain. Training data is scraped from sources anyone can write to; pretrained weights are downloaded; datasets are assembled from other datasets. A backdoor placed in any of those is inherited by everything downstream, and no accuracy metric detects it because accuracy is exactly what it preserves.

§36 Model extraction

Why the defences are weak, and what they are for

Rate limiting, output truncation and watermarking all raise the cost and none prevents the attack: Figure 8 measures a sixteen-fold increase from removing probabilities entirely, which is a speed bump rather than a wall.

The honest position is that query access is disclosure, at a price the attacker can pay, and the defences shift where the economics land rather than closing the door. Watermarking is therefore the most useful of them: it does not prevent extraction, it makes the result attributable, which is a legal remedy rather than a technical one.

§37 Agents, and the confused deputy

Pitfall: the oldest bug in security, in a new place

A model that reads untrusted content and takes actions has no reliable boundary between instruction and data. Text in a web page, a document or an email can be read as a command, and the model executes it with the user's authority. That is a confused deputy: a privileged component persuaded to act for someone who lacks the privilege.

What makes it hard is that the separation is architectural everywhere else and absent here. Parameterised queries exist because SQL had this problem and solved it structurally; a language model has one channel, so the same fix is not available.

Which leaves the remedies where they should have been anyway: least privilege, so a compromised agent can do little; confirmation for actions that are hard to reverse; and isolation, so what an agent reads cannot reach what it can spend. Part XXXI §35's shield, in another setting.

§38 What defends, and what does not

Go and read

Athalye, Carlini & Wagner (2018), Obfuscated gradients give a false sense of security. Seven defences broken in one paper, all by the same mechanism. The most useful thing to read before proposing a defence, and the source of the rule below: if a defence works because the attacker cannot compute something, assume it will be computed.

Three that hold up, and the pattern in the ones that do not

Adversarial training, training on attacked examples, is the only empirical defence that has held up broadly, and it costs accuracy and compute.

Certification gives a guarantee for a small radius, honestly: §34's smoothed classifier and its radius $R = \sigma\Phi^{-1}(p_A)$ are the mechanism, and "small" is quantitative, not a figure of speech.

Architecture (least privilege, isolation, confirmation) is what actually protects a deployed system, and it is not a model property at all.

The pattern in what fails: anything that obscures the gradient rather than changing the decision boundary. It defeats the standard attack, is published as a defence, and falls to an attack that estimates the gradient differently. If a defence's mechanism is that the attacker cannot compute something, assume it will be computed.

§39 What movement E establishes

Three things

A defence without a threat model is not a claim, and the assumption that fails is always that the adversary does not know the defence.

Query access is disclosure. Figure 8 measures a clone at 95% agreement in 40 queries, and removing probabilities raises the cost sixteen-fold without preventing it.

What protects a deployed system is architecture, not model properties. Least privilege, isolation and confirmation work against attacks nobody has thought of; a robustness number does not.
