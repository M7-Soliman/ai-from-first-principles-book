§9 The objective, and why it was enough

One loss

Predict the next token. Part XVI §8's chain-rule factorisation, Part XII's architecture, and cross-entropy. There is nothing else in the objective: no task, no labels, no auxiliary losses.

The reason this is more than it sounds: to predict the next token well over a large enough corpus you must model whatever generates the text. Predicting the last word of a proof requires the proof to be right. Predicting the closing brace requires tracking scope. The objective is trivial and the competence required to minimise it is not bounded in any obvious way.

Why this was not the expected outcome

Worth stating plainly, because the current view is retrospective. Language modelling was for decades an auxiliary task: a component of speech recognition or translation, evaluated by perplexity, not expected to produce anything usable on its own.

The change was not conceptual. It was that at sufficient scale (Part XVII), the same objective on the same architecture began producing behaviours nobody had trained for, and the sequence of results establishing that took the field by surprise more than once. The honest summary is that the objective's sufficiency was discovered empirically and is still not well explained.

§10 The recipe

What a pretraining run consists of
  1. A corpus, filtered, deduplicated and mixed (Part XIII, and the part where most of the differentiation actually lives).
  2. A tokenizer, fitted and frozen (§7).
  3. An architecture: a decoder-only transformer, pre-norm, rotary positions, grouped-query attention, a gated feed-forward. Part XII §§14–20's convergent stack.
  4. A budget, allocated between parameters and tokens by Part XVII §12, adjusted well past compute-optimal because the model will be served (Part XVII §14).
  5. A schedule: warmup, cosine or trapezoidal decay, AdamW, gradient clipping, bf16.
  6. The infrastructure of Part XVII's movements C and D.

Almost none of this is specific to language, which is the point. Six of these seven items were established in earlier parts, and the seventh is the corpus.

§11 The data mixture

Where the remaining differentiation is

Web text supplies scale, and most of it is low quality. Books and papers supply long-range structure. Code supplies something disproportionate: strict syntax, verifiable correctness, and long-range dependencies that must be exactly right, and models trained with more code are measurably better at reasoning tasks that are not code.

The mixture weights matter and are among the least-published details in the field. Upsampling high-quality sources, downsampling the web, and holding a small very-high-quality set for the end of training are all standard and rarely quantified.

Pitfall: the late-training mixture is doing work that looks like alignment

Changing the mixture in the final fraction of training (toward cleaner, more instructional, more carefully written text) improves the model in ways usually attributed to the post-training stages of movement D.

This matters for attribution. A comparison between two alignment methods is only about those methods if the pretraining ended the same way, and it often did not. Part XIV §21's ablation problem, in the place where it is hardest to control.

§12 Context length

Go and read

Liu et al. (2024), Lost in the middle: how language models use long contexts. The position effect measured across depths and models. The reason to report a depth profile rather than a window size, and the reason §38's context assembly puts the important material at the edges.

Trained short, extended long

Training at long context is expensive: quadratic attention, and Part XVII §16's activation memory. So the standard approach trains most of the run at a short context and extends at the end: continue training on longer sequences, with the position encoding adjusted so that positions beyond the original range are representable.

Rotary embeddings make this tractable, because they encode relative position and can be interpolated: compress the rotation frequencies so that a longer range maps into the range the model was trained on. A small amount of continued training then adapts it.

Pitfall: a long context window is not long-context ability

A model advertising a million tokens has been extended to a million and trained almost entirely on far less. What it can attend to and what it uses well are different quantities.

The measurement that separates them is a retrieval probe: place a fact at a controlled depth and ask for it. The reliable finding is a position effect: information at the beginning and end is used well and information in the middle is used worse, sometimes far worse. Report the depth profile, not the window size, and Part XIV §22's benchmark decay applies here too, since the standard probes are now trained against.

§13 What is actually learned, and in what order

A rough progression

Training is not uniform. Roughly, and measurable with the right probes: token frequencies first, then local syntax, then longer-range agreement, then the ability to use the context itself as a source of rules (§17). Factual content accumulates throughout and slowly.

The transitions can be sharp. Part XII §37's induction heads form over a narrow band of training and coincide with a visible bump in the loss curve, one of the few cases where a specific mechanism has been tied to a specific moment in training.

§14 Memorisation

What determines whether a sequence is stored verbatim

Part XIII §33 established that models memorise and that duplication is the main driver. Restated for this setting: verbatim recall rises sharply with the number of times a sequence appears in the corpus, and deduplication is therefore the most effective intervention; it is a data change rather than a modelling one.

The uncomfortable part is that memorisation and generalisation are not separable. The same capacity that stores a rare fact you want stores a training document you do not, and no known method removes one without the other. What is available is control of the inputs: deduplicate, filter, and know what is in the corpus.

§15 The loss curve, and what it hides

Pitfall: a smooth loss is compatible with a great deal of structure

The pretraining loss falls smoothly and predictably, which is Part XVII's whole subject. It is tempting to read that smoothness as meaning nothing discrete is happening.

It does not. Individual capabilities can appear over narrow bands while the aggregate loss (an average over billions of tokens, most of them easy) moves imperceptibly. §13's induction heads are the clearest case.

So the loss is the right thing to extrapolate and the wrong thing to watch if you want to know what the model can do. Evaluate capabilities separately and on a schedule, because the loss will not tell you.

§16 What pretraining does not give you

Three gaps, and movement D is about the first

It does not answer questions. A pretrained model continues text. Asked a question, its most likely continuation may be another question, because that is what the corpus contains. The capability is present and the interface is not.

It has no notion of what should be said. The objective rewards likelihood, and plenty of likely text is false, harmful or unhelpful. Nothing in pretraining distinguishes these.

It is fixed in time and cannot look anything up. Movement E.
