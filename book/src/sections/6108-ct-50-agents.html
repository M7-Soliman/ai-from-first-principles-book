Part EAgents

The word arrived twice in this book from opposite directions: an agent in Part XVIII is something that maximises reward in an environment, and an agent in Part XX §36 is a language model calling tools. They are the same object, and the second inherits every difficulty the first has spent forty years on.

§33 Long horizons, and structure

Why a flat policy fails at length

A task taking a thousand steps with reward only at the end gives a learner one bit of information per thousand decisions. Nothing recovers from that by sampling harder.

Every response imposes structure. Hierarchy gives a high-level policy that selects sub-policies running for many steps, so the outer problem is short. Goal conditioning trains one policy on many goals, so every trajectory is a success for the goal it happened to reach, which turns failures into data. Reward shaping supplies intermediate signal and risks Part XXI §18's problem: the shaped reward is what gets optimised.

§34 Other agents

Why this is not a harder environment

When other agents adapt, the environment is non-stationary from your side, so the convergence arguments of Part XVIII do not apply. Two agents each improving against the other can cycle forever without either being wrong.

The equilibrium concepts of game theory replace optimality, and self-play is the practical method: an opponent that improves as you do supplies a curriculum for free. Its characteristic failure is a strategy that beats its own lineage and loses to anything outside it, which is why populations and past-version pools exist.

For driving the interaction is the whole problem. A merge is a negotiation, and a planner that models other vehicles as obstacles on fixed trajectories is solving a different task from the one it faces.

§35 Acting under a safety requirement

Three approaches, and only one of them is a guarantee

Penalise. Add a cost for unsafe states. Simple, and it trades safety against reward at an exchange rate you set, so it will accept a violation if the reward is high enough.

Constrain. Optimise reward subject to a bound on expected cost. Better, and expectations say nothing about the tail.

Shield. Keep a verified fallback controller and a condition under which it takes over, so the learned policy acts only inside a region the fallback can recover from. This is the only one of the three that yields a statement you can put in a safety case, and it requires the fallback and the region to be established without learning.

§36 Tool use is control

The same loop, wearing different clothes

A language model that calls a tool, reads the result and calls another is running a control loop: observe, decide, act, observe. Part XX §36 introduced it as a capability; every difficulty in this part applies to it unchanged.

Errors compound: §19, exactly, and a policy trained on successful traces has never seen the states its own mistakes produce. Horizons are long and reward is terminal, so §33's structure problem is live. The environment is non-stationary when the tools are other agents. And latency is a stability question, which is §7 arriving somewhere nobody expects it.

The one honest difference is that the action space is discrete and enormous rather than continuous and small, which changes the algorithms and none of the difficulties.

§37 What an agent needs that a model does not

Four things, all of them state

Memory across steps, because the observation is not the state: §28's point in a different medium.

A way to stop. A policy with no terminal condition loops, and detecting "this is not working" is a separate competence from acting well.

Recovery. Undoing a bad action is a skill, and it is absent from any dataset of successful trajectories.

Calibrated uncertainty, because the decision to ask for help rather than act is a threshold on it: Part XXIV §33's cost ratio, in the loop.

§38 Modular or end-to-end

The argument, and what neither side disputes

For end-to-end: the interfaces of §31 are where information is lost and objectives diverge. A detector tuned for average precision is not tuned for what the planner needs, and no amount of improving it in isolation fixes that.

For modular: the intermediate representations are what make a system testable against ground truth, debuggable when it fails, and arguable about in the safety case of §43. Remove them and a failure has no locus.

Not in dispute: the geometry. Projection, calibration and rigid-body kinematics are correct by construction, and a learned system must approximate them from data. The productive middle keeps the parts that are derivable as structure and learns the rest, which is what every system that works currently does.

§39 What movement E establishes

Three things

Long horizons need structure, and every source of structure (hierarchy, goals, shaping) trades a difficulty for a different one.

A tool-using language model is a control loop, and inherits compounding error, terminal reward, non-stationarity and the latency-stability link, all of them unchanged.

Only a shield yields a guarantee. Penalties trade safety for reward, constraints bound an expectation, and neither is a statement about the tail.
