BuildThe project

Twelve to sixteen hours. You build a system whose ground truth you know, then try every technique in this part on it, which is the only setting where you can find out whether they work.

P.1 What you are building

glassbox: a small network with a known internal structure, a toolkit of interpretability methods applied to it, and an evaluation suite that reports what it establishes rather than what it measures.

The rule that governs the whole project: you must be able to check every answer. Every system here is one you constructed, so a claim about its internals is either right or wrong and you can tell. That is the opposite of how interpretability is usually practised, and it is why this is a project rather than a reading list.

P.2 Stage 1: build superposition, then look at it

  1. Implement the toy model: $m$ features into $n \lt m$ dimensions, ReLU reconstruction, importance-weighted loss.
  2. Sweep sparsity. Count represented features and measure the mean overlap.
  3. Now examine a single "neuron": one coordinate of the hidden vector. Find its top-activating inputs and write down the interpretation you would have published.
  4. Then check it against the ground truth you have.

Deliverable: the sparsity curve, and the neuron interpretation next to what the neuron actually is. Step four is the point of the stage.

P.3 Stage 2: recover the features

  1. Train a sparse autoencoder on the hidden activations. Sweep dictionary size and the $\ell_1$ coefficient.
  2. Measure recovery against the true directions, which you have.
  3. Run PCA as the control and explain its ceiling before you measure it.
  4. Widen the dictionary further and watch entries split. Decide where you would stop, and write down what makes that choice arbitrary.

Deliverable: a recovery curve, the PCA control, and a paragraph on the features you did not recover, which in a real model would be invisible to you.

P.4 Stage 3: present against used

  1. Train a network on a task depending on one part of its input.
  2. Probe the hidden layer for a property of a different part, which the task never consults.
  3. Ablate the probe's direction and re-measure the task. Include a random-direction control at the same norm.
  4. Then ablate the direction the task does use, so you have all three numbers.

Deliverable: the four accuracies. Predict them before running, and note which prediction you got wrong.

P.5 Stage 4: localise a computation

  1. Train a small transformer on a task with known structure and an irrelevant input.
  2. Build minimal pairs and sweep the patch site over layers and positions.
  3. Read the map. Explain every zero in it: there will be at least two kinds.
  4. Corrupt a different input and confirm the map changes as your explanation predicts.

Deliverable: the restoration grid, and a written account of what the method can and cannot see given the corruption you chose.

P.6 Stage 5: optimise a proxy until it breaks

  1. Build a true objective and a proxy that correlates with it. Sweep selection pressure and plot the true reward.
  2. Repeat with a heavier-tailed error at matched correlation.
  3. Add the two standard mitigations (cap the search, and ensemble the proxies) and measure what each buys.
  4. Throughout, plot the proxy score on the same axes.

Deliverable: the two curves, and the mitigation results with the tail statistic reported separately from the scale. Step four is what makes the failure legible: the proxy rises the whole time.

P.7 Stage 6: an evaluation that states what it establishes

  1. Write the sizing calculator and use it before designing the evaluation.
  2. Red-team your own system in all three modes for a fixed budget. Fix what you find.
  3. Red-team again, from a clean start, and compare the rates.
  4. Write the safety case: claim, evidence, assumptions, defeaters.

Deliverable: the safety case, with a stated bound rather than an absence of findings. It should be uncomfortable to write, and the discomfort is the accurate part.

P.8 Stretch

  • A dictionary on a real model, with the prediction test from §15.
  • A circuit, with sufficiency and necessity both checked.
  • The randomised-weights sanity check applied to every method you have built.
  • A reward hack under PPO, rather than under best-of-$n$.
  • Weak-to-strong at two different capability gaps.
  • Audit a published system card and write the safety case it implies.

P.9 Done when

  • Your toy model represents more features than it has dimensions, and you measured the interference that costs.
  • You wrote a neuron interpretation and then checked it against the truth.
  • Your dictionary beats PCA and does not reach 100%, and you can say what that implies for a model where you cannot check.
  • You have a probe above chance on a property whose ablation costs nothing, with a random control.
  • Your patching map has at least two zeros that mean different things, and you can say which.
  • You have a proxy whose score rose the entire time the true objective collapsed.
  • You measured what ensembling does to the scale and to the tail, separately.
  • Your safety case states a numerical bound and names a defeater.
  • Your README names one claim you would have published before running the control that killed it.
Why this project, and why it is last

Every technique here is normally applied to systems whose internals nobody knows, which means the practitioner never finds out when it fails. Building the system first inverts that: the methods can be scored, and several of them score worse than their reputation.

The final item on the checklist is the one that matters. Somewhere in this project you will form a confident interpretation and a control will destroy it. That experience is the actual deliverable, and it is the closest thing to a transferable skill this part contains.

The framework, one last time

Every model in this book was four choices: a dataset, a model family, a loss, and an optimiser. This part added a fifth that was implicit all along: a claim about what the result establishes. Movement A's probes and movement D's evaluations are both measurements of a trained system, and both are routinely reported as establishing more than they do.

That fifth choice is the one the framework never forced you to write down, and it is the one this part exists to make explicit. Write it down.
