Part BThe operations

§4 Matrix times vector: three views of one idea

In 60 seconds

$A\mathbf{x}$ has three readings, identical in arithmetic and very different in what they make obvious. Row view: a stack of dot products, best for computing. Column view: a mixture of $A$'s columns, with $\mathbf{x}$ as the recipe, best for reasoning about what is reachable. Function view: a transformation of space, best for geometry and composition. Fluent people switch between all three without noticing; this section is about doing it deliberately.

If you take one thing from this entire document, take this section. The expression $A\mathbf{x}$ has three interpretations. They are mathematically identical: same arithmetic, same answer, every time, but they are not cognitively identical, and different problems become easy under different readings. People who find linear algebra hard usually know exactly one of these. People who find it easy know all three and switch without thinking.

We will use one running example throughout, so you can watch the same computation three ways:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

View 1: the row view, a stack of dot products

This is the mechanical definition, and the one you would use to write the operation as a loop. Each entry of the output is one row of $A$ paired off against $\mathbf{x}$, multiplied entry by entry, and summed:

$$(A\mathbf{x})_i = \sum_{j} A_{i,j}\, x_j$$

Concretely, for our example:

Row view, worked

Row 1 of $A$ is $(2, -1)$. Pair it with $\mathbf{x} = (1.5, 2)$: $\;2 \times 1.5 + (-1) \times 2 = 3 - 2 = 1$.

Row 2 of $A$ is $(1, 1.5)$. Pair it with $\mathbf{x}$: $\;1 \times 1.5 + 1.5 \times 2 = 1.5 + 3 = 4.5$.

So $A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4.5 \end{bmatrix}$.

The row view answers "how do I compute this?" efficiently. It also explains the shape rule immediately: to pair a row of $A$ against $\mathbf{x}$, the row must have as many entries as $\mathbf{x}$ does. Hence the rule everyone memorises (the columns of $A$ must match the length of $\mathbf{x}$) is not an arbitrary convention but a direct consequence of what the operation does.

Watch the shapes

$A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ times $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ gives $A\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$. Write the shapes adjacent and cancel the inner pair: $(m \times \underline{n}) \cdot (\underline{n}) \to (m)$. The inner dimensions must agree and then vanish; the outer one survives. This one trick handles every shape question in the chapter.

View 2: the column view, a recipe for mixing

The sentence to keep: the columns of $A$ are the ingredients; $\mathbf{x}$ is the recipe. Most of Part I is this idea in different costumes.

This is the view that makes linear algebra make sense, and it is chronically underemphasised. Rewrite the same computation grouped by columns instead of rows:

$$A\mathbf{x} = x_1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + x_2 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1.5 \end{bmatrix} = 1.5 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1.5 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4.5 \end{bmatrix}$$

Same answer: check it against the row view above. But look at what the expression is saying: $A\mathbf{x}$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$, and the entries of $\mathbf{x}$ are the mixing amounts.

Think of the columns as ingredients and $\mathbf{x}$ as amounts. $A$ owns a fixed pantry of ingredient vectors; $\mathbf{x}$ is the recipe card saying how much of each to use; $A\mathbf{x}$ is the dish.

This reframing pays off immediately and repeatedly:

  • Which vectors $\mathbf{b}$ can $A\mathbf{x}$ produce? Exactly the ones you can mix from the columns. That set has a name (the column space), and it is the entire content of Section 12.
  • Why does $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ sometimes have no solution? Because $\mathbf{b}$ is not mixable from the available ingredients. No recipe can produce a dish requiring something not in the pantry.
  • What is rank? How many genuinely different ingredients you have, once you discard the duplicates. Section 11.
Intuition: the sentence to memorise

The columns of $A$ are the ingredients; $\mathbf{x}$ is the recipe. When a later section confuses you (span, rank, solvability, the pseudoinverse, even PCA), come back to this sentence first. A remarkable amount of the chapter is this one idea in different costumes.

View 3: the function view, a matrix is a verb

The third view steps back. Fix $A$ and let $\mathbf{x}$ vary. Then $A$ is a function that eats a vector and returns a vector:

$$f_A : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m, \qquad f_A(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}$$

Under this reading a matrix is not data at all; it is a transformation, a verb. It takes the whole input space and moves it somewhere.

These are not arbitrary functions. They are exactly the functions that are linear, meaning they respect addition and scaling:

$$f(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = f(\mathbf{u}) + f(\mathbf{v}), \qquad f(c\,\mathbf{u}) = c\, f(\mathbf{u})$$

Those two lines look like arbitrary bookkeeping, so it is worth saying what they actually demand. Both say the same thing in different clothes: the transformation does not care whether you combine before or after applying it. You may add two vectors and then transform the sum, or transform each and then add: same answer. You may double a vector and then transform it, or transform it and then double: same answer. The operation commutes with mixing.

An everyday example makes the restriction concrete. Converting currency is linear: exchange £50 and £30 separately, or exchange £80 at once, and you end up with the same money. Computing income tax is not linear, because the rate changes with the amount: two people each earning £30,000 pay less between them than one person earning £60,000. Tax cares about how the total was assembled; currency conversion does not. Only the second kind of operation is a matrix.

That is also why the constraint bites so hard. A linear map cannot have a threshold, a saturation point, a special case, or a bonus for being large, as any of those would mean the result depends on how the input was put together. What survives is a very small repertoire: stretch, squash, rotate, reflect, shear, flatten. Nothing bends, and the origin never moves (put $c = 0$ in the second equation and you get $f(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0}$, with no choice in the matter).

That is a strong restriction with a beautiful consequence: a linear function is completely determined by what it does to a handful of basis vectors. Know where $\mathbf{e}_1 = (1,0)$ and $\mathbf{e}_2 = (0,1)$ land, and you know where everything lands, because every other vector is a combination of those two and linearity carries the combination through. And where do they land? Look:

$$A\mathbf{e}_1 = A\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} = \text{column 1 of } A, \qquad A\mathbf{e}_2 = A\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix} = \text{column 2 of } A$$

So the columns of $A$ are the images of the basis vectors. The column view and the function view are the same fact seen from two angles, which is exactly why the ingredient metaphor keeps working.

Intuition: what linear really rules out

Linear maps can rotate, stretch, squash, shear, reflect, and flatten. They cannot bend, curve, or move the origin. A useful test: if grid lines stay straight, evenly spaced, and parallel, and the origin does not move, it is linear. This is precisely why neural networks need those nonlinear functions between layers: a stack of linear maps is still just a linear map, so without $g$ and $f$ the network in Section 1 could only ever draw straight boundaries no matter how many layers it had.

Try it

Let $B = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 & -1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and $\mathbf{v} = (2, 1, 0)$. First: what shape is $B\mathbf{v}$? Then compute it using the row view, and again using the column view.

Answer. $B$ is $2 \times 3$ and $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, so $(2 \times \underline{3}) \cdot (\underline{3}) \to \mathbb{R}^2$: the output is a vector of length 2.

Row view: row 1 is $(1, 0, 2)$, giving $1(2) + 0(1) + 2(0) = 2$. Row 2 is $(0, 3, -1)$, giving $0(2) + 3(1) + (-1)(0) = 3$. So $B\mathbf{v} = (2, 3)$.

Column view: $2\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix}0\\3\end{bmatrix} + 0\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\0\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}0\\3\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix}$. Notice the third column contributed nothing, because the recipe asked for zero of it, a small preview of why some directions can be invisible to a transformation.

§5 Matrix times matrix

History: why multiplication is defined so strangely

Matrix multiplication is the one operation students reliably find arbitrary. Why rows-against-columns? Why not multiply entrywise, which is simpler and more obvious?

Because Cayley did not define it to combine numbers. He defined it to compose transformations. If you substitute one linear change of variables into another and grind out the algebra, the coefficients of the result are exactly rows-times-columns. The definition is not a choice; it is what falls out.

The entrywise product is perfectly well defined and gets a name of its own, the Hadamard product in §6. It is simpler and far less useful, because it corresponds to no natural operation on transformations.

This is why §4's function view is worth the effort. Composition explains the definition, explains why order matters, and explains why $AB$ means "do $B$ first", none of which the arithmetic alone can motivate.

Once matrix-vector multiplication is solid, matrix-matrix is a short step. The definition:

$$C = AB, \qquad C_{i,j} = \sum_{k} A_{i,k} B_{k,j}$$

In words: entry $(i,j)$ of the product is row $i$ of $A$ paired against column $j$ of $B$. But there is a better way to hold it in your head.

Intuition: matrix-matrix is matrix-vector, in parallel

Read $B$ as a collection of columns sitting side by side. Then $$AB = A\begin{bmatrix} | & | & & | \\ \mathbf{b}_1 & \mathbf{b}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{b}_p \\ | & | & & | \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} | & | & & | \\ A\mathbf{b}_1 & A\mathbf{b}_2 & \cdots & A\mathbf{b}_p \\ | & | & & | \end{bmatrix}$$ Each column of the output is $A$ applied to the corresponding column of the input. Matrix-matrix multiplication is just matrix-vector multiplication done to several vectors at once, which, not coincidentally, is exactly why neural networks process data in batches.

Under the function view there is an even cleaner reading: $AB$ is the composition of two transformations. Apply $B$ first, then $A$. The product matrix is the single matrix that does both in one step, which is a genuinely useful fact: it means a deep stack of linear layers can always be collapsed into one layer.

That reading also removes the arbitrariness from the definition, which is worth doing because "rows against columns" is the step students most often accept without believing. Here is where it comes from, using nothing but §4.

We want a single matrix $C$ that behaves like "do $B$, then $A$". Recall from §4 that a matrix is completely determined by where it sends the basis vectors, and that column $j$ of a matrix is exactly where it sends $\mathbf{e}_j$. So to build $C$, all we have to do is work out where the composite sends each basis vector, and that is a two-step question with an obvious answer. Send $\mathbf{e}_j$ through $B$: you land on column $j$ of $B$. Now send that through $A$. Whatever comes out is column $j$ of $C$.

In symbols, column $j$ of $C$ is $A\mathbf{b}_j$, which is precisely the picture in the box above. And expanding $A\mathbf{b}_j$ by the row view, row $i$ of $A$ paired against $\mathbf{b}_j$, gives entry $(i,j)$ as $\sum_k A_{i,k}B_{k,j}$. Rows against columns was never a convention. It is the arithmetic you are forced into once you decide that multiplying matrices should mean composing the transformations they represent.

The order convention falls out of the same argument, which is some comfort if it annoys you. $AB$ means "$A$ applied to the result of $B$" because that is what $A(B\mathbf{x})$ says, and the matrix nearest the vector necessarily touches it first.

Pitfall: the order reads right to left

In $AB\mathbf{x}$, the matrix nearest the vector acts first. So $AB$ means "do $B$, then $A$", which is the opposite of English reading order and catches everyone at least once. It matches function notation ($f(g(x))$ also does $g$ first), so if you find the convention annoying, blame $f \circ g$.

Watch the shapes

$(m \times \underline{n})(\underline{n} \times p) \to (m \times p)$. Inner dimensions must match and then disappear; outer dimensions survive. So a $3\times4$ times a $4\times2$ gives a $3\times2$, and a $3\times4$ times a $2\times4$ is simply undefined; no amount of wishing makes it work.

Try it

$A$ is $5 \times 3$, $B$ is $3 \times 7$, $C$ is $7 \times 2$. Which of $ABC$, $CBA$, $A^\top B$ are defined, and what shape is each?

Answer. $ABC$: $(5\times3)(3\times7)(7\times2) \to 5 \times 2$. Defined: the inner dimensions chain correctly. $CBA$: $(7\times2)(3\times7)$ needs $2 = 3$, so it fails immediately. $A^\top B$: transposing $A$ gives $3 \times 5$, and $(3\times5)(3\times7)$ needs $5 = 3$, so this fails too.

§6 Transpose, elementwise products, broadcasting

Transpose

Flip the matrix across its main diagonal: rows become columns:

$$(A^\top)_{i,j} = A_{j,i}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}^{\!\top} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

Shape $m \times n$ becomes $n \times m$. That is the mechanics, and the mechanics are all most treatments give you, which leaves the transpose feeling like a piece of notational housekeeping that exists to make shapes line up. It is worth knowing what it actually means, because $A^\top$ appears in nearly every gradient formula in this book and reading it as "the flipped one" gets you nothing.

What a transpose means

Start from the row and column views. In $A\mathbf{x}$, the columns of $A$ are the ingredients and the rows are the measurements: each row, dotted against the input, produces one output number. The transpose swaps those two roles. What were the measuring instruments become the ingredients, and vice versa.

The consequence is that $A^\top$ sends information the other way. If $A$ maps $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, then $A^\top$ maps $\mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$, back across the same connection, in the reverse direction. Picture $A$ as a bundle of wires between two banks of units, with $A_{i,j}$ the strength of the wire from input $j$ to output $i$. Then $A^\top$ is the very same wiring, read from the far end: the strength of the connection into $j$ from $i$. Nothing was recomputed. You changed which end you are standing at.

Why deep learning cares: the transpose is the backward pass

This is why every backpropagation formula is full of transposes, and it is worth seeing now rather than being surprised by it in Part VII.

A network layer sends activations forward through $W$. Training needs to send error signals backward along exactly the same connections: a unit that contributed strongly to an output should receive a correspondingly strong share of the blame for that output's error. The strengths are already recorded in $W$; the only thing that changes is which end you read them from.

So the backward pass through a linear layer is multiplication by $W^\top$. Not an approximation, not a convenient coincidence; the same wires, traversed the other way. When you meet $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)} = W^{(k+1)\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k+1)}$ in Part VII, that transpose is this paragraph.

Two properties you will use without thinking within a week:

$$(A^\top)^\top = A, \qquad (AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$$

The second one (the order reverses) is worth a moment. It looks like a quirk but it is forced: for $(AB)^\top$ to even have valid shapes, the factors must swap. Under the function view it is the same reason that undoing "socks then shoes" requires "shoes off, then socks off".

The transpose is also what lets us write the dot product compactly. For $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v} = \sum_i u_i v_i$$

By convention vectors are columns, so $\mathbf{u}^\top$ is a row, a $1 \times n$ matrix, and $(1 \times n)(n \times 1) \to 1 \times 1$, a scalar. The shape rule delivers the right answer without any special-casing.

Pitfall: $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v}$ versus $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top$

These look nearly identical and produce wildly different objects. With $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$:

$\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}$ is $(1\times3)(3\times1) \to 1 \times 1$: a scalar, the dot product.

$\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top$ is $(3\times1)(1\times3) \to 3 \times 3$: a whole matrix, called the outer product. Every entry is $u_i v_j$.

One number versus nine. Read the transposes carefully: this is one of the most common misreadings in the entire book.

Elementwise (Hadamard) product

Multiply matching entries. Written $A \odot B$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \odot \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 12 \\ 21 & 32 \end{bmatrix}$$

Both operands must be the same shape, and the output keeps that shape. This is a completely different operation from matrix multiplication and shares nothing with it but the word "product".

Pitfall: * is not matrix multiplication in NumPy

In NumPy, A * B is the elementwise product and A @ B is the matrix product. Getting these confused is a rite of passage, and it is especially nasty because when $A$ and $B$ are square and the same size, both operations run without error and quietly give different answers. Your code does not crash; it is just wrong. Use @ when you mean matrix multiplication, always.

Broadcasting

Strictly, you cannot add a vector to a matrix, as the shapes differ. In practice every numerical library lets you, by silently copying the vector across the rows:

$$C = A + \mathbf{b} \quad \text{means} \quad C_{i,j} = A_{i,j} + b_j$$

This is called broadcasting, and the book uses it without much ceremony because it is so convenient. Its main use is exactly the bias term in a neural network layer: you have a batch of 32 examples in a $32 \times 10$ matrix and one bias vector of length 10, and you want to add that same bias to every row.

import numpy as np

A = np.arange(6).reshape(3, 2)     # shape (3, 2)
b = np.array([10, 20])              # shape (2,)

print(A + b)      # b is copied down all 3 rows
### [[10 21]
###  [12 23]
###  [14 25]]

# the two "products" are genuinely different operations
M = np.array([[1., 2.], [3., 4.]])
print(M * M)      ### [[ 1.  4.]   elementwise
                  ###  [ 9. 16.]]
print(M @ M)      ### [[ 7. 10.]   matrix product
                  ###  [15. 22.]]
Pitfall: broadcasting hides bugs

Broadcasting is convenient precisely because it is silent, and silent is exactly what you do not want when a shape is wrong. Adding a $(3,)$ vector to a $(3,1)$ column does not fail; it broadcasts to a $(3,3)$ matrix, which is almost never what anyone meant. If a result has a shape you did not expect, suspect broadcasting first.

In the framework: the operations of this part, and the batch axis

Everything in §§4–6 exists in PyTorch under the same names, and the notation is close enough to the mathematics that translating a formula is usually mechanical:

import torch

M = torch.tensor([[1.,2.],[3.,4.]])
M * M          # [[1, 4], [9, 16]]    elementwise -- the Hadamard product
M @ M          # [[7, 10], [15, 22]]  matrix product

u, v = torch.tensor([1.,2.,3.]), torch.tensor([4.,5.,6.])
u @ v                    # 32.0        the dot product,   u'v
torch.outer(u, v).shape  # (3, 3)      the outer product, uv'

A.T            # transpose, for a 2-D tensor
A.mT           # transpose of the LAST TWO axes -- the one to use on batches

The * versus @ trap is identical here, and identically dangerous: on square operands both run and return different answers. The pitfall above is not a NumPy quirk, it is a consequence of one symbol having been claimed by elementwise multiplication long before matrix libraries existed.

The batch axis, and why @ is stranger than it looks

Section 5 said matrix-matrix multiplication is matrix-vector multiplication done to several vectors at once, and noted this is why networks process data in batches. Frameworks build that observation into the operator itself.

X = torch.randn(32, 4, 3)      # 32 separate 4x3 matrices
Y = torch.randn(32, 3, 5)      # 32 separate 3x5 matrices
(X @ Y).shape             # (32, 4, 5) -- 32 independent products, in one call

Given tensors of more than two axes, @ treats the last two as the matrix and every axis before them as a stack of independent problems. The leading axes are batch axes: not part of the linear algebra, just a count of how many copies you are doing at once.

This is the single most important convention to internalise before reading real model code, and it explains two things that otherwise look arbitrary. It is why .mT exists; a plain .T would reverse all the axes and scramble the batch dimension along with the matrix. And it is why almost every shape in a modern codebase begins with something like (batch, sequence, features): the mathematics only ever concerns the last one or two entries, and everything to the left is bookkeeping about how many instances are travelling together.

The pitfall about silent broadcasting compounds here rather than disappearing. A batch axis of 1 will broadcast against a batch axis of 32 without complaint, which is sometimes precisely what you want and sometimes a bug that produces plausible numbers for hours. Write the shape down at every step: §0.4's habit is worth more in a framework than it was on paper, because the framework will not stop you.

§7 The rules of the algebra (and the one that fails)

Matrix algebra behaves like ordinary algebra in most respects. The exceptions matter enormously.

What holds

PropertyStatement
Distributive$A(B + C) = AB + AC$
Associative$(AB)C = A(BC)$
Scalars slide$c(AB) = (cA)B = A(cB)$
Transpose reverses$(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$
Identity does nothing$I A = A I = A$

Associativity deserves a note, because it is not merely aesthetic; it has real performance consequences. Suppose $A$ is $1000 \times 1000$, $B$ is $1000 \times 1000$, and $\mathbf{x}$ is a vector. Computing $(AB)\mathbf{x}$ forms a full $1000 \times 1000$ product first, at a cost of about $10^9$ multiplications. Computing $A(B\mathbf{x})$ does two matrix-vector products, about $2 \times 10^6$. Same answer, five hundred times less work. Associativity is what makes that rearrangement legal.

What fails

Matrix multiplication is not commutative. In general:

$$AB \neq BA$$

This is not an edge case; it is the normal situation. A concrete pair:

Order matters, worked

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (a shear), $\;B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (a quarter-turn rotation).

$AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

$BA = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Different matrices. Under the function view this is obvious rather than surprising: shearing a shape and then rotating it does not land in the same place as rotating it first and then shearing. Order of operations changes outcomes in the physical world too.

One consequence to internalise now: you cannot "cancel" matrices the way you cancel numbers. From $AB = AC$ you may not conclude $B = C$; that step requires $A$ to be invertible, a condition Section 10 makes precise and Section 12 shows often fails.

Pitfall: the dot product is the exception

Vector dot products are commutative: $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{u}$, because both are the same sum of products of numbers. So you will see commutativity work for dot products and fail for matrices in the same paragraph. That is correct, not a typo.

§8 Worked example: a neural network layer, by hand

Time to collect the winnings. We now have enough to compute a real neural network layer with actual numbers, with no libraries, no hand-waving. This is the same operation a GPU performs billions of times per second during training.

The setup: three input features, two output units.

$$W = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2}, \quad \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$$

The layer computes $\mathbf{h} = g(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})$, where $g$ is the ReLU function, the simplest nonlinearity in wide use, defined as $g(z) = \max(0, z)$ applied to each entry independently. It keeps positive numbers and zeroes out negative ones. That is all it does.

Step 1: check the shapes before computing anything

$W\mathbf{x}$ is $(2 \times \underline{3})(\underline{3}) \to \mathbb{R}^2$. Then $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ matches, so the addition is legal. Then $g$ is applied entrywise and preserves shape. Final output: $\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^2$.

Notice we established the output shape without doing a single multiplication. This is the habit from §0.4, and it is how you catch errors before they cost you an hour.

Step 2: compute $W\mathbf{x}$, both ways

Row view. Row 1 of $W$ is $(0.5, -1, 2)$, so $0.5(2) + (-1)(1) + 2(3) = 1 - 1 + 6 = 6$. Row 2 is $(1, 0, -0.5)$, so $1(2) + 0(1) + (-0.5)(3) = 2 + 0 - 1.5 = 0.5$. Thus $W\mathbf{x} = (6,\, 0.5)$.

Column view, as a check. The columns of $W$ are $(0.5, 1)$, $(-1, 0)$ and $(2, -0.5)$, and the recipe is $\mathbf{x} = (2, 1, 3)$:

$2\begin{bmatrix}0.5\\1\end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\end{bmatrix} + 3\begin{bmatrix}2\\-0.5\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}6\\-1.5\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}6\\0.5\end{bmatrix}$, as expected.

Step 3: add the bias, then apply the nonlinearity

$W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix}6\\0.5\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}7\\-1.5\end{bmatrix}$

$g\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}7\\-1.5\end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix}\max(0,7)\\\max(0,-1.5)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}7\\0\end{bmatrix}$

So $\mathbf{h} = (7, 0)$. The second unit was switched off by the ReLU. That is not a malfunction; it is the entire point of a nonlinearity. Different inputs activate different subsets of units, and that selectivity is what lets a network carve up its input space into regions rather than applying one uniform linear rule.

Verify the whole thing in five lines:

import numpy as np

W = np.array([[0.5, -1., 2.], [1., 0., -0.5]])   # (2, 3)
b = np.array([1., -2.])                            # (2,)
x = np.array([2., 1., 3.])                        # (3,)

h = np.maximum(0, W @ x + b)
print(h)     ### [7. 0.]
In the framework: this layer is nn.Linear, and nothing more

The computation you just did by hand has a name in every deep learning library. Here it is, forced to use your $W$ and $\mathbf{b}$ so the output is comparable:

import torch, torch.nn as nn

layer = nn.Linear(3, 2)                    # 3 inputs -> 2 outputs
with torch.no_grad():                     # overwrite the random initialisation
    layer.weight.copy_(torch.tensor([[0.5,-1.,2.],[1.,0.,-0.5]]))
    layer.bias.copy_(torch.tensor([1., -2.]))

x = torch.tensor([2., 1., 3.])
torch.relu(layer(x))                      ### tensor([7., 0.])

Same $(7, 0)$, same second unit switched off. nn.Linear is $W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ and contains no other idea.

Three things the wrapper is actually giving you

1. The weights are stored transposed relative to your instinct. Note layer.weight has shape $(2, 3)$, output size first. The layer computes $\mathbf{x}W^\top + \mathbf{b}$ rather than $W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$, because in practice the input arrives as a batch: a matrix $X$ of shape $(n, 3)$ with one example per row, and $XW^\top$ is $(n,2)$, one output per row. The convention exists so the batch axis stays leftmost, per the box in §6. It is the same arithmetic wearing a transpose.

2. It registers its parameters. The weight and bias are not ordinary tensors but nn.Parameters, which means the layer can hand them to an optimiser on request and autograd knows to differentiate with respect to them. That bookkeeping (knowing which of the thousands of tensors in a program are the ones being learned) is most of what a layer object is for.

3. It initialises them. The random values you just overwrote were not arbitrary; they were drawn from a distribution scaled to the layer's width. Do that wrong and a deep network will not train at all, which is a fact this book does not ask you to take on faith; Part VII §11 derives the correct scale and Figure 7 there shows what happens without it.

In Keras the same object is tf.keras.layers.Dense(2), which infers the input size on first call rather than taking it as an argument. The stored weight is $(3, 2)$ rather than $(2, 3)$ (the opposite convention), which is a genuine source of confusion when porting weights between the two, and a good reason to check shapes rather than assume them.

Why deep learning cares

You have now hand-computed a forward pass. A real network stacks perhaps fifty of these layers, with $W$ matrices of size $4096 \times 4096$ rather than $2 \times 3$, but the arithmetic is identical, and every one of those layers is doing exactly what you just did. Training is the process of nudging the entries of every $W$ and $\mathbf{b}$ until the outputs are useful, which is Parts III, V and VII. Nothing about the linear algebra changes; only the numbers get bigger.

Try it

Same $W$ and $\mathbf{b}$, new input $\mathbf{x} = (0, 4, 1)$. Compute $\mathbf{h}$ by hand. How many units survive the ReLU this time?

Answer. Row 1: $0.5(0) + (-1)(4) + 2(1) = -2$. Row 2: $1(0) + 0(4) + (-0.5)(1) = -0.5$. Adding the bias gives $(-2 + 1,\; -0.5 - 2) = (-1, -2.5)$. Both entries are negative, so the ReLU zeroes both and $\mathbf{h} = (0, 0)$. Zero units survive; this input activates nothing in this layer. In a real network that happens routinely, and a layer where it happens for every input is a well-known failure mode called a dead unit.
