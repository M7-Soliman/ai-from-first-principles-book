Part ATokenization

§1 A decision images do not require

In 60 seconds

An image arrives as numbers. Text arrives as characters, and before any model sees it someone must decide what the units are. That decision is made once, frozen into the vocabulary, and it silently determines what the model finds easy, including some things it will never do well.

Two obvious choices, and why both fail

Characters. A tiny vocabulary and no unknown inputs, at the cost of very long sequences, and attention is quadratic in length (Part XII §13), so this is expensive in exactly the wrong way.

Words. Short sequences and a vocabulary that is unbounded: new words, names, typos, compounds and every other language. Whatever you do not have becomes a single unknown symbol, which throws information away irrecoverably.

Subword tokenization is the compromise, and it is not a neutral one.

§2 Byte-pair encoding

Go and read

Sennrich, Haddow & Birch (2016), Neural machine translation of rare words with subword units. Byte-pair encoding brought to neural sequence models, and the argument for why the unknown token had to go. The algorithm is three lines and the paper is short; what makes it worth reading is how directly the motivation, rare words and morphology, leads to the method.

The algorithm, which is three lines

Start from bytes. Count every adjacent pair in the corpus, merge the most frequent into a new symbol, and repeat until the vocabulary is the size you want.

The result: frequent words become single tokens, rare words split into pieces, and anything never seen still encodes, because bytes are always available. No unknown token is ever needed, which is the property that made it standard.

Two details that matter more than they look. Merges are not allowed to cross a pre-tokenization boundary, so a word cannot merge with the punctuation after it. And training is done on unique word types weighted by frequency rather than on the raw stream, which is what makes it affordable.

§3 What a tokenizer commits you to

Why the fertility gap is a fairness problem and a cost problem

A tokenizer trained mostly on English represents English efficiently and everything else in pieces. The consequences are all direct, and none of them is about model quality:

  • Cost. Priced per token, the same content costs several times more in an underrepresented language.
  • Context. A fixed context window holds several times less of it.
  • Quality. More tokens per unit of meaning means longer dependencies for the same content, and Part XI §11 says longer is harder.

None of this is a claim about which languages are harder. It is a consequence of which text was in the tokenizer's training corpus, decided before any model existed.

§4 Numbers, and the failure everyone has seen

Pitfall: arithmetic fails at the tokenizer, not at the model

The figure's right panel is the mechanism. With merges allowed inside numbers, 1024 becomes two tokens because that digit run is frequent in the corpus, while 9999 stays at four, unmerged. Two four-digit numbers, different decompositions, and neither respects place value.

A model asked to add them is not working with digits in columns. It is working with tokens whose boundaries fall in arbitrary places, and the carry structure of arithmetic is invisible in its input. That is why large models were unreliable at multi-digit arithmetic long after they were writing working code.

The fix is in the tokenizer: isolate digits at the pre-tokenization boundary, so runs of digits cannot merge freely and place value is at least partly recoverable. Some tokenizers (Llama, PaLM, Gemma) split every digit singly; the widely used GPT-3.5/4/4o family instead groups digits into fixed runs of up to three, which is a weaker version of the same fix; it still stops a four-digit and a five-digit number from being indistinguishable single tokens. Either way, the improvement in arithmetic is a tokenizer change rather than a modelling one.

The same argument explains spelling and rhyming failures. If strawberry is two tokens, the letters are not in the input as separate objects, so counting them is a task about something the model cannot see.

§5 Vocabulary size

What sets the optimum

Larger vocabulary means shorter sequences, and attention is quadratic in length. It also means a larger embedding matrix and a larger output softmax, both linear in vocabulary, and the output layer is computed at every position.

The figure's shape is the argument: the sequence shortens quickly at first and then slowly, while the embedding grows without limit. So there is an interior optimum, and it moves with the model's width: a wider model pays more per vocabulary entry, so it wants a relatively smaller vocabulary than the token-count curve alone suggests.

Typical modern choices are 32,000 to 256,000. The upper end is driven by multilingual coverage rather than by English efficiency: buying down §3's fertility gap costs vocabulary.

§6 What byte-level would cost

Why the obvious clean solution is not used

Operating on raw bytes removes every problem in this movement: no vocabulary decision, no fertility gap, no arithmetic pathology, no unknown inputs, and identical treatment of every language.

It costs roughly the sequence-length factor in the figure (three to five times more positions for the same text), and attention is quadratic, so that is an order of magnitude of compute for a fixed context. That is the entire reason it is not standard.

The work on removing the tokenizer is therefore mostly work on hierarchy: process bytes locally with something cheap, pool into larger units, and run the expensive stack on those. The interesting versions learn where the boundaries go rather than fixing them, which would make §3's commitment a learned quantity instead of a frozen one.

§7 The tokenizer is frozen first

Pitfall: it is chosen before anything can be measured

The tokenizer must exist before pretraining starts, so it is fitted on a sample of the corpus and then frozen for the model's entire life. Every downstream decision inherits it, and changing it invalidates the weights completely.

Which means the decision is made at the point of least information: before any model has been trained, before anyone knows what the model will be used for, and often before the final data mixture is settled. Part XIII §34's point about data decisions being the hardest to revise, in its sharpest form.

The practical advice is narrow but worth stating: measure fertility on the languages and domains you actually care about before committing, because it is cheap then and impossible later.

§8 What movement A establishes

Three things

The units are a choice, made by a frequency-counting algorithm on a corpus someone selected, and frozen before the model exists.

Several famous failures are tokenizer failures. Arithmetic, spelling and rhyming are tasks about structure the model cannot see, and the fix for the first one was a change to the pre-tokenization rule.

The cost is unevenly distributed, in money and in context, along the axis of who was in the training corpus.
