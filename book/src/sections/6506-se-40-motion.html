Part DA continuous world

Everything so far assumed a finite set of states you could put in a hash table. A robot arm, a quadrotor and a car have none of that: the state is a vector of real numbers, the actions are a continuum, and the obstacles are described in the wrong coordinates. This movement is how search survives that, and it is the part of the classical tradition that never went out of use.

§23 Configuration space

One point per pose

The configuration space $\mathcal{C}$ has one dimension per degree of freedom, so a whole robot in a whole pose is a single point. A six-joint arm lives in a six-dimensional space; a car in the plane lives in $\mathbb{R}^2\times S^1$: two positions and an angle, on a space that wraps.

Obstacles transform too. A physical obstacle becomes $\mathcal{C}_{\text{obs}}$, the set of configurations in which the robot touches it, and its shape depends on the robot's geometry as much as the obstacle's. Planning is then finding a continuous curve in $\mathcal{C}_{\text{free}}$ from start to goal, a clean, coordinate-free statement of a problem that is a mess in the original coordinates.

What the transformation costs

The abstraction is exactly right and computing it is intractable. $\mathcal{C}_{\text{obs}}$ is generally not convex, not connected, and has no closed form for a nontrivial robot, so nobody builds it. What is available instead is a collision check: a routine that takes a configuration and answers whether it is free. That single primitive is what every algorithm below is built on, and it is the dominant cost: a planner's running time is usually measured in collision checks rather than seconds, because that is the number that transfers between machines.

§24 Why you cannot just discretise

The arithmetic that ends the discussion

The obvious move is to grid the space and run A*. At a resolution of 100 per axis: a two-dimensional problem has $10^4$ cells, which is nothing; a six-joint arm has $10^{12}$; a two-arm manipulation problem in twelve dimensions has $10^{24}$, which is more cells than a modern machine has bytes by six orders of magnitude.

And resolution is not a free parameter to lower. A passage narrower than one cell disappears, so the planner reports failure on a solvable problem, and the coarser the grid the more often that happens. You are trading completeness against memory with no setting that gives you both, the same bind as Part VI §21's curse of dimensionality, in the one place where it cannot be argued away by saying real data lies on a manifold. The free space really is high-dimensional.

§25 Sampling instead of enumerating

The move that made motion planning practical

Do not represent the free space. Sample configurations, keep the ones that are collision free, and connect nearby ones with straight-line segments that also test free. The graph you build is a finite approximation of a continuous space that was never constructed, and its size is chosen by you rather than dictated by the dimension.

What is given up is exact completeness. What replaces it is probabilistic completeness: if a solution exists, the probability of finding it goes to one as the number of samples grows. If no solution exists, the algorithm runs forever and never says so. That asymmetry is real and is why deployed planners always carry a timeout and a fallback.

§26 Probabilistic roadmaps

Go and read

Kavraki, Švestka, Latombe & Overmars (1996), Probabilistic roadmaps for path planning in high-dimensional configuration spaces. The paper that made motion planning practical by refusing to represent the free space. The narrow-passage problem is stated honestly here, which is more than most of the follow-up literature does.

Build the graph once, query it many times

Learning phase. Sample $N$ free configurations. Connect each to its $k$ nearest neighbours when the straight segment between them is collision free. The result is a roadmap of the free space.

Query phase. Connect start and goal to the roadmap and run A* on it, a small finite graph, back in the territory of Part B.

The split is the point. A factory arm works in a fixed cell for years, so the expensive phase is paid once and every subsequent query is milliseconds. PRMs are the right answer for a static environment with many queries, and the wrong answer for a changing one, where the roadmap is invalidated by the first thing that moves.

Two refinements matter in practice. Uniform sampling wastes almost everything on wide-open regions and starves the narrow passages that are the actual difficulty, so real implementations bias sampling toward the obstacle boundary or toward regions where connection attempts fail. And connecting to $k$ nearest neighbours needs a nearest-neighbour structure in $\mathcal{C}$, which means a metric on a space that mixes metres with radians, a weighting choice that is arbitrary, consequential, and almost never reported.

§27 Rapidly-exploring random trees

Go and read

LaValle & Kuffner (2001), Randomized kinodynamic planning. RRTs, including the Voronoi-bias argument for why the tree explores without being told to. The kinodynamic half is the part that matters for anything with a body, and it is the half usually skipped.

Grow toward the parts you have not been

Start a tree at the initial configuration. Repeat: sample a random configuration $q$; find the nearest node in the tree; take a step of fixed size from it toward $q$; add the new node if the step is collision free.

The bias is the elegant part and it is not imposed, it falls out. A node is extended in proportion to the volume of its Voronoi region, so the tree pulls hardest toward large unexplored areas. Nobody has to tell it where the frontier is.

Single query, and why that is usually what you want

An RRT does not build a reusable structure; it grows one tree toward one goal and throws it away. That is the right shape when the environment changes between queries, which is the case for anything that moves through the world rather than sitting in a cell. Bidirectional RRT (a tree from each end, growing toward each other) is the standard implementation and typically the fastest thing available.

The paths are ugly. Random extension produces jagged, wandering routes that no vehicle should follow and no arm should execute, so every real system post-processes: shortcut smoothing repeatedly tries to replace two segments with one and keeps the replacement if it is collision free. Cheap, effective, and universally present in the code and absent from the paper.

§28 Optimality, dynamics, and the real stack

Go and read

Karaman & Frazzoli (2011), Sampling-based algorithms for optimal motion planning. Proves that RRT converges to a suboptimal path with probability one, then fixes it. The shrinking connection radius and its $1/d$ exponent are where the dimension reappears after sampling seemed to have removed it.

Three things the basic algorithm does not have

Optimality. RRT converges to a path, not a good one, and more samples do not improve the path it already has. RRT* fixes this with two additions: connect a new node to the cheapest nearby parent rather than the nearest, and then rewire nearby nodes to go through it if that is cheaper. The result is asymptotically optimal (the cost converges to the true optimum) at the price of a rewiring step whose radius must shrink like $(\log n / n)^{1/d}$ for the guarantee to hold.

Dynamics. A car cannot move sideways, so a straight segment in $\mathcal{C}$ is not a feasible motion. Kinodynamic planning replaces the straight-line connector with a short trajectory generated by integrating the actual dynamics under some control, which makes the steering problem (get exactly from state A to state B) hard, and is why practical systems use a fixed library of motion primitives instead of solving it.

Time. Moving obstacles make the free space a function of time, so the state must include it, and the plan must be recomputed continuously rather than once.

How this composes with Part XXXI

A deployed motion stack is three layers with different clocks, and confusing them is the standard architectural mistake.

Route: a graph search over the road network, seconds to minutes of horizon, replanned rarely. Behaviour and trajectory: the search in this movement, a few seconds of horizon, replanned at 10 Hz against the current world model, and always producing a trajectory rather than a path, because the timing is part of the answer. Control: the feedback loop of Part XXXI, 100 Hz or faster, tracking the trajectory and rejecting the disturbances the planner never modelled.

The planner is allowed to be slow and approximate because the controller is fast and stabilising; the controller is allowed to be simple because the planner has already respected the constraints. A learned end-to-end policy collapses all three into one clock, which is exactly the trade being made and is worth naming as a trade rather than an upgrade. It is also why the interfaces here are where the safety argument lives: a trajectory with a feasibility guarantee and a fallback is checkable in a way that a single network output is not.
