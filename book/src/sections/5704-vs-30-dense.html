Part CDense prediction

One output per pixel, so the matching problem of movement A disappears and two others replace it: a network that downsamples cannot produce a full-resolution answer without help, and a per-pixel average hides exactly the errors that matter.

§17 Semantic segmentation

Classification at every pixel

Predict a class per pixel. The loss is cross-entropy summed over pixels, the output shape is fixed, and there is nothing to match, which makes it the easiest of these tasks to set up and one of the harder ones to evaluate honestly.

The standard metric is mean intersection over union, computed per class and averaged over classes rather than over pixels. That choice matters: averaging over pixels would let a class covering 40% of every image drown out one covering 0.1%, and in a driving scene the second class is the pedestrian.

§18 The resolution problem

Three ways to get the resolution back, and what each costs

A backbone downsamples by 32. A segmentation needs the original resolution. Something has to bridge that.

Learned upsampling. Transposed convolution, which produces the checkerboard artefact of Part X §27 whenever the kernel size is not a multiple of the stride, a defect with an exact arithmetic cause and a one-line fix.

Skip connections. Carry the shallow, high-resolution features across to the decoder. The U-shape that dominates medical and satellite work, and the reason boundaries survive at all.

Dilated convolution. Enlarge the receptive field without downsampling, so resolution is never lost. Expensive in memory, since every layer runs at full resolution, and it makes the gridding artefact of Part X §29 possible instead.

§19 Instance and panoptic

Three questions that sound like one

Semantic: which class is this pixel. Two adjacent cars are one region of "car".

Instance: which object is this pixel. Two adjacent cars are two regions, and pixels belonging to no object have no answer.

Panoptic: both, with every pixel assigned exactly once: countable things get identities, uncountable stuff gets classes.

The distinction is not pedantry. A planner needs to know that there are two cars rather than a large car-shaped region, and a semantic model cannot tell it.

Left out on purpose: occupancy grids (a per-cell occupied/free probability over a voxel or bird's-eye-view grid, used where the set of things worth flagging is open-ended rather than a fixed class list) are named in §2 among the dense outputs and not developed as their own topic here. The representation is close enough to this section's per-pixel classification that little would be new; what is genuinely different is fusing the grid over time and across sensors, which belongs with tracking (movement E) rather than with a single frame.

§20 Depth from one image

Pitfall: monocular depth is scale-ambiguous, and the ambiguity is exact

A single projection cannot determine absolute scale: a scene twice as large at twice the distance produces an identical image. Any absolute depth a monocular network reports comes from the training data's statistics (typical object sizes, typical camera height), not from the image.

Which is why monocular depth is usually evaluated after fitting a scale factor per image, and why a number reported that way says nothing about whether the system knows how far away anything is. Move the camera to a different height and the scale prior is wrong in a way no amount of data from the original rig will reveal.

§21 Optical flow

Go and read

Lucas & Kanade (1981), An iterative image registration technique. Where the brightness-constancy formulation and the local-constancy assumption come from. The aperture problem is visible in the algebra on the second page, forty years before anyone measured it with a network.

A motion vector per pixel

The classical assumption is that brightness is constant along a pixel's motion, which linearises to

$$I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0$$

One equation, two unknowns, per pixel. So flow is underdetermined pointwise and every method is a way of adding the missing constraint: assume the flow is locally constant, or penalise its gradient, or learn a prior from data. The next section is what happens when the assumption does not supply enough.

§22 The aperture problem

Why this is worth more than its size suggests

It is a rare thing in this book and the second instance of it: a limit that is exact and provable rather than a measured tendency, like Part XXVI's Weisfeiler–Leman bound. No network, however large, recovers the tangential motion of a featureless straight edge from a local window, because the information is not there.

What a network can do is supply a prior: the rest of the object moves this way, so this edge probably does too. That is a real and legitimate answer, and it is inference from context rather than measurement, which is exactly the distinction that matters when the context is unusual.

The same eigenvalue that decides this decides where a corner detector fires. Good features to track and solvable flow are the same condition, seen from either side.

§23 What dense metrics hide

Four

Boundaries. Most pixels are interior and easy; the errors are on edges, and a per-pixel average buries them. Boundary-specific metrics exist and are rarely reported.

Rare classes. Averaging over classes helps and does not fix a class that appears in one image in a thousand.

Range. Depth error is usually reported as a single figure over the whole image, and §29 shows the error at 100 m is a different quantity from the error at 10 m.

Consistency between tasks. A system predicting depth and segmentation separately can report a pedestrian-shaped region at a depth belonging to the building behind it, and neither metric notices.
