Part EFunctions that keep appearing

Two small functions turn up so often that a reference gives them their own section. Both solve the same problem: a neural network produces arbitrary real numbers, but a probability has to live in a specific range and sum to one.

§19 Sigmoid and softplus

The logistic sigmoid

$$\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$$

It maps the entire real line into $(0,1)$, smoothly and monotonically. Feed it $-\infty$ and you get 0; feed it $+\infty$ and you get 1; feed it 0 and you get exactly $0.5$.

Which raises a question most treatments skip. Plenty of functions squash the real line into $(0,1)$: you could rescale an arctangent, or use $x/(1+|x|)$ shifted and scaled, or invent a dozen others. Why this one, with its slightly ungainly formula? It is not convention and it is not aesthetics. The sigmoid is forced, and Part 0 already derived the thing that forces it without saying so.

Where the sigmoid comes from

Start from the problem rather than the answer. A network's final layer produces an unbounded real number, and you need a probability. Instead of asking "what function squashes", ask the better question: what unbounded quantity is a probability naturally equivalent to? If such a thing exists, the network should be producing that, and converting back should be a translation rather than a squashing.

It exists. Take a probability $p$ and form the odds, $p/(1-p)$, the familiar gambling ratio, three-to-one and so on. Odds run from 0 to $\infty$ as $p$ runs from 0 to 1, which removes the upper bound but leaves a floor at zero and, worse, is wildly asymmetric: $p = 0.9$ gives odds of 9, while the equally lopsided $p = 0.1$ gives $0.111$. Now take the log:

$$\text{logit}(p) = \log\frac{p}{1-p}$$

The floor is gone and the asymmetry with it. This quantity (the log-odds, or logit) runs over the entire real line, is 0 at $p = 0.5$, and is symmetric: $p = 0.9$ gives $+2.197$ and $p = 0.1$ gives $-2.197$. It is the natural unbounded coordinate for a probability.

And the sigmoid is exactly its inverse. Part 0 §5 solved $y = 1/(1+e^{-x})$ for $x$ and arrived at $\log\!\big(y/(1-y)\big)$, the same expression, obtained as a routine algebra exercise long before probability had been mentioned. Undoing that step is what $\sigma$ does.

Intuition: a network predicts evidence, not probability

This is why the raw output of a classifier is called a logit. The name is not decoration; it says what the number is.

Read the network as accumulating evidence. Each layer contributes an amount for or against; the contributions add, because that is what a linear layer does. Adding is the right operation in log-odds space; this is precisely Turing's weight of evidence from §21, measured in bans, which accumulated by addition as each new intercept arrived. Evidence does not add in probability space, where you are penned into $[0,1]$ and would keep running into the walls.

So the network works in the coordinate where its natural operation is correct, and the sigmoid converts back at the very end. A logit of 0 is no evidence either way, giving $p = 0.5$. A logit of $+2.2$ is odds of about 9 to 1, giving $p = 0.9$. Every logit you ever print is a quantity of evidence.

That framing also explains the flatness in Figure 12 that the caption flags as a problem. Moving from $p = 0.99$ to $p = 0.999$ requires the logit to travel from about $4.6$ to about $6.9$, a long way in evidence for a small way in probability. The function is flat out there because the probability scale is running out of room while the evidence scale is not, and a flat function is one with a vanishing derivative.

Two identities you will need constantly:

$$\sigma(-x) = 1 - \sigma(x), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\sigma(x) = \sigma(x)\big(1 - \sigma(x)\big)$$

The derivative is remarkable; it is expressible entirely in terms of the function's own output. During backpropagation you have already computed $\sigma(x)$ on the forward pass, so the gradient costs one multiplication and no new evaluation. That efficiency is part of why the sigmoid dominated early neural networks.

Why deep learning cares, and why it fell out of favour

The sigmoid is exactly the function that converts a network's unbounded output into a Bernoulli parameter, which is why it terminates every binary classifier.

But look again at that derivative. It is largest at $x = 0$, where it equals $0.25$, and it collapses toward zero at both extremes. So when a unit is confidently saturated, its gradient nearly vanishes and it learns almost nothing, the vanishing gradient problem. Stack many sigmoid layers and gradients get multiplied by numbers below $0.25$ repeatedly, decaying exponentially with depth. This is the single biggest reason ReLU replaced the sigmoid in hidden layers, and it is a central story in Part VII §18 and Part IX §10.

Softplus

$$\zeta(x) = \log\big(1 + e^{x}\big)$$

A smooth approximation to $\max(0, x)$, always strictly positive. Its main use is producing parameters that are required to be positive: a variance, for instance, must never be negative, so a network predicts an unconstrained number and passes it through softplus.

The two functions are closely related: the derivative of softplus is exactly the sigmoid.

Pitfall: softplus overflows to a wrong answer; sigmoid only warns

The two naive formulas fail differently, and it matters which. For large positive $x$, np.exp(x) genuinely overflows (around $x = 710$ in float64), so np.log(1+np.exp(x)) comes back inf: a corrupted value that will turn the next gradient into nan and quietly poison a training run.

The sigmoid is milder than it looks. For very negative $x$ (again around $x = -710$), np.exp(-x) overflows and NumPy raises a RuntimeWarning, but 1/(1+np.exp(-x)) still evaluates to $1/\infty = 0.0$, which is the correct limit of the sigmoid, not a corrupted one. The warning is worth silencing (it will drown out warnings that do matter), but the number underneath it is right.

The fixes are standard regardless: use scipy.special.expit for the sigmoid, which suppresses the spurious warning without changing the answer, and compute softplus as np.log1p(np.exp(-abs(x))) + np.maximum(x, 0), which is algebraically identical but never exponentiates a large positive number, so it never produces the genuine inf. This is the same defensive habit as the stable softmax in Part I's drill W6, and it is why frameworks provide fused implementations rather than letting you compose these yourself.

§20 Softmax

The multi-class counterpart. Given a vector $\mathbf{z}$ of $k$ arbitrary real numbers, usually called logits, softmax produces a valid categorical distribution:

$$\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} e^{z_j}}$$

Two steps, each doing one job. Exponentiate, which forces everything positive, since $e^{z}$ is positive for every real $z$. Then divide by the total, which forces the entries to sum to 1. The output is a legitimate probability vector by construction.

The same question arises as for the sigmoid, and it has the same answer. Squaring would also force everything positive, and so would taking absolute values, and both are cheaper. The exponential is not chosen for its range.

It is chosen because the logits are log-probabilities, and the exponential is what undoes a logarithm. That is the entire content of the operation: the network reports each class's score on a log scale, where evidence adds, and softmax converts those scores back to probabilities and renormalises because the network's scores carry no commitment to summing to anything.

You can watch it work. Take genuine probabilities $(0.2, 0.3, 0.5)$, take their logs to get $(-1.609, -1.204, -0.693)$, and push those through softmax. You get $(0.2, 0.3, 0.5)$ back, exactly. Softmax is not imposing a shape on arbitrary numbers; it is inverting a log, and it returns the original distribution untouched when the input really was a set of log-probabilities.

Squaring would have no such property. It would produce a valid probability vector and a meaningless one, because nothing about the network's training would make the squares mean anything. The exponential is the only choice under which the additive structure of the layers before it corresponds to multiplying probabilities, which, by §8, is what combining independent evidence actually does.

Intuition: softmax is the sigmoid, generalised

The two functions are the same function. With exactly two classes, divide numerator and denominator by $e^{z_1}$:

$$\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_1 = \frac{e^{z_1}}{e^{z_1} + e^{z_2}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(z_1 - z_2)}} = \sigma(z_1 - z_2)$$

Softmax over two classes is a sigmoid applied to the difference of the logits, and that difference is the log-odds from §19, since it is the log of one class's score divided by the other's. Two sections, one function.

This also explains a practical fact: a binary classifier can be built either with one output and a sigmoid, or with two outputs and a softmax, and the two are equivalent. The two-output version simply carries a redundant parameter, which is the non-identifiability noted below.

Intuition: "soft" argmax, not "soft" max

The name is a historical misnomer worth untangling. Softmax is a smoothed version of argmax, not of max. A hard argmax would return $(0,0,1,0)$: all the weight on the winner. Softmax returns something like $(0.02, 0.05, 0.88, 0.05)$: the winner dominates but the others keep a share.

The exponential is what makes the winner dominate. Because $e^z$ grows so fast, modest differences in logits become large differences in probability. Logits $(1, 2, 3)$ give roughly $(0.09, 0.24, 0.67)$: a gap of 2 in the logits became a factor of 7 in probability.

The shift-invariance property

Softmax is unchanged by adding any constant to every logit:

$$\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z} + c)_i = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_i$$

The factor $e^c$ appears in both numerator and denominator and cancels exactly. Two consequences follow, and both matter.

It is the basis of the numerically stable implementation. Subtract the maximum logit before exponentiating and the largest exponent becomes $e^0 = 1$, so nothing can overflow, while the result is provably identical.

def softmax(z):
    """Stable softmax. The shift changes nothing mathematically."""
    z = z - np.max(z)            # now max(z) == 0, so exp() is safe
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum()

# naive version dies here; this one does not
print(softmax(np.array([1000., 1001., 1002.])))
### [0.09003057 0.24472847 0.66524096]

It means the logits are not unique. A network can add 5 to every logit and produce identical predictions, so the parameters are only determined up to that shift. This is a mild non-identifiability that occasionally surfaces when comparing models.

Pitfall: do not chain softmax into a "softmax loss"

Frameworks provide functions with names like softmax_cross_entropy_with_logits, and they expect raw logits, not softmax output. Applying softmax yourself and then passing the result is a genuine bug: it applies the operation twice, flattening your distribution toward uniform.

The fused versions exist because computing $\log(\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}))$ directly is far more numerically stable than taking a log of a softmax that may contain values rounded to exactly zero, and $\log 0$ is $-\infty$. Section 25 shows why the log and the softmax belong together.

Try it

Compute $\text{softmax}((0, 0, 0))$ and $\text{softmax}((0, 0, 10))$ without a calculator, approximately is fine. What does the comparison tell you?

Answer. For $(0,0,0)$, all three exponentials equal 1, so the result is $(1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$: equal logits mean maximum uncertainty. For $(0,0,10)$, the third exponential is $e^{10} \approx 22{,}026$ against two 1s, so the total is about 22,028 and the result is roughly $(0.00005, 0.00005, 0.9999)$.

A logit gap of 10 produces near-certainty. That sensitivity is why models can become badly overconfident, and why calibration (making a model's stated 90% confidence correspond to actually being right 90% of the time) is a real research problem rather than an automatic property.
