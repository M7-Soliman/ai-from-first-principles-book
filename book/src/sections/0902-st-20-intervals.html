Part BSampling distributions

§6 The distribution of a statistic

In 60 seconds

Any quantity computed from a sample has its own distribution across possible samples: the sampling distribution. For the mean, the central limit theorem says it is approximately Gaussian, centred on the truth, with spread $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. That single result underwrites almost every interval and test in this part.

Section 1 introduced the object; here is the one result that makes it tractable. From Part II §15, sums of independent quantities tend toward a Gaussian regardless of what they started as. A sample mean is a sum divided by $n$. Therefore:

The sampling distribution of the mean

For $n$ independent observations with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, the sample mean is approximately $$\bar{x} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$$ for $n$ large enough, and "large enough" is often smaller than people expect.

What makes this powerful is the phrase regardless of what they started as. Your data can be skewed, bounded, discrete, or bimodal; the distribution of its mean is still approximately Gaussian. You get to reason about your estimate with a known distribution without knowing the distribution of the data at all.

§7 When $\sigma$ is unknown

In 60 seconds

When $\sigma$ is unknown you estimate it from the same data, which adds a second source of randomness: occasionally you underestimate $s$ and divide by something too small. The result is no longer Gaussian; it has heavier tails. The correct distribution is Student's $t$, derived by Gosset in 1908. It approaches the Gaussian as $n$ grows, and past roughly $n = 50$ the distinction stops mattering.

Go and read

Student [Gosset] (1908), The probable error of a mean. The $t$-distribution, published under a pseudonym because Gosset's employer treated statistical method as a trade secret; the history box below has the rest. Read it for the motivation: he needed it because brewing experiments came in samples of four, and the normal approximation was lying to him.

There is a hole in §6. The formula needs $\sigma$, the true population standard deviation, which you do not have; you have $s$, an estimate of it from the same data.

Substituting $s$ for $\sigma$ adds a second source of randomness, and the result is no longer Gaussian. It has heavier tails, because occasionally you underestimate $s$ and divide by something too small. The correct distribution is Student's $t$, and it approaches the Gaussian as $n$ grows.

History: published under a pseudonym because of beer

William Sealy Gosset was a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, where his job involved judging barley and yeast from small experimental batches. Small samples were unavoidable (you cannot run a thousand trial brews), and he found the standard methods gave answers that were too confident.

He worked out the correct distribution and published it in 1908. Guinness prohibited employees from publishing, having previously had trade secrets leak, so he wrote under the pen name Student.

The distribution has carried that pseudonym ever since. It is a rare case of a result named after nobody at all, and it exists because industrial statistics, unlike the astronomy that motivated so much of Part I and Part II, could not simply collect more data.

Part CIntervals

§8 Confidence intervals

In 60 seconds

A point estimate alone is close to useless. A confidence interval reports a range instead, constructed so that the procedure captures the truth a specified fraction of the time. For a mean the recipe is estimate $\pm\ 1.96 \times$ standard error, and the 1.96 comes straight from the Gaussian in §6.

Definition: confidence interval

A 95% confidence interval is an interval computed from data by a procedure which, across repeated samples, contains the true value 95% of the time.

Read that definition carefully, because the guarantee attaches to the procedure, not to any particular interval you have in your hands. Section 9 is entirely about what goes wrong when people forget this.

The construction for a mean:

$$\bar{x} \;\pm\; 1.96 \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

and the left panel of Figure 4 is the definition made literal: a hundred intervals from a hundred samples, with the ones that missed drawn in pink. Roughly five of them miss, which is what 95% means.

One clause is owed to §7: this is the large-$n$ form. It uses the Gaussian quantile 1.96 where $s$ standing in for $\sigma$ technically calls for Student's $t$'s wider one, which is a fine approximation past roughly $n=50$ (true of the large test sets typical in machine learning) but not below it, where Figure 4's right panel shows this exact formula under-covering (88% instead of 95% at $n=5$). At small $n$, replace 1.96 with the $t$-multiplier from §7.

§9 What a confidence interval does not mean

Pitfall: the interpretation almost everyone gets wrong

Given a 95% interval of $[0.91, 0.97]$, the tempting reading is: there is a 95% probability the true value lies in this interval.

Under the frequentist framework that statement is not merely imprecise, it is meaningless. The true value is a fixed number. It is either in $[0.91, 0.97]$ or it is not: the probability is 0 or 1, and you do not know which. Nothing is random about the interval once you have computed it; the randomness was in the sampling that produced it.

The correct statement is about the method: intervals constructed this way contain the truth 95% of the time. This one has either succeeded or failed, and you will never find out which.

If that feels like an unsatisfying answer to the question you actually asked, your instinct is sound. The statement people want (a probability attached to this specific interval) requires treating the unknown value as a random quantity with a distribution, which is exactly the Bayesian move from Part II §2. Its corresponding object is called a credible interval, and about it you may legitimately say "95% probability the value lies here."

The price is a prior. That is the whole trade, and §17 returns to it.

§10 The bootstrap

Go and read

Efron (1979), Bootstrap methods: another look at the jackknife. The bootstrap, introduced. It is worth reading mostly to register how simple the idea is once someone has said it, and how long it took anyone to say it; the computation it requires was unthinkable before cheap cycles, which is why a technique this obvious is younger than most of this part.

The idea is close to absurd on first hearing, and it works.

Intuition: your sample is your best picture of the population

The obstacle is that the population is unavailable. But your sample was drawn from it, so it is an estimate of it, the empirical distribution of Part II §17.

So: treat your sample as if it were the population, and draw new samples from it. Sampling with replacement gives datasets of the same size that differ in exactly the way real repeated samples would. Compute your statistic on each, and the spread you observe estimates the spread you would have seen with genuine resampling.

def bootstrap_ci(data, stat, n_boot=10000, alpha=0.05):
    """Confidence interval for any statistic, with no formula required."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    n = len(data)
    reps = [stat(rng.choice(data, n, replace=True)) for _ in range(n_boot)]
    return np.percentile(reps, [100*alpha/2, 100*(1-alpha/2)])

# works for the mean, the median, a correlation, an F1 score, a model's accuracy --
# anything you can compute, without deriving its sampling distribution
History: a method that had to wait for computers

Bradley Efron introduced the bootstrap in 1979. The underlying idea is simple enough that it could have been proposed a century earlier, and was not, because it is useless without a machine. Ten thousand resamples by hand is not a method, it is a life's work.

The name is from the expression about pulling yourself up by your own bootstraps, chosen for its air of the impossible: you are extracting information about sampling variability from a single sample, apparently getting something for nothing.

Its arrival marked a real shift. Classical statistics spent a century deriving the sampling distribution of each new statistic analytically, which is hard and often impossible. The bootstrap replaced derivation with computation, the same trade Part II's Monte Carlo methods make against integration, and one the modern field makes constantly.

Why this matters: use it on your model metrics

Almost nobody reports uncertainty on model performance, and the usual reason given is that the sampling distribution of an F1 score or a BLEU score is unknown.

It does not need to be known. Resample your test set with replacement a few thousand times, recompute the metric each time, take percentiles. Ten lines of code, no theory, and you can now say whether your improvement is larger than the noise. Given §14, that is frequently the most informative thing you can report.

Try it

A model gets 94.2% on a test set of 1,000 examples. Roughly what is the standard error, and how wide is a 95% interval?

Answer. Accuracy on $n$ examples is a proportion, whose standard error is $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$. With $p = 0.942$ and $n = 1000$:

$\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.942 \times 0.058 / 1000} = \sqrt{5.46\times10^{-5}} \approx 0.0074$

So the 95% interval is roughly $94.2\% \pm 1.5\%$, or about $[92.7\%,\ 95.7\%]$.

That width is the point. A model reporting 94.2% and one reporting 93.5% on this test set are not distinguishable: the intervals overlap heavily. Reporting the first as an improvement over the second is not supportable, and the calculation that shows it took one line.
