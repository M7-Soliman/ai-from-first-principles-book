§14 Stochastic gradient descent

In 60 seconds

The loss is an average over the whole dataset, so its exact gradient costs a full pass through the data, for one step. Instead, estimate the gradient from a small random sample. The estimate is unbiased but noisy. You trade accuracy per step for enormously more steps per second, and that trade is overwhelmingly worth making.

Go and read

Robbins & Monro (1951), A stochastic approximation method. The ancestor of every optimiser in this book, written for a problem in sequential experimentation with no connection to learning whatever. The step-size conditions it proves convergence under are the reason your learning rate schedule decays; see the history box below, then read the paper and notice how little it needed to assume.

The objective in supervised learning is an average over examples:

$$f(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} L\big(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, y^{(i)}; \boldsymbol{\theta}\big)$$

Its gradient is therefore also an average, over $m$ terms. With $m$ in the millions, computing it exactly to take one step is absurd; you would spend an entire pass over the data to move once.

But an average is exactly the thing that can be estimated by sampling. Draw a minibatch $\mathcal{B}$ of size $b$ and compute

$$\hat{\mathbf{g}} = \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \nabla L\big(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, y^{(i)}; \boldsymbol{\theta}\big)$$

By Part II §10, $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{g}}] = \nabla f$: the estimate is unbiased. It points in the right direction on average, and its cost is independent of dataset size.

Intuition: the accuracy you are buying is wasted

Part II's Monte Carlo result says the standard error of an average falls like $1/\sqrt{b}$. So going from a batch of 100 to a batch of 10,000 costs 100 times the computation and buys a tenfold reduction in gradient noise.

That would be a bad trade even if the extra precision were useful, and it mostly is not. You are about to take a small step and recompute anyway. A roughly correct direction now beats a precisely correct direction a hundred times later, which is why essentially all large-scale training is stochastic.

Why this matters: the noise is not purely a cost

Gradient noise turns out to help in two ways nobody designed for.

It lets the optimiser escape saddle points. Exact gradient descent that lands on a saddle stops there, since the gradient is zero; a noisy step is knocked off and continues downhill. Given §18, this matters a great deal.

And it appears to improve generalisation. Small-batch training reliably finds solutions that perform better on unseen data than large-batch training of the same model, an effect usually attributed to noise preferring wide flat minima over narrow sharp ones. The full explanation is still contested, but the effect is robust enough that batch size is treated as a regularization knob rather than purely a throughput one.

History: invented for a problem that had nothing to do with learning

In 1951 Herbert Robbins and Sutton Monro published a method for a problem in sequential experimentation: find the input at which an unknown function crosses zero, when every measurement of that function is corrupted by noise.

Their insight was that you do not need accurate measurements if you take enough cheap ones and shrink your step sizes appropriately. They proved convergence under conditions on the step sequence: that the steps must sum to infinity so you can still travel any distance, while their squares must sum to something finite so the noise eventually settles. Those two conditions are why learning rate schedules decay, and §17 is their descendant.

The paper never mentions machine learning, which did not meaningfully exist. It is about stochastic approximation as a general technique, and it is the direct ancestor of the optimiser used to train every large model in existence.

§15 Momentum

In 60 seconds

Keep a running velocity rather than stepping along the raw gradient each time. Successive gradients in the same direction accumulate; successive gradients that alternate in sign cancel. The result damps oscillation across narrow valleys and builds speed along the valley floor. At $\beta = 0.9$ the effective step along a consistent direction is $1/(1-\beta) = 10$ times a single gradient step, close to free improvement, which is why momentum is on by default in essentially every modern optimiser.

Go and read

Polyak (1964), Some methods of speeding up the convergence of iteration methods. Momentum, in four pages. Then Nesterov (1983) for the accelerated variant and its $O(1/k^2)$ guarantee, a rate that Part IX §15 will show does not survive contact with stochastic gradients, which is worth knowing before you read the theorem.

Section 9 diagnosed the narrow valley: the gradient points mostly across it, so descent oscillates while creeping along the useful direction. Momentum attacks that oscillation directly.

Momentum

$$\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \beta\mathbf{v} + \nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}), \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \alpha\mathbf{v}$$

Keep a running velocity rather than stepping along the raw gradient. Typically $\beta \approx 0.9$.

Intuition: averaging cancels what alternates

Across the valley, successive gradients point in opposite directions: left, right, left. Accumulating them makes those contributions cancel.

Along the valley, successive gradients point the same way every time. Accumulating them makes those contributions add, building up speed.

So momentum is a filter that suppresses whatever alternates and amplifies whatever persists. The physical picture (a heavy ball rolling down the valley rather than a particle teleporting to wherever the local slope points) is the standard one and is reasonably faithful.

The effective step along a consistent direction is roughly $1/(1-\beta)$ times a single gradient step, so $\beta = 0.9$ multiplies progress along the valley floor by about ten. That factor is why momentum is close to free improvement, and why it is on by default almost everywhere.

That factor is worth deriving rather than accepting, because it is one line and it makes the choice of $\beta$ stop being a mystery. Suppose the gradient is the same vector $\mathbf{g}$ every step, which is the situation along the valley floor. The velocity settles at whatever value reproduces itself:

$$\mathbf{v} = \beta\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{g} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{v}(1-\beta) = \mathbf{g} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{g}}{1-\beta}$$

Equivalently, unrolling the recursion gives $\mathbf{g} + \beta\mathbf{g} + \beta^2\mathbf{g} + \cdots$, a geometric series summing to $\mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)$. At $\beta = 0.9$ that is $10\mathbf{g}$; at $\beta = 0.99$ it is $100\mathbf{g}$.

The same number has a second reading that is more useful in practice. The velocity is a weighted average of past gradients with weights $1, \beta, \beta^2, \dots$, so it is effectively averaging over roughly the last $1/(1-\beta)$ gradients. That is the sentence to carry: $\beta = 0.9$ remembers about ten steps, $\beta = 0.99$ about a hundred. Both facts about $\beta$ (how much it amplifies a persistent direction and how long it remembers) are the same quantity, which is why raising it helps and hurts at the same time.

§16 Adaptive methods

In 60 seconds

Give each parameter its own step size, scaled by the size of the gradients that parameter has been receiving. Parameters with consistently large gradients get small steps; rarely-updated ones get large steps. This is a cheap diagonal stand-in for the curvature rescaling that Newton's method does exactly, at $O(n)$ instead of $O(n^3)$. AdaGrad introduced the idea; RMSProp fixed its fatal flaw (a step size that only ever shrinks); Adam combined per-parameter scaling with momentum and became the default optimiser for deep learning.

Go and read

Duchi, Hazan & Singer (2011), Adaptive subgradient methods. AdaGrad, and the convex analysis that motivated the per-parameter scaling. Then Kingma & Ba (2015) for Adam, which is unusually readable for an optimisation paper; and then Reddi, Kale & Kumar (2018), which finds the error in Adam's proof and builds a convex problem where it fails outright. Read the three in that order. The sequence is a better education in how this field works than any commentary on it, including the history box below.

Three methods in a line of descent, each fixing the previous one's flaw.

AdaGrad

Accumulate the squared gradients seen so far, and divide by the square root:

$$\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{g}^2, \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}} + \epsilon}\,\mathbf{g}$$

All operations elementwise. Excellent for sparse features; a parameter that is rarely active keeps a small $\mathbf{s}$ and so retains a large step. Its flaw is structural: $\mathbf{s}$ only ever grows, so the effective learning rate decays monotonically toward zero and training eventually stalls whether or not it has converged.

Intuition: why the square root, and why squares in the first place

Both choices in that formula look arbitrary and neither is.

Why accumulate squares. You want to know how large this parameter's gradients have been, not which way they pointed. Accumulating $\mathbf{g}$ itself would cancel: a parameter oscillating between $+5$ and $-5$ would register as quiet when it is the noisiest one you have. Squaring discards the sign and keeps the magnitude, exactly as it does for variance in Part II §11.

Why the square root. For RMSProp (just below), $\mathbf{s}$ is a running average of $g^2$, so $\sqrt{\mathbf{s}}$ is a root-mean-square gradient, a quantity in the same units as $\mathbf{g}$. The ratio $\mathbf{g}/\sqrt{\mathbf{s}}$ is therefore unit-free: it says "how many typical gradients is this one", and it lands near $\pm 1$ for every parameter no matter what scale that parameter's gradients live on. That is the whole point: every coordinate is put on a common footing so that a single global $\alpha$ can serve all of them. For AdaGrad, $\mathbf{s}$ is a running sum that only ever grows, so $\sqrt{\mathbf{s}}$ grows like $\sqrt{t}$ and the effective step shrinks toward zero, which is the structural flaw that RMSProp fixes.

Dividing by $\mathbf{s}$ instead would be a genuine bug, not a variant. The update would scale like $g/g^2 = 1/g$, which grows as gradients shrink, so the closer you got to a minimum, the more violently you would step. The square root is what keeps the rescaling a normalisation rather than an inversion.

RMSProp

Replace the running sum with a running average, which forgets:

$$\mathbf{s} \leftarrow \rho\,\mathbf{s} + (1-\rho)\,\mathbf{g}^2$$

Now $\mathbf{s}$ tracks the recent gradient scale rather than the whole history, so the step size can recover when curvature changes. This one fix makes the method work on non-convex problems, where the landscape genuinely differs between regions.

Adam

Combine RMSProp's per-parameter scaling with momentum's velocity, and correct both for the fact that they start at zero:

$$\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \beta_1\mathbf{v} + (1-\beta_1)\mathbf{g}, \qquad \mathbf{s} \leftarrow \beta_2\mathbf{s} + (1-\beta_2)\mathbf{g}^2$$ $$\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{1-\beta_1^t}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{s}} = \frac{\mathbf{s}}{1-\beta_2^t}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \frac{\alpha\,\hat{\mathbf{v}}}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{s}}} + \epsilon}$$

The bias correction matters more than it looks. Both accumulators are initialised to zero, so early on they badly underestimate their targets; dividing by $1 - \beta^t$ compensates exactly, and without it the first several hundred steps are far too small.

History: the proof was wrong, and it did not matter much

Adam was published in 2015 with a convergence proof and became the default optimiser for deep learning within about two years.

In 2018 a paper titled On the Convergence of Adam and Beyond showed the proof contained an error, and (worse) constructed a simple convex problem on which Adam provably fails to converge at all. The paper won a best-paper award at ICLR that year.

What happened next is the interesting part: essentially nothing. Adam remained the default, because the counterexamples were not representative of the problems people actually train on, and no proposed replacement was reliably better in practice. The field's response to "your theoretical justification is invalid" was, in effect, "the method still works."

That is worth sitting with. It is a real and uncomfortable feature of this field: practice frequently runs ahead of theory, sometimes for years, and the empirical evidence is often stronger than the proofs. Part 0 §14 made the same observation about calculus operating for 150 years without rigorous foundations.

Pitfall: adaptive does not mean automatic

Adam still has a learning rate, and it still matters. The adaptivity rescales relative step sizes across parameters; the global scale is yours to choose.

There is also persistent evidence that well-tuned plain SGD with momentum generalises better than Adam on some problems, particularly in vision. Adam converges faster in training loss; that is not the same as ending up at a better solution.

§17 Learning rate schedules

Go and read

Loshchilov & Hutter (2019), Decoupled weight decay regularization. Why Adam(weight_decay=λ) and AdamW(weight_decay=λ) do different things, worked out precisely. The framework box at the end of this section states the conclusion; the paper is where the argument is, and it is short.

Robbins and Monro's conditions from §14 imply that a constant step size cannot converge in the presence of noise; it can only reach a neighbourhood of the optimum and then rattle around inside it, at a radius set by the noise and the step size.

The fix is to shrink the step over time.

ScheduleBehaviour
ConstantSimple; plateaus at a noise floor.
Step decayMultiply by a factor every $k$ epochs. Crude, effective, and visible as steps in the loss curve.
CosineSmoothly anneal to near zero over the run. Common in modern practice.
WarmupStart small and increase for the first few hundred steps. Prevents early instability when gradients are large and adaptive estimates are still unreliable.
In the framework: every symbol in §§14–17 is a keyword argument

The formulas of this part are not implemented by users. They are constructor arguments, and the names map one-to-one onto the mathematics:

import torch

opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1, momentum=0.9)
#                                          alpha        beta   (section 15)

opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3,
                       betas=(0.9, 0.999),   # beta_1 and beta_2 from section 16
                       eps=1e-8)              # the epsilon in the denominator

sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, T_max=n_steps)

Adam's defaults ($\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$) are the values from the original paper and are almost never changed. Read them through §15's memory formula and they say something concrete: the velocity averages over roughly the last $1/(1-0.9) = 10$ gradients and the squared-gradient estimate over the last $1/(1-0.999) = 1000$. The scale estimate is deliberately far more sluggish than the direction estimate, because a per-parameter scale should reflect the terrain rather than the last few steps.

The loop, and the one line that surprises everybody

for x, y in loader:
    opt.zero_grad()            # <-- this one
    loss = F.cross_entropy(model(x), y)
    loss.backward()
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)   # Part I section 15
    opt.step()
    sched.step()

zero_grad() exists because gradients accumulate rather than overwrite. Autograd adds each new gradient into .grad with +=, so without clearing it you are stepping on the sum of every batch so far. The symptom is a loss that diverges after a few dozen steps for no visible reason.

Part VII §21 explains why the += is not a design mistake but a necessity: a value used twice in a computation must receive both contributions. The framework cannot distinguish "this value was reused within one backward pass" from "this is a new backward pass", so it accumulates always and leaves the clearing to you. One line of inconvenience bought by a correctness requirement three parts away.

Note also that the optimiser is an object, not a function. It has to be: momentum and the squared-gradient average are state, carried between steps. That is why opt.step() takes no arguments; everything it needs, including the gradients autograd deposited, it already holds.

Adam versus AdamW, which is not a cosmetic difference

Both take a weight_decay argument and they do different things with it, and this catches experienced people.

Adam(weight_decay=λ) adds $\lambda\mathbf{w}$ to the gradient, which then passes through the $1/\sqrt{\mathbf{s}}$ rescaling like everything else. So the effective penalty on each weight is divided by that weight's recent gradient magnitude; parameters with large gradients get weakly regularized and quiet ones get hammered. That is not $L^2$ regularization, and it is not the Gaussian prior of Part V §18 either.

AdamW(weight_decay=λ) applies the decay directly to the weights, outside the adaptive rescaling, which is what the derivation in Part V §18 actually says to do. It is the reason the variant exists and the reason its default is $0.01$ where Adam's is $0$. If you want weight decay with an adaptive optimiser, use AdamW.

Keras spells all of this the same way: tf.keras.optimizers.Adam(learning_rate=1e-3, beta_1=0.9, beta_2=0.999), and hides the zeroing inside model.fit, which is convenient until you write a custom loop with tf.GradientTape and have to know it was there.

§18 Saddle points and high dimensions

Folklore says optimisation of non-convex functions fails because it gets stuck in bad local minima. In high dimensions this appears to be substantially wrong, and the reason is a counting argument.

A critical point is a minimum only if the Hessian's curvature is positive in every direction. For $n$ parameters that is $n$ eigenvalues that must all be positive. If each sign were independent and equally likely, the probability is $2^{-n}$, which for any realistic $n$ is indistinguishable from zero.

Why this matters: it reframes the whole difficulty

If saddles dominate, then the problem is not escaping bad minima but escaping plateaus. Near a saddle the gradient is small in every direction, so progress stalls even though a descent direction exists.

This explains several things at once. It explains why training loss curves plateau and then resume. It explains why gradient noise helps rather than merely being tolerated; noise knocks the iterate off the saddle. And it explains why Newton's method is actively unsuitable here, as §12 noted: it is attracted to critical points of any kind, and most of them are exactly the ones you want to leave.

Try it

Momentum with $\beta = 0.99$ instead of $0.9$. What happens to the effective step along a consistent direction, and what is the risk?

Answer. The amplification is $1/(1-\beta) = 100$, against 10 at $\beta = 0.9$, a tenfold larger effective step along any persistent direction.

Two risks follow, and it matters which one is real. The velocity now averages over roughly the last hundred gradients, so it responds sluggishly when the right direction changes, and it will overshoot a minimum it arrives at with speed; that risk is real. What is not true is that raising $\beta$ tightens the deterministic stability limit from §9. Working through the heavy-ball recursion $\mathbf{v}\leftarrow\beta\mathbf{v}+\nabla f$, $\boldsymbol{\theta}\leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta}-\alpha\mathbf{v}$ on the same quadratic gives a divergence threshold of $\alpha < 2(1+\beta)/\lambda_{\max}$, looser as $\beta$ grows, from $2/\lambda_{\max}$ at $\beta=0$ toward $4/\lambda_{\max}$ as $\beta\to1$. A learning rate that is stable at $\beta=0.9$ ($\alpha<3.8/\lambda_{\max}$) is still stable at $\beta=0.99$ ($\alpha<3.98/\lambda_{\max}$) on this deterministic problem; it does not "diverge outright." The runs that do blow up at high momentum are explained by something this deterministic analysis does not model: a stochastic gradient's noise is amplified by the same $1/(1-\beta)$ factor that amplifies its signal, and a large, badly-timed overshoot compounds across the hundred-gradient memory window before it damps out. High momentum and high learning rate is still a common and puzzling way to blow up a training run: the mechanism is noise amplification and transient overshoot, not a tighter deterministic limit.
