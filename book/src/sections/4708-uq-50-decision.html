Part EFrom a probability to a decision

A calibrated probability is not an action. Turning one into the other requires a statement about what outcomes are worth, and that statement is not in the data, which is why a perfectly calibrated model can still make expensive mistakes, and why the threshold is the part nobody documents.

§32 Utility, and why the loss is not it

Expected utility

Given a predictive distribution $p(y \mid x)$ and a utility $u(a, y)$ for taking action $a$ when the outcome is $y$, choose

$$a^{*} = \arg\max_{a} \; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p(y \mid x)}\big[u(a, y)\big]$$

The separation this enforces is the useful part. The model supplies $p(y \mid x)$; the utility is supplied by whoever bears the consequences. Training loss is neither: it is a surrogate chosen for optimisation, and Part IX §3 said so.

Why conflating them is the most common error in applied work

A model trained with cross-entropy and thresholded at 0.5 has silently asserted that a false positive and a false negative cost the same. Nobody wrote that down and it is almost never true.

The fix costs nothing once the probability is calibrated: change the threshold. What it requires is someone stating the ratio, which is a conversation rather than a computation, and the reason it does not happen is that the default never announces itself as a choice.

§33 The optimal threshold

Where it belongs, exactly

With cost $c_{\text{FP}}$ for a false alarm and $c_{\text{FN}}$ for a miss, acting is worth it when the expected cost of acting is lower:

$$p \cdot 0 + (1-p)\,c_{\text{FP}} \;<\; p \cdot c_{\text{FN}} + (1-p)\cdot 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p \;>\; \frac{c_{\text{FP}}}{c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}}}$$

The threshold depends only on the ratio of costs, never on the base rate or the model. Equal costs give 0.5, and that is the only circumstance in which 0.5 is correct.

§34 What the default costs

Try it on something you run

Find the threshold in a system you operate. It is probably 0.5, and probably nobody chose it. Ask what a false positive costs and what a false negative costs, in whatever units the business actually uses, and compute the ratio. If it is not 1:1, you have found a free improvement, and Figure 6 says how large.

§35 Risk aversion

When the expectation is the wrong summary

Maximising expected utility is correct when you face the decision many times and the losses add up. It is not obviously correct for a single decision with an irreversible outcome, and it is not what people do.

The standard repair is a concave utility on wealth, which makes the same expectation calculus produce risk-averse behaviour without changing the framework. The alternatives (conditional value-at-risk, worst-case objectives) optimise a tail statistic instead of a mean, and connect directly to Part XXIII §42's distributionally robust objectives and Part XXI's treatment of rare harms.

The point worth carrying: which functional of the predictive distribution you optimise is a choice, and reporting only a mean prediction has made it implicitly.

§36 The value of information

What a measurement is worth, before taking it

The expected value of information is the gain in expected utility from observing something before deciding:

$$\mathrm{EVI} = \mathbb{E}_{z}\Big[\max_a \mathbb{E}_{y \mid z}\big[u(a,y)\big]\Big] - \max_a \mathbb{E}_{y}\big[u(a,y)\big]$$

It is never negative, and it is zero whenever the observation would not change the decision, which is the useful part. A test that is highly informative about the outcome but never flips the action is worth nothing, and a cheap test that occasionally flips it can be worth a great deal.

This is the principled version of "is this feature worth collecting", and it explains why feature importance is the wrong instrument for that question: importance measures effect on the prediction, not effect on the decision.

§37 Abstention as an action

Refusing, priced

Add a third action with a fixed cost $c_{\text{abstain}}$: the cost of escalating to a human, or of declining. Then abstain exactly when both other actions have higher expected cost, which carves a band of probabilities around the threshold.

Part XIV §4 introduced coverage as a diagnostic. This makes it a decision with a price, and §28's prediction sets make it operational: refuse when the set is larger than you can act on.

Note what this needs. A refusal policy built on uncalibrated probabilities refuses the wrong cases, and one built on a point estimate refuses none of the cases where the model is confidently wrong off-distribution: Figure 3's 0.790 arriving as a business outcome.

§38 What movement E establishes

Three things

The probability and the utility come from different places. The model supplies one and cannot supply the other, and 0.5 is a claim about costs rather than a neutral default.

A perfectly calibrated model still loses money at the wrong threshold: 4.7× at a 50:1 cost ratio in Figure 6, with nothing wrong with any probability it produced.

Information is worth what it changes. The value of an observation is zero if it never flips the decision, however predictive it is, which is a different question from the one every feature-importance method answers.
