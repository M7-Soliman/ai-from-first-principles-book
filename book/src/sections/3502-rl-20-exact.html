Part BSolving it exactly

When the model is known and the state space is small, the problem is solved: completely, with a convergence rate you can compute in advance. It is worth doing this case properly, because every approximate method later is a sampled version of one of these.

§9 Policy evaluation

Two ways, and they are not equivalent in cost

Directly. §5's equation is linear: $(I - \gamma P_\pi)V = r_\pi$, so $V^\pi = (I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1} r_\pi$. Exact, and $O(|\mathcal{S}|^3)$.

Iteratively. Apply the Bellman operator repeatedly. Each sweep is $O(|\mathcal{S}|^2)$ and the error falls geometrically, so a few dozen sweeps beat a matrix inverse as soon as the state space is at all large.

The matrix inverse exists because $\gamma < 1$ guarantees $I - \gamma P_\pi$ is invertible. That is the same fact that makes the iteration converge, seen from the other side.

§10 Policy iteration

Evaluate, improve, repeat

Evaluate $\pi$ to get $V^\pi$. Then improve: $\pi'(s) = \arg\max_a [r(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} p(s'\mid s,a)V^\pi(s')]$. Repeat.

The policy improvement theorem guarantees $V^{\pi'} \geq V^\pi$ at every state, with strict improvement somewhere unless $\pi$ is already optimal. So the sequence of policies improves monotonically, the number of deterministic policies is finite, and the algorithm therefore terminates at the optimum exactly, usually in a handful of iterations.

§11 Value iteration

Do not wait for evaluation to finish

$$V_{k+1}(s) = \max_a \Big[r(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} p(s'\mid s,a) V_k(s')\Big]$$

One sweep, no inner loop. This is policy iteration with evaluation truncated to a single step, and it converges to $V^*$ from any starting point, including a deliberately silly one.

The two are ends of a spectrum. Evaluate to convergence and it is policy iteration; evaluate once and it is value iteration; anything between is modified policy iteration, and in practice a few evaluation sweeps per improvement is faster than either extreme.

§12 Why it converges, and how fast

The contraction argument, in three lines

Let $T$ be the Bellman optimality operator. For any $U, V$,

$$\|TU - TV\|_\infty \leq \gamma \|U - V\|_\infty$$

because a max of sums differs by at most the largest difference of the terms, and every term carries a factor $\gamma$. So $T$ is a contraction in the max norm with modulus $\gamma$, and Banach's fixed-point theorem gives the rest: a unique fixed point, convergence from anywhere, at a geometric rate.

The rate is what makes this practical rather than merely reassuring. To reach accuracy $\epsilon$ takes about $\log \epsilon / \log \gamma$ sweeps: 132 at $\gamma = 0.9$ and 1,375 at $\gamma = 0.99$ for six decimal places. Long horizons are expensive in a way you can compute before starting.

This is also the deepest reason $\gamma$ appears at all. It is not primarily a preference for the present; it is the parameter that makes the whole apparatus a contraction, and everything downstream inherits its guarantee.

§13 Why exact methods do not scale

Three walls, and they arrive together

The state space. A sweep touches every state. Backgammon has around $10^{20}$ positions; a robot with twelve continuous joints has none you can enumerate at all. Part VI §21's curse of dimensionality, arriving where it is fatal rather than inconvenient.

The model. Every method here needs $p(s'\mid s,a)$ in closed form. For most problems worth solving, nobody has it.

The horizon. Even with both, §12's rate says a long horizon costs sweeps in proportion to $1/(1-\gamma)$.

Movement C removes the first two by sampling instead of sweeping and by learning from transitions instead of from a model. Everything it gains it pays for in the guarantees this movement had.

§14 What survives

Three things to carry into the approximate case

The Bellman equation is the target. Every method in movement C is a way of solving it from samples. When one of them misbehaves, the question is always which term is being estimated badly.

The contraction is the reason anything converges, and it is exactly what function approximation breaks (§21). Knowing that the guarantee came from $\gamma$-contraction in the max norm tells you why a method minimising squared error in a different norm has no such guarantee.

Generalised policy iteration is the shape of every algorithm here. Something estimates values, something improves the policy against them, and the two chase each other. Q-learning, actor–critic and PPO are all this pattern with different approximations in the two boxes.
