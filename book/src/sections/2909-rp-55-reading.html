Part EReading a representation

A representation is a large array of numbers with no labels on the axes. This movement is about finding out what it holds, and about how much of the standard method establishes less than it appears to.

§31 Probing

The method

To ask whether a representation encodes some property, train a small classifier (a probe) to predict that property from the frozen representation, and report its accuracy on held-out data.

It is cheap, it applies to any representation, and it has been used to establish that language models encode part of speech, syntactic structure, and factual attributes, and that vision models encode object position and pose. The practice is standard and the interpretation of its output is where the difficulty lives.

Why "linear" is doing the real work

Restricting the probe to a linear model is not a convenience. It changes the claim.

A linear probe succeeding means the property is linearly available: readable by the kind of model that sits on top of a representation in practice, at the cost of one matrix multiply. That is a statement about usability, and it is the statement worth making.

A powerful probe succeeding means only that the property is recoverable somehow, which is a much weaker claim and, as §32 measures, often not a claim about the representation at all.

§32 What a probe establishes

Go and read

Hewitt & Liang (2019), Designing and interpreting probes with control tasks. The paper behind this section's figure, and the one that should have changed probing practice more than it did. Control tasks, selectivity, and the demonstration that a sufficiently powerful probe learns whatever labels you hand it, which makes its accuracy a statement about the probe.

Control tasks and selectivity

A control task assigns each example identity a fixed random label, consistent between train and test. The mapping is perfectly learnable (it is a deterministic function of the input) and it means nothing whatever.

Selectivity is accuracy on the real task minus accuracy on the control. A probe that scores well on both was memorising identities, not reading a property. A probe that scores well on the real task and badly on the control has demonstrated that the representation makes the property specifically available.

Pitfall: probe accuracy is not evidence

The figure is the argument. An MLP probe reports 0.961 accuracy for a property, which under the usual convention would be written up as "the representation encodes it strongly". The same probe reports 0.952 for random noise, so the 0.961 tells you about the probe.

Notice which arrangement survives. The contrastive representation is selective under a linear probe (+0.605) and much less so under an MLP (+0.076), because a strong enough probe starts recovering identities again. So selectivity is a property of the pair, the representation and the probe, and reporting it without saying which probe was used is meaningless.

The minimum honest report for any probing result: the probe family, its capacity, its regularization, the control-task accuracy, and the selectivity. Accuracy alone belongs in the same category as Part XIV §19's single-seed comparison.

Why the contrastive encoder is the selective one

Worth following, because it connects the two halves of this part. The control task requires identifying which example this is. The raw input supports that completely, so the control is as learnable as the real task and selectivity vanishes.

The contrastive encoder was trained to be invariant to the nuisance factor (§19), so it has discarded the information the control task needed. That is precisely why a probe on it means something.

The general form: a representation is interpretable to the extent that it has thrown things away. A representation that retains everything supports every probe equally and licenses no conclusion at all.

§33 Superposition

Go and read

Elhage et al. (2022), Toy models of superposition. The model this section's figure reproduces. Its contribution is explanatory: polysemantic neurons stop being an anomaly once you accept that a layer stores more features than it has dimensions. Unusually clear about what a toy setting does and does not establish. Then Bricken et al. (2023) for the same idea on a real model, whose limitations section is the part most often dropped when it is cited.

Probing asks whether a named property is present. The harder question is what a representation holds that you did not think to name, and the answer starts with a counting problem.

The phenomenon

Superposition is a layer representing more features than it has dimensions, by assigning them directions that are not orthogonal. The price is interference: reading one feature picks up a contribution from the others.

The condition that makes the trade worthwhile is sparsity. If any given feature is present in only 1% of inputs, then two features sharing a direction collide rarely, and the occasional corrupted reading costs less than dropping a feature entirely.

Why this explains something everyone has noticed

Individual neurons in trained networks are frequently polysemantic: one unit responds to cat faces, car fronts and a particular texture, with no relationship among them. This has been observed since people started looking and it has no good explanation under the assumption that features are neurons.

Superposition explains it. If the network is storing more features than it has neurons, then features must share, and a neuron is a projection of several rather than one thing. The polysemanticity is not noise or a failure to converge; it is the encoding scheme.

Which means the basis you are handed, the neuron basis, is the wrong one, and the meaningful directions are somewhere else in the space. That is a problem with a known shape: recovering an overcomplete set of directions from their sparse mixtures. §10's sparse coding, arriving where nobody expected it.

§34 Sparse autoencoders, for interpretability

The construction

Take a trained network. Collect the activations of one layer over a large corpus. Train a sparse autoencoder on those activations, with a code much wider than the layer, often by a factor of 8 to 64, and an L1 penalty on the code.

$$\min \;\; \|\mathbf{a} - W_{\text{dec}} \mathbf{h}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{h}\|_1, \qquad \mathbf{h} = \text{ReLU}(W_{\text{enc}}\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b})$$

Wider, because §33 says there are more features than dimensions. Sparse, because §33 says sparsity is the condition that made the packing possible. The autoencoder is being asked to invert the superposition.

Why this is the same object as §10 and why that is reassuring

This is a sparse autoencoder in the exact sense of §10 (the same objective, the same Laplace prior reading) with one change: it is trained on a network's activations rather than on data.

The reason to find that reassuring is that the method was not invented for this purpose. The penalty was proposed to learn features from images in the 1990s, justified by a claim about how natural signals are structured. It turns out to recover interpretable directions from a transformer's residual stream, and it does so because the same claim (most features are absent from most inputs) is true in both places.

Where the learned features are inspected, many are strikingly specific and the top-activating examples for a single feature are recognisably one thing where the underlying neurons were several.

§35 What a learned feature is, and is not

Pitfall: four things that get skipped

The features are not unique. Different dictionary widths, sparsity coefficients and seeds give different feature sets, and there is no criterion within the method that selects among them. The unidentifiability of §39, in a practical form.

Interpretation is not measurement. "The top twenty activating examples all look like X" is a hypothesis. It becomes a finding when you intervene: clamp the feature and check the output changes in the predicted way. Reading the top activations is Part XIV §11's looking-at-mistakes, and it has the same status: it generates the hypothesis.

Dead features and reconstruction error. A substantial fraction of features often never activate, and the autoencoder does not reconstruct the activations perfectly. Both numbers should be reported and frequently are not; a dictionary that explains 70% of the variance leaves 30% unaccounted for and no claim about completeness is available.

Selection. Papers show the interpretable features. The denominator, how many were examined, is what determines whether that means anything. Part XIV §20, in a setting where the search size is almost never stated.

Why it is nonetheless the most promising thing here

It is unsupervised, so it can find features nobody thought to look for, unlike probing, which can only confirm or deny a property you named in advance. It is scalable, having been run on production-scale models. And it makes predictions that can be checked by intervention rather than by inspection.

The honest position: a real advance on reading representations, with an unsolved identifiability problem underneath it, and a literature whose evaluation standards are still forming.

§36 Multimodal alignment

Two towers, one space

Encode images with one network and text with another, and train contrastively on paired data: matched image–caption pairs are positives, all other pairs in the batch are negatives. This is §17's objective with the two views drawn from different modalities.

What it buys is §38's zero-shot classification, and retrieval in either direction. The supervision is the pairing, which exists in quantity on the web without anyone labelling anything, the same move as §19, with the alignment supplied by the world rather than by an augmentation.

Pitfall: the modality gap

The two modalities do not end up in the same region of the space. They occupy separate cones with a measurable gap between their centroids, present from initialisation and preserved by training.

The reason is that the objective only constrains relative similarities within a row: a matched pair must score higher than the mismatched pairs it is compared against in that batch, and the loss is indifferent to how those comparisons are achieved. Nothing in it ever compares an image embedding to another image embedding, so nothing in it rewards moving the image cloud and the text cloud closer together: only rewards separating matches from mismatches within each row. A gap that starts at initialisation is therefore never optimised against; it is inherited, not produced, and training has no term that would close it.

The consequence is practical: cross-modal similarities are not comparable to within-modal ones. A threshold tuned on image–image similarity is meaningless applied to image–text, and systems that mix the two silently favour one. Measure the two distributions separately before choosing any threshold.

What movement E establishes

Probing tells you what is linearly available, and only if you ran the control. Accuracy without selectivity is not evidence, and selectivity is a property of the probe as much as of the representation.

The neuron basis is the wrong basis. Networks store more features than they have dimensions, so individual units are mixtures, and looking at them one at a time was always going to be confusing.

Sparsity is the tool for both problems: it is what makes superposition possible and what makes recovering the features from it possible.
