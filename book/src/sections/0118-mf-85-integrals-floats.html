Part JIntegrals

§25 Enough integration to read Part II

Good news, stated up front: you will never compute an integral by hand in this book. You need to recognise one and know what it means. That is a much smaller ask.

An integral accumulates. Where a derivative asks "how fast is this changing?", an integral asks "how much has piled up in total?"

The pairing is easiest to feel with a car. The speedometer reports a rate, a derivative of position. The odometer reports a total, an integral of speed. Drive for an hour at a steady 60 and the odometer gains 60, which is just multiplication. Drive for an hour while constantly speeding up and slowing down, and multiplication no longer applies, because there is no single speed to multiply by. What the odometer does instead is add up a great many tiny contributions: a little distance at this speed, a little more at that one, continuously. That summing-up of infinitely many small pieces is what the $\int$ symbol denotes, and the odometer performs it correctly without knowing any calculus.

Notice how the same limiting move from §15 appears again, applied to a different quantity. There we could not get a slope at a point, so we took slopes over short intervals and shrank them. Here we cannot get an area under a curved boundary, so we take areas of rectangles and shrink their width. Both times: answer a manageable approximate question, then watch where the answers go. Calculus is essentially that one idea used twice, in opposite directions, and the fundamental theorem is the statement that the two directions undo each other.

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \text{the area under the curve } f \text{ between } a \text{ and } b$$

Read the notation as a continuous sum, because that is exactly what it is:

  • $\int$ is a stretched letter S, for "sum". It is a $\sum$ for continuous quantities.
  • $f(x)$ is the height of each sliver.
  • $dx$ is its infinitesimal width.
  • $f(x)\,dx$ is therefore one sliver's area, and the $\int$ adds them all.
Intuition: the sum-to-integral dictionary

Every discrete formula has a continuous twin, obtained by one substitution:

DiscreteContinuous
$\sum_x P(x) = 1$$\int p(x)\,dx = 1$
$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x P(x)$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\,p(x)\,dx$
$\sum_y P(x,y)$$\int p(x,y)\,dy$

Swap $\sum$ for $\int$ and add a $dx$. That is the whole translation, and it covers every integral in Part II. If a continuous formula confuses you, read the discrete version instead; they say the same thing.

The one fact worth knowing

Differentiation and integration are inverse operations. This is the fundamental theorem of calculus, and its practical content is that accumulating a rate of change recovers the original quantity. Integrate velocity and you get distance travelled.

Why deep learning cares: mostly, it does not

Integrals appear in Part II whenever a variable is continuous: normalising a density, taking an expectation, marginalising out a variable. You read them; you do not evaluate them.

When an integral genuinely must be computed and has no closed form (which is the usual situation in probabilistic modelling), the field does not integrate. It samples. That is what Monte Carlo methods are, and it is why Part XXVIII exists. The practical answer to a hard integral is almost always "draw random samples and average", which requires no integration technique whatsoever.

Try it

What does $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(x)\,dx = 1$ say, in English? And what would the discrete version be?

Answer. "The total area under the density curve, across all possible values, is exactly 1": that is, the outcome is certain to be something. The discrete twin is $\sum_x P(x) = 1$: the probabilities of all outcomes add to one. Identical statement, two notations.

Part KNumbers in a computer

§26 Floating point, overflow, underflow

In 60 seconds

Computers store approximations, not real numbers. Three consequences: spacing between representable values grows with magnitude; going too large gives inf; going too small gives exactly zero. Every numerical trick in this book (log-likelihoods, subtracting the max in softmax, log-sum-exp, gradient clipping) is a defence against one of those three.

Go and read

Goldberg (1991), What every computer scientist should know about floating-point arithmetic. Long, and the title is not a boast: this is the definitive treatment of everything in this section and of Part III's opening. Read it once properly and you will stop being surprised by numerical bugs for the rest of your career.

History: when floating point goes wrong, it goes expensively

Before 1985 every manufacturer handled fractional arithmetic differently, so the same program produced different answers on different machines. The IEEE 754 standard, driven substantially by William Kahan at Berkeley, fixed the representation the world now uses, and won Kahan the Turing Award.

Two reminders of what the standard is defending against:

Ariane 5, 1996. Forty seconds after launch, the rocket destroyed itself. A 64-bit floating point value describing horizontal velocity was converted to a 16-bit signed integer. The new trajectory produced a number too large for the destination, the conversion failed, and the backup system (running identical code) failed the same way a moment later.

The Pentium FDIV bug, 1994. A handful of missing entries in a lookup table made certain division results wrong in the fourth significant digit. Intel initially argued the error was too rare to matter, then recalled the chips at a cost of several hundred million dollars.

Neither was a mathematical mistake. Both were arithmetic that ran, produced a number, and was believed. That is the failure mode this section is about, and it is exactly the failure mode of a training run that silently returns nan.

Not traditionally part of a math review, and included because it causes more real bugs in machine learning code than any theorem in this document.

A computer does not store real numbers. It stores floating point approximations, a fixed number of significant digits plus an exponent, like scientific notation with a hard limit on precision.

The scientific-notation comparison is not a loose analogy; it is the design. Think about what a physicist means by $6.022 \times 10^{23}$. Two separate pieces of information are being recorded: which digits (6022) and where the decimal point sits (23). A float works exactly this way, in binary, with a fixed budget for each piece. A 64-bit float spends 52 bits on the digits and 11 on the exponent, which works out to about 16 decimal digits of precision and a range of roughly $10^{-308}$ to $10^{308}$.

Everything that goes wrong follows from those two budgets being separate and fixed. The digit budget controls how precisely a number can be recorded; the exponent budget controls how large or small it can be. Run out of digits and you lose accuracy quietly; the number is still roughly right. Run out of exponent and you lose the number entirely, to inf at the top or to zero at the bottom.

The word floating names the crucial consequence. The decimal point is not fixed in place; it moves, and the sixteen digits of precision follow it. That is why a float can represent $10^{-300}$ and $10^{300}$ with equal relative accuracy, and it is also why precision is relative rather than absolute. You always get about sixteen significant digits: near 1 that means resolving differences of $10^{-16}$, and near $10^{15}$ it means resolving differences of about 1. Nothing is broken there. The same sixteen digits simply buy you less absolute precision when they are describing a larger number, exactly as quoting a national budget to four significant figures leaves millions unaccounted for while quoting your lunch bill to four is overkill.

Three consequences

Every trick in this book (log-likelihoods, max subtraction, log-sum-exp, clipping) is a defence against one of these three.

1. Spacing is not uniform. Representable numbers cluster densely near zero and spread out as magnitude grows. Near 1 the gap between neighbours is about $10^{-16}$; near $10^{15}$ it is about 1.

2. Overflow. Exceed roughly $1.8 \times 10^{308}$ and a float64 becomes inf. Then $\texttt{inf}/\texttt{inf}$ gives nan, and nan propagates through every subsequent operation, poisoning the entire computation silently.

3. Underflow. Go below about $2.2 \times 10^{-308}$ and a float64 leaves its normal range and enters the subnormal range, where it keeps shrinking but sheds precision as it does. Only below about $4.9 \times 10^{-324}$ does it collapse to exactly zero (§11's $0.9^{10000} \approx 10^{-458}$ is far past even that floor, which is why it is stored as exactly 0). Then $\log(0) = -\infty$, and you are in the same trouble from the other direction.

Why deep learning cares: this is where the tricks come from

Every numerical trick you will meet in this book is a defence against this section:

  • Log-likelihoods instead of likelihoods (§11), defeating underflow.
  • Subtracting the max in softmax: makes the largest exponent $e^0 = 1$, defeating overflow.
  • Log-sum-exp: summing in log space without leaving it.
  • Adding $\epsilon$ inside a log or a denominator: preventing $\log 0$ and division by zero.
  • Gradient clipping: bounding an update before it becomes inf.

None of these change the mathematics. All of them change whether the code runs.

Pitfall: never test floats for exact equality

0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3 is False in Python, and in essentially every language, because none of those values is exactly representable in binary.

Use a tolerance instead: np.isclose(a, b) for scalars, np.allclose(A, B) for arrays. Every drill in this project uses these for exactly that reason.

import numpy as np

print(0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3)              ### False
print(np.isclose(0.1 + 0.2, 0.3))    ### True

# overflow, and the standard fix
z = np.array([1000., 1001., 1002.])
with np.errstate(over='ignore', invalid='ignore'):
    print(np.exp(z) / np.exp(z).sum())     ### [nan nan nan]

zs = z - z.max()                           # now the largest is exp(0) = 1
print(np.exp(zs) / np.exp(zs).sum())       ### [0.09 0.245 0.665]
Try it

Why does subtracting the maximum leave the softmax result unchanged? Argue it algebraically.

Answer. Subtracting a constant $c$ multiplies every exponential by $e^{-c}$:

$\dfrac{e^{z_i - c}}{\sum_j e^{z_j - c}} = \dfrac{e^{z_i}e^{-c}}{e^{-c}\sum_j e^{z_j}} = \dfrac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}$

The $e^{-c}$ factors out of both numerator and denominator and cancels exactly. So the result is mathematically identical for any $c$, and choosing $c = \max(z)$ makes the largest exponent zero, which is the safest possible place to be. Free numerical safety, from one line of algebra.
