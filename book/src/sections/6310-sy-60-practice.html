Part FPractice

What to measure before optimising anything, what to state before claiming anything is secure, and, because this part sits closest to the line, what this book can and cannot establish about either.

§40 Before you optimise

Four measurements, in order, and most work stops after two

Where does the time go? Profile before changing anything. The distribution is almost always concentrated, and optimising outside the top few kernels cannot move the total.

Which side of the roofline is the hot kernel on? Compute its arithmetic intensity. Memory-bound means move less data; compute-bound means do less arithmetic or use fewer bits. These are disjoint sets of remedies and applying the wrong one wastes the week.

How close is it to the relevant ceiling? At 80% of achievable bandwidth there is nothing left; at 15% there is an access-pattern problem.

Is communication exposed or overlapped? For a distributed job this is usually the largest single item and it is invisible in a kernel profile; it shows as a gap.

§41 What to report about performance

Six things

The hardware. Every number in movement A and B is a property of a machine.

Arithmetic intensity for any kernel you claim to have optimised.

Achieved fraction of the relevant ceiling, not raw throughput: 40 GFLOP/s means nothing without the peak beside it.

Batch size and precision, since both change the regime rather than the constant.

For a distributed number, the topology and which axis was placed where.

For serving, a tail percentile and the utilisation it was measured at, because those two numbers are not separable.

§42 What to state before claiming security

Five things

The threat model: capability, goal, knowledge. Without it a defence is not a claim.

Which attack an empirical robustness number was measured against, and whether it was adapted to the defence.

The privacy unit, per record or per person, and the total budget across the whole training run rather than one step, accounted properly: basic composition (§26) is a valid but loose upper bound, and the moments accountant / Rényi-DP with subsampling amplification (§27) is what a real run should report.

What the defence does not cover. Rate limiting raises extraction's cost by a factor; Figure 8 measures sixteen. That is a useful sentence and "we rate limit" is not.

Whether an architectural control exists, since §38 says that is what actually protects a deployment.

§44 What is unsolved, and what this part cannot establish

Four, and a statement about evidence

This is the part of the book that sits closest to what this book's standard of evidence will support. The systems half is measurable on any machine and was measured. Several claims in the privacy and adversarial movements are not establishable at the scale a figure script reaches, and are presented as relationships rather than as numbers. That is a weaker standard than the rest of the book holds to, and it is marked on every figure it applies to.

Not yet measured. What differential privacy costs at frontier scale, at a budget anyone would call strong. The published points are sparse and not comparable.

Not currently measurable. Whether a model contains a backdoor. Accuracy is exactly what a backdoor preserves, and no general detection method exists.

Genuinely open. Separating instruction from data for a model with one input channel. Everything deployed is mitigation rather than solution.

Not empirical. What level of leakage is acceptable, and to whom. Part XXI's subject.

§45 What to carry forward

Five things

The currency is bytes moved. Figure 1 puts the ridge at 1.96 flops per byte, and everything below it is waiting for memory however fast the arithmetic gets.

The two transformations that matter change no arithmetic. Figure 2 measures fusion at 7.5× on a chain of eight; Figure 3 measures blocking at 3.5×, with the block size predictable from cache size rather than swept.

The ring is optimal in volume and not in latency. Figure 4 measures two buffers per worker against linear growth, a factor of 32 at 64 workers, and real libraries still switch algorithm by message size.

Query access is disclosure. Figure 8 measures a clone at 95% agreement in 40 queries against a victim fitted on thousands of examples, with label-only outputs costing sixteen times more and failing to prevent it.

What protects a deployment is architecture. Least privilege, isolation and confirmation work against attacks nobody has thought of; a robustness number does not.

Sixty seconds

Underneath every model is a machine that can do a hundred arithmetic operations in the time it takes to fetch one number, so the quantity that predicts how long anything takes is bytes moved rather than operations counted, and the two transformations that make numerical code fast, fusing chains and blocking for cache, change no arithmetic at all. Across machines the same logic holds: the ring all-reduce is used everywhere because its volume per worker does not grow with the worker count, and putting the chattiest parallelism axis on the slowest links costs more than any kernel optimisation recovers. Then the adversarial half, where the average case stops being the question. A model leaks its training data by several routes, one of which needs no attack at all; query access alone is enough to clone it; and the thing that actually protects a deployed system is not a property of the model but the architecture around it: least privilege, isolation, and asking before doing anything hard to undo.
