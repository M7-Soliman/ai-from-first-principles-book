§23 Self-information and entropy

In 60 seconds

Information is surprise: $-\log P(x)$. Certain events carry none, rare events carry a lot, and independent events add, which forces the logarithm and nothing else. Averaging surprise over a whole distribution gives entropy, which has a concrete meaning: the minimum average number of bits needed to encode outcomes from it. Predictability is compressibility.

Read this one

Shannon (1948), A mathematical theory of communication. If you read a single paper from this whole book, make it this. An entire field arrives essentially complete in seventy-nine pages, written with a clarity that most textbook treatments of it have not matched since. Everything in this part is downstream of it, and it assumes nothing you do not already have.

History: a whole field, arriving essentially complete, in one paper

Scientific fields normally accumulate. Information theory did not. In 1948 Claude Shannon, an engineer at Bell Labs, published a paper that defined the field's central quantities, proved its two foundational theorems, and established the limits of compression and of communication over a noisy channel, very nearly all of it at once.

The paper's decisive move was to strip meaning out of the problem. Shannon argued that for an engineer building a communication system, what a message means is irrelevant; what matters is that it was selected from a set of possible messages. Once information is defined as reduction of uncertainty rather than conveyance of meaning, it becomes measurable, and the measure is the entropy in this section.

The unit came from a colleague. John Tukey had coined bit, contracting "binary digit"; Shannon adopted it, and its first appearance in print is in his paper.

Practically everything downstream (data compression, error-correcting codes, cryptography, and the loss function in §25 of this part) descends from that one publication.

Start with a question that sounds philosophical and turns out to have a clean answer. How much information does learning an outcome give you?

Three requirements pin the answer down almost completely:

  1. Learning something certain conveys no information. If the sun rose this morning, being told so tells you nothing.
  2. Learning something unlikely conveys a lot. A one-in-a-million event carries far more news than a coin flip.
  3. Information from independent events should add. Two coin flips should carry twice the information of one.

Requirement 3 is the binding one. We need a function turning probabilities into information that converts multiplication into addition, since independent probabilities multiply. That is precisely what a logarithm does, and up to a choice of base it is the only thing that does.

Definition: self-information

$$I(x) = -\log P(x)$$ Measured in nats with the natural log, or bits with $\log_2$.

The minus sign is there because probabilities are at most 1, so their logs are at most 0. Negating gives a non-negative quantity. Check it against the requirements: $P = 1$ gives $I = 0$, and as $P \to 0$, $I \to \infty$.

Entropy: the average surprise

History: the name was reportedly chosen to win arguments

Shannon had the quantity before he had a word for it. By his own later account, he consulted John von Neumann, and the advice he received has become the most repeated anecdote in the field.

Call it entropy, von Neumann is said to have suggested, for two reasons. First, the same mathematical expression already appears in statistical mechanics, where Boltzmann and Gibbs had used $-\sum p \log p$ decades earlier to count the microscopic arrangements consistent with a macroscopic state. Second, and more usefully: nobody really understands entropy, so in a debate you will always have the advantage.

The story is second-hand and may have improved in transmission. But the connection it points at is real. Thermodynamic entropy counts how many microscopic configurations could produce what you observe; Shannon entropy counts how many messages could have produced what you received. Both are measures of how much you do not know, and they are the same formula for the same reason.

It also explains why the word feels borrowed when you meet it here. It was.

Self-information describes a single outcome. Average it over all outcomes and you get a property of the whole distribution.

Definition: Shannon entropy

$$H(P) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[-\log P(x)] = -\sum_x P(x) \log P(x)$$

Intuition: entropy is the cost of describing an outcome

Entropy has a concrete operational meaning: it is the minimum average number of bits needed to encode outcomes from the distribution.

A fair coin needs 1 bit; there is no cleverer scheme. But a coin landing heads 99% of the time needs only about 0.08 bits on average, because you can assign a very short code to the common outcome and a long one to the rare one. Predictability is compressibility, and entropy is exactly the exchange rate between them.

This is why file compression works, and why a random file cannot be compressed: it has maximal entropy, so there is no structure to exploit.

That claim about bits deserves to be shown rather than asserted, because it is what turns entropy from a formula into a thing you can hold. Here it is with numbers, and the agreement is exact.

Worked: entropy is a code length, to the digit

Setup. Four outcomes, unequally likely: say four commands sent to a robot, with probabilities

$P(A) = \tfrac12, \quad P(B) = \tfrac14, \quad P(C) = \tfrac18, \quad P(D) = \tfrac18$

The obvious code. Four outcomes, so two bits each: $A=00$, $B=01$, $C=10$, $D=11$. Average cost, 2 bits per message. Nothing wasted, apparently.

A better code. Give the common outcome a short codeword and make the rare ones pay for it:

$A = 0, \qquad B = 10, \qquad C = 110, \qquad D = 111$

These are still unambiguous when run together: no codeword is a prefix of another, so 0110100 decodes uniquely as $A, C, B, A$. Reading left to right, you always know when a codeword has ended.

What it costs on average. Weight each length by how often it is used:

$\tfrac12(1) + \tfrac14(2) + \tfrac18(3) + \tfrac18(3) = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.375 = \mathbf{1.75}$ bits

Now the entropy.

$H = -\big[\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 + \tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14 + \tfrac18\log_2\tfrac18 + \tfrac18\log_2\tfrac18\big] = \tfrac12(1) + \tfrac14(2) + \tfrac18(3) + \tfrac18(3) = \mathbf{1.75}$ bits

The same number, and not by luck: the same arithmetic. Look at the two lines: they are identical. The codeword lengths $1, 2, 3, 3$ are exactly $-\log_2 P$ for each outcome, and the averaging weights are the probabilities. Entropy is not like an average code length. It is one.

And no code does better. 1.75 against the naive 2 bits is a 12.5% saving, extracted purely from the fact that the outcomes were not equally likely.

That worked example also explains where the formula's shape comes from, which is otherwise mysterious. An outcome of probability $p$ deserves a codeword of about $-\log_2 p$ bits: halve the probability and you need one more bit, because each extra bit doubles how many things you can distinguish. Entropy averages those ideal lengths, weighted by how often each is used. That is all $-\sum P \log P$ says: ideal cost, times how often you pay it.

It is worth noticing what happens when the probabilities are not such convenient fractions. If $P(A) = 0.3$ the ideal length is $-\log_2 0.3 \approx 1.74$ bits, and you cannot send $1.74$ bits: codewords come in whole numbers. Entropy is therefore a bound that real codes approach without quite reaching, and they get closer when many symbols are encoded together. That gap is the entire subject of coding theory, and none of it is needed here. What matters for this book is that entropy is a cost, measured in bits, and §24 and §25 are both about paying more of it than you had to.

Two facts to carry forward. Entropy is maximised by the uniform distribution: knowing nothing is the most uncertain state you can be in. And it is zero for a deterministic outcome. This also makes precise the "maximum entropy" justification for the Gaussian from §15: among all distributions with a given variance, it is the one with the largest entropy, meaning the one that commits to the least.

§24 KL divergence

In 60 seconds

KL divergence measures how many extra bits you pay for believing $Q$ when reality is $P$. It is zero only when the two match, never negative, and (crucially) asymmetric: $D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) \neq D_{KL}(Q \parallel P)$. The forward direction spreads out to cover everything; the reverse locks onto one mode. Choosing between them is a real modelling decision.

Go and read

Kullback & Leibler (1951), On information and sufficiency. The divergence, at its origin, framed as a question this section does not ask: how much evidence does one observation give you for telling two hypotheses apart? That framing makes the asymmetry feel inevitable rather than awkward.

History: from cryptanalysis to the objective of every generative model

Solomon Kullback and Richard Leibler introduced the quantity in 1951, in a paper about the information contained in a statistical observation for discriminating between two hypotheses. Both were cryptanalysts at what became the National Security Agency, and the framing shows: they were interested in how much evidence a piece of intercepted data provides for one explanation over another.

It is closely related to the weight-of-evidence measure Turing had used at Bletchley Park, described in §21; Kullback had worked on similar problems, and the wartime lineage of these ideas is denser than the citation record suggests.

They called it divergence rather than distance deliberately, aware that the asymmetry disqualified it from being a metric. That caveat is now the most practically important thing about it: variational inference and the VAE objective in later parts choose the reverse direction, and the mode-seeking behaviour that follows is visible in the samples those models produce.

Now the question that machine learning actually needs answered: given two distributions, how different are they?

Definition: Kullback–Leibler divergence

$$D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\!\left[\log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right] = \sum_x P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$$

Intuition: the price of believing the wrong distribution

Reality follows $P$. You mistakenly designed your code assuming $Q$. KL divergence is how many extra bits per message that mistake costs you.

If $Q = P$ you designed optimally and the extra cost is zero. The further $Q$ is from $P$, the more you waste. This framing explains its two key properties at once: it is always $\geq 0$ (you cannot beat the optimal code), and it equals zero exactly when $P = Q$.

It is not a distance

Forward KL is afraid of missing anything. Reverse KL is afraid of being wrong. Figure 15 shows what each fear looks like.

This trips people up, so it is worth stating plainly:

$$D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) \neq D_{KL}(Q \parallel P)$$

KL divergence is asymmetric. It also fails the triangle inequality. It is therefore not a metric, which is why it is called a divergence; the terminology is a warning label.

The asymmetry is not a defect to work around; the two directions ask genuinely different questions, and choosing between them is a real modelling decision.

Intuition: why each direction behaves that way

Forward, $D_{KL}(p \parallel q)$: the expectation is over $p$, so wherever $p$ has mass, a small $q$ makes $\log(p/q)$ enormous. The penalty is for failing to cover what really happens, so $q$ spreads out to avoid it. This is mean-seeking.

Reverse, $D_{KL}(q \parallel p)$: the expectation is over $q$, so regions where $q$ is near zero contribute nothing regardless of $p$. Placing mass where $p$ is small is punished, but ignoring parts of $p$ entirely is free. So $q$ retreats to a single mode. This is mode-seeking.

Memorable version: forward KL is afraid of missing anything; reverse KL is afraid of being wrong. Variational inference (Part XXVIII §17–24) and the VAE (Part XVI §14–20) use the reverse form, and the blurriness people complain about in VAE samples traces partly back to this choice.

§25 Cross-entropy: your loss function

In 60 seconds

$H(P,Q) = H(P) + D_{KL}(P \parallel Q)$. Since the true distribution $P$ is fixed by the data, $H(P)$ is a constant, so minimising cross-entropy is exactly minimising KL divergence, more cheaply. For a one-hot label the whole thing collapses to $-\log q(\text{correct class})$: the negative log of the probability your model put on the right answer.

Definition: cross-entropy

$$H(P, Q) = -\mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[\log Q(x)] = -\sum_x P(x)\log Q(x)$$

Expand the KL divergence and the connection appears immediately:

$$D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) = \sum_x P(x)\log P(x) - \sum_x P(x)\log Q(x) = -H(P) + H(P, Q)$$

so

$$H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_{KL}(P \parallel Q)$$

Now the punchline. In supervised learning $P$ is the true label distribution: fixed, given by the data, entirely outside your control. So $H(P)$ is a constant, and:

The key consequence

Minimising cross-entropy is exactly minimising KL divergence. They differ by a constant that does not depend on the model, so they have identical minimisers and identical gradients.

That is why nobody optimises KL divergence directly: cross-entropy is equivalent and cheaper, since it drops the $H(P)$ term you would only compute to discard.

What it looks like in practice

For classification, the true distribution is one-hot: probability 1 on the correct class, 0 elsewhere. Every term in the sum vanishes except one, and the whole cross-entropy collapses to:

$$\text{loss} = -\log q(\text{correct class})$$

The negative log of the probability your model assigned to the right answer. Nothing more.

Look back at the right panel of Figure 14, which is exactly this function. Assign the correct class probability 1 and the loss is 0. Assign it 0.5 and you pay about 0.69. Assign it 0.01 and you pay 4.6. As the assigned probability approaches zero, the loss grows without bound: confident and wrong is punished arbitrarily hard, which is precisely the incentive you want.

import numpy as np

def cross_entropy(logits, label):
    """Loss for one example. Note: never exponentiate then log -- do it in one step."""
    z = logits - logits.max()                 # stabilise (§20)
    log_probs = z - np.log(np.exp(z).sum())   # log-softmax, computed directly
    return -log_probs[label]

print(cross_entropy(np.array([2., 1., 0.]), 0))   ### 0.4076  -- confident, correct
print(cross_entropy(np.array([2., 1., 0.]), 2))   ### 2.4076  -- confident, wrong
Pitfall: never compute $\log(\text{softmax}(z))$ in two steps

If a softmax output underflows to exactly 0, taking its log gives $-\infty$ and your loss becomes nan, silently destroying the run. Computing the log-softmax directly, as above, subtracts in log-space and never evaluates $\log 0$. This is why every framework ships a fused cross_entropy_with_logits, and why you should use it.

In the framework: the most-used function in deep learning

The loss you have just derived is one call, and it returns exactly the numbers the NumPy version above produced:

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F

logits = torch.tensor([[2., 1., 0.]])       # shape (batch, classes) -- RAW scores
F.cross_entropy(logits, torch.tensor([0]))   ### 0.4076  confident, correct
F.cross_entropy(logits, torch.tensor([2]))   ### 2.4076  confident, wrong

Note what the target is. Not a one-hot vector: the integer index of the correct class. That is §25's collapse made literal: since every term of the sum vanishes except the one where $P(x) = 1$, the library never builds the one-hot vector at all. It takes the index and looks up a single number. For a language model with a vocabulary of 50,000, that is the difference between allocating a 50,000-wide vector per token and reading one element.

The name lies, and the lie matters

Despite being called cross_entropy, the function does not take a probability distribution. It takes logits (raw, unbounded network outputs) and performs the softmax itself, internally, fused with the log. It is log_softmax followed by a lookup, in one operation.

So the pitfall above is not hypothetical. Applying softmax yourself and passing the result runs the operation twice:

p = F.softmax(logits, dim=1)                  # DON'T
F.cross_entropy(p, torch.tensor([0]))        ### 0.7972 -- wrong, and it does not crash

0.7972 against the correct 0.4076. The model has not changed and the answer is nearly twice as large. Nothing raises an error, the loss still decreases during training, and the model simply learns worse than it should, which is the most expensive kind of bug, because it presents as a mediocre result rather than a failure.

Why fusing them is not merely convenient

Section 25's pitfall gave the reason and it is worth seeing the arithmetic. Computing the two steps separately means forming a probability and then taking its log. If any probability underflows to exactly zero (which for a confidently wrong prediction in float32 it will), then $\log 0 = -\infty$, the loss becomes nan, and every subsequent gradient is nan as well.

The fused version never forms the probability. It computes $\log \text{softmax}(z)_i = z_i - \operatorname{logsumexp}(z)$ directly, which is a subtraction in log space and cannot underflow. Part III §4 covers the logsumexp half of that identity; this is where it earns its keep.

§26 Maximum likelihood, and where the loss came from

In 60 seconds

Choose the parameters that make the observed data most probable. Take a log to turn the product into a sum, divide by $m$, and the expression is cross-entropy between the data distribution and the model. Three names, one procedure: maximum likelihood, minimum cross-entropy, minimum KL divergence. Cross-entropy loss is not a convention somebody picked; it is what maximum likelihood is.

History: Fisher's principle, and how long it took to reach neural networks

Maximum likelihood was introduced and named by Ronald Fisher in the 1920s, and he regarded it as among his most important contributions. The principle (prefer the parameters under which what actually happened was most probable) is so natural that Gauss and others had used special cases of it a century earlier without isolating it as a general method.

The connection this section derives, between maximum likelihood and cross-entropy, is a straightforward consequence once Shannon's 1948 work exists. But the two literatures were separate, and neural networks took a surprisingly long time to adopt it.

Early networks were routinely trained with squared error even for classification, which (as drill C14 shows) is maximum likelihood under an assumption of Gaussian noise, and a poor description of a categorical label. The change to cross-entropy through the 1980s and 90s produced better gradients and faster training, and it came from taking the probabilistic interpretation seriously rather than from any architectural insight.

That is the argument for this whole part in one example. Knowing what your loss function means occasionally tells you it should be a different function.

One derivation to close the chapter. It answers a question you may not have known to ask: why that loss function and not some other?

Suppose your model, with parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, assigns probability $p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta})$ to each data point. The obvious principle: choose the parameters that make the observed data most probable.

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{ML} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \; \prod_{i=1}^{m} p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}; \boldsymbol{\theta})$$

That product is a computational disaster: multiply ten thousand numbers below 1 and you underflow to zero. So take the log, which is monotonic and therefore preserves the location of the maximum while turning the product into a sum:

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{ML} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \; \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}; \boldsymbol{\theta})$$

Divide by $m$, which changes nothing about the maximiser, and recognise the result as an expectation under the empirical distribution from §17:

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{ML} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim \hat{p}_{\text{data}}}\big[\log p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta})\big]$$

Flip the sign to make it a minimisation, and that expression is exactly the cross-entropy between the empirical data distribution and the model, which by §25 is exactly the KL divergence between them, up to a constant.

The three-way equivalence

These are the same procedure, stated in three vocabularies:

  • Maximum likelihood: make the observed data as probable as possible.
  • Minimum cross-entropy: minimise $-\log$ of the probability assigned to the truth.
  • Minimum KL divergence: make the model distribution close to the data distribution.

Not analogies. The same optimisation problem.

Why deep learning cares: this is the whole justification

Cross-entropy loss is not a convention someone picked because it worked. It is what maximum likelihood is, once you write it down carefully. That is why it is the default for essentially every classifier ever trained.

The frame keeps paying out. Mean squared error is maximum likelihood under an assumption of Gaussian noise; assume Laplace noise instead and you get mean absolute error. $L^2$ regularization is maximum a posteriori estimation with a Gaussian prior on the weights; $L^1$ is the same with a Laplace prior, which closes the loop opened in §17. Part V develops all of this properly, and it will feel like consolidation rather than new material, because the whole structure is already here.

Try it: the last one

A binary classifier outputs $0.9$ for an example whose true label is 1, and $0.3$ for an example whose true label is 0. Compute the cross-entropy loss for each, and the average.

Answer. For the first, the probability assigned to the truth is $0.9$, so the loss is $-\log(0.9) \approx 0.105$. For the second, the true label is 0 and the model assigned it probability $1 - 0.3 = 0.7$, so the loss is $-\log(0.7) \approx 0.357$. The average is about $0.231$.

Note the second example carries more than three times the loss of the first, even though both predictions are on the correct side of $0.5$. Cross-entropy rewards confident correctness, not merely correctness, which is exactly why models trained on it produce usable probability estimates rather than bare decisions.
