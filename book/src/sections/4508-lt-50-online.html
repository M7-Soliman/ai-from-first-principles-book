Part ELearning against an adversary

Every bound so far assumed the data was drawn independently from a fixed distribution. Drop that assumption entirely, let an adversary choose each example after seeing what you did, and there is still a theory, with guarantees that hold unconditionally. It is a different subject with a different currency, and it is the one that survives contact with a world that moves.

§31 The online protocol

The game, and what replaces risk

For $t = 1, \dots, T$: you commit to $w_t$; the adversary reveals a loss function $\ell_t$; you suffer $\ell_t(w_t)$.

Nothing is assumed about how the adversary chooses: no distribution, no independence, no stationarity. So "true risk" has no referent, and the quantity to minimise is regret:

$$\mathrm{Regret}_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \ell_t(w_t) \;-\; \min_{w \in \mathcal{W}} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \ell_t(w)$$

How much worse you did than the single best fixed choice, judged with hindsight. An algorithm with regret $o(T)$ has average loss approaching the best fixed choice; it is no-regret.

Why a guarantee is possible at all against an adversary

Because the comparison is weak in a specific way: not against the best sequence of decisions, which is hopeless, but against the best single fixed decision. The adversary can make you do badly in absolute terms; it cannot make you do much worse than the best constant strategy would have done on the same sequence.

That is a smaller promise than the PAC framework's, and unlike it, it requires no assumption that can turn out to be false.

§32 Why following the leader fails

The obvious algorithm, and the sequence that beats it

Play whatever would have been best so far. Against an adversary this can incur regret proportional to $T$: with two actions alternating in which is best, the leader flips every round and you are wrong every round, while the best fixed action is right half the time.

The failure is instability: the algorithm's output changes completely in response to one observation. Adding a regulariser that keeps consecutive decisions close (follow the regularised leader) removes it, and the connection to §19's stability is not a coincidence: in both settings, an algorithm that reacts too strongly to one point does not generalise.

§33 Online gradient descent

The algorithm and its guarantee

$w_{t+1} = \Pi_{\mathcal{W}}\!\left(w_t - \eta_t \nabla \ell_t(w_t)\right)$: one gradient step on the loss you just saw, projected back into the feasible set.

For convex losses with bounded gradients and a bounded domain, and $\eta_t = \Theta(1/\sqrt{t})$, the regret is $O(\sqrt{T})$, so average regret falls as $O(1/\sqrt{T})$. Strong convexity improves this to $O(\log T)$.

Both are tight. And the algorithm is the one from Part IX, unchanged, now carrying a guarantee that assumes nothing about where the data came from.

The connection back

An online-to-batch conversion turns any no-regret algorithm into a batch learner: run it over an i.i.d. sample, average the iterates, and the regret bound becomes a risk bound. So the online guarantees imply the statistical ones for convex problems.

Which reframes Part IX §13's stochastic gradient descent. Its analysis was presented as an optimisation result about reaching a minimum. It is also a learning result, and the reason averaging the iterates, Part IX §25's Polyak averaging, is the form with the guarantee.

§34 Multiplicative weights

Combining experts

Maintain weights over $K$ experts, predict by their weighted vote, and after each round multiply each weight by $e^{-\eta \ell_t(i)}$. Regret is $O(\sqrt{T \log K})$.

The $\log K$ is §4's union bound again, in a setting with no distribution anywhere. You can track the best of exponentially many experts at logarithmic cost, which is the same bargain that made model selection viable and here needs no i.i.d. assumption.

The algorithm reappears throughout: boosting is multiplicative weights over training examples, and Part XVIII §35's exploration strategies are close relatives.

§35 Bandits, and the cost of not seeing

Full information against partial

In the full-information setting you learn every expert's loss. In the bandit setting you learn only the loss of what you chose, Part XVIII §34's problem, now with a regret guarantee rather than an empirical comparison.

The cost of partial feedback is a factor of $\sqrt{K}$: regret goes from $O(\sqrt{T \log K})$ to $O(\sqrt{TK})$. That quantifies exploration exactly: it is what not seeing the counterfactual costs, and it is the same missing quantity as Part XXII §32's off-policy problem.

Contextual bandits add a state before each choice, which is the formal model of most recommendation and ad systems. Regret becomes $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{TK\log|\mathcal{H}|})$, the union bound, one more time.

§36 Non-stationarity

When the best fixed choice is the wrong benchmark

Regret compares against the best fixed decision. If the world genuinely changes, that benchmark is weak: a no-regret algorithm can be matching a comparator that is itself bad.

The repairs: tracking regret compares against the best sequence with at most $k$ switches; dynamic regret compares against the best decision each round, and is bounded only in terms of how much the world moved.

Both formalise the same practical point Part XIV §26 made from the deployment side: a system performing well against a stale baseline is not necessarily performing well.

§37 What the adversarial framing buys and costs

The trade, stated plainly

Buys: guarantees that cannot be invalidated by an assumption failing, because there are no distributional assumptions. Algorithms that adapt continuously rather than being retrained. And a single framework covering prediction, bandits and games.

Costs: a weaker comparator (the best fixed decision, not the best possible). Rates of $O(\sqrt{T})$ where the i.i.d. setting sometimes gives $O(\log T)$ or better. And convexity, which most of the theory still needs and neural networks do not have.

The honest summary: where the i.i.d. assumption holds, the statistical theory says more. Where it does not, this is what remains, and what remains is unconditional.

§38 What movement E establishes

Three things

Learning is possible with no distributional assumption at all, if you weaken the comparator to the best fixed decision. Regret is what replaces risk, and no-regret algorithms exist with tight rates.

Stability is the requirement in both theories. Following the leader fails for the same reason an unstable algorithm fails to generalise, and adding a regulariser fixes both.

The union bound's logarithm reappears everywhere: finite classes, expert weights, contextual bandits. It is the same bargain each time: search over exponentially many options and pay logarithmically.
