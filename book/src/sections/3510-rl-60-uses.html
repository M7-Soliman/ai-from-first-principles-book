Part FWhat it is used for

§39 Reward design

Go and read

Ng, Harada & Russell (1999), Policy invariance under reward transformations. The proof that potential-based shaping leaves the optimal policy unchanged and that essentially nothing else does. The telescoping argument is half a page and worth seeing once, because it tells you exactly which hints are free and which change the problem.

Why this is the characteristic failure of the whole subject

A reward function is a specification written by a person, and the agent is a search over policies paid to find whatever it literally says. Any gap between the specification and the intention is a gap the optimiser is being rewarded to exploit.

The figure's policy is not confused. It found a cycle that pays repeatedly and, with discounting, a repeatable small reward beats a single terminal one. The reward said so.

Part XIV §33's Goodhart's law is the general statement, and reinforcement learning is its sharpest case for two reasons: the agent has full freedom over its own data, and the optimisation is powerful enough to find edges a person would not think to check.

What helps

Potential-based shaping is the one technique with a guarantee. Adding $F(s,a,s') = \gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$ for any function $\Phi$ leaves the optimal policy provably unchanged: the added terms telescope over any trajectory and cancel. So you can give dense hints without changing what is optimal, and any shaping not of this form can change the answer.

Reward from a model of preferences rather than hand-written rules (§41).

Constraints rather than penalties. A penalty is a price the agent may choose to pay; a constraint is not negotiable.

And looking at the behaviour. Part XIV §11's discipline. The figure's failure is obvious in one rollout and invisible in the reward curve, which was going up the whole time.

§40 Offline reinforcement learning

Go and read

Levine et al. (2020), Offline reinforcement learning: tutorial, review, and perspectives. Why learning from a fixed dataset is not simply supervised learning: the max in the Bellman target ranges over actions the data does not cover, and nothing corrects the extrapolation.

Learning from a fixed dataset

No interaction: a logged dataset of transitions from some other policy. Attractive wherever exploration is expensive or dangerous: medicine, industrial control, recommendation.

The specific difficulty is extrapolation. The Bellman target contains $\max_{a'} Q(s',a')$, and that max ranges over actions the dataset may contain no examples of. The network extrapolates, extrapolation is optimistic, the optimistic value becomes a bootstrap target, and nothing in the data ever corrects it: §19's maximisation bias with the correction mechanism removed.

The remedies all constrain the policy to stay near the data: penalise divergence from the behaviour policy, learn deliberately pessimistic values, or avoid bootstrapping entirely by treating the problem as conditional sequence modelling. The last is worth noting for what it implies: a large part of offline RL turns into Part XVI's autoregressive modelling with the return as a conditioning variable.

§41 Learning from human feedback

Go and read

Christiano et al. (2017), Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences. The construction behind RLHF, several years before it was used that way, and clear about why comparisons rather than scores. Then Gao, Schulman & Hilton (2023) for the measured version of overoptimisation: reward-model score rising while true quality peaks and falls.

When the reward cannot be written down

For "a helpful answer" there is no reward function to write. The standard construction has three stages: collect comparisons between pairs of outputs, fit a reward model to predict which a person preferred, then optimise the policy against that model with PPO plus a KL penalty against the starting policy.

Comparisons rather than scores because people are far more consistent at ranking two things than at assigning absolute numbers (Part XIII §5's annotator agreement, turned into a design decision).

Pitfall: the reward model is a learned proxy, and §39 applies to it

Optimising hard against a learned reward model finds its errors. Measured behaviour: reward model score rises steadily while human ratings of the same outputs plateau and then fall. The policy has moved off the distribution the reward model was trained on, where its predictions were never validated.

The KL penalty against the initial policy is the direct response: it prices moving far from where the reward model is trustworthy. Its coefficient is therefore not a regularisation detail but the main safety-against-overoptimisation knob, and it is tuned by watching the gap between reward-model score and human judgement.

DPO and its relatives remove the separate reward model and the RL loop entirely, optimising a classification loss on the preference pairs that has the same optimum, an equivalence derived in Part XX §29. Simpler, stabler, and it gives up the ability to keep sampling fresh outputs for the reward model to judge, which is the thing online methods buy.

§42 Training for reasoning

Where the reward is checkable

The setting that changed the picture recently: tasks where correctness can be verified automatically: a proof checker, a unit test, a numerical answer. The reward is then not a learned proxy but a fact, and §39's central failure does not apply.

The policy is a language model, an episode is a generated solution, and the reward arrives at the end. Long horizon, extremely sparse, enormous action space (everything this part says is hard), and it works, largely because the starting policy is already competent, so exploration is a search over plausible solutions rather than over random tokens.

That last point is the general lesson and it is worth extracting: a strong initial policy converts exploration from an unsolved problem into a manageable one. §38's avoidance, deployed deliberately.

The dominant algorithm family here is critic-free and group-relative (GRPO and its relatives): sample a group of completions for the same prompt, use the group's own reward mean as the baseline in place of a learned $V_\phi$, and skip the critic entirely. It is §27's baseline idea taken to its cheapest extreme: once many rollouts per prompt are already being sampled, the group mean is a serviceable baseline and a critic is one more network that has to be fitted well.

The failures that remain are the ones you would predict. A test suite that can be satisfied without solving the problem will be. Reward on the final answer alone gives no credit assignment across a long derivation, and process-level reward requires judging the steps, which returns you to §41's learned proxy.

§43 Evaluating a reinforcement learning result

Go and read

Henderson et al. (2018), Deep reinforcement learning that matters. Results varying more across random seeds than across the algorithms being compared, with three seeds the norm at the time. Read alongside Part XIV §19: this is that section's problem in the setting where it is worst, because the policy determines its own data and an early difference compounds.

Pitfall: this literature has a reproducibility problem, and it is measured

Careful replication studies have found that reported results in deep RL vary enormously across random seeds, often more than across the algorithms being compared, and that a small number of seeds is standard practice.

Part XIV §19's seed variance, in the setting where it is worst: the policy determines its own data, so an early difference compounds rather than averaging out. Two runs of the same algorithm can end in genuinely different places.

What to require, and it is Part XIV's list with one addition: many seeds (ten, not three) with the full distribution rather than the mean; the same environment version and preprocessing; the implementation details (§32 says they matter as much as the method); and a random-policy baseline, which is more often informative than it should be.

§44 When not to use it

Four cases, and they cover most attempts

You have the labels. If demonstrations of correct behaviour exist, imitation learning is supervised learning: no exploration, no credit assignment, no reward design, and it is almost always the right first thing to try.

The problem is not sequential. If actions do not affect what you see next, it is a bandit or a supervised problem. Reinforcement learning solves a harder problem and pays for it.

You cannot specify the reward. §39 says a wrong specification is optimised faithfully. If you cannot write it down and cannot get preferences, the project has no objective.

Interaction is expensive and you have no simulator. Sample efficiency is this field's standing weakness, and the successes are overwhelmingly in domains with a fast, faithful simulator or a verifier.

§45 What to carry forward

The one thing

The agent optimises the reward you wrote, using data it chose to collect.

Both halves of that sentence cause the difficulties. Because the reward is what is optimised, any gap between it and your intention is found and exploited (§39). Because the data is chosen by the policy, an early mistake removes the evidence that would have corrected it (§33), a bad step is not recoverable (§30), and the training distribution moves as the learner moves (§1).

Every method in this part is a response to one of those. The Bellman equation handles credit assignment. Baselines and advantage estimation handle the variance that comes from long trajectories. Trust regions handle the step that cannot be taken back. Optimism and posterior sampling handle the evidence that will not arrive on its own.

And the practical summary that most often matters: if you can turn the problem into supervised learning, do. The cases where this part is necessary are the cases where you genuinely cannot, and they are fewer than the number of projects that reach for it.
