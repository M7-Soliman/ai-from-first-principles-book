BuildThe project

Twelve to sixteen hours. You measure your own machine, use the numbers to redirect an optimisation you were about to get wrong, and then attack a model you trained yourself.

P.1 What you are building

metal: a roofline for your hardware, a fused kernel, a blocked multiplication, a collective simulator, a private training loop, and three attacks with ground truth.

The rule: measure before changing anything, and report every number against a ceiling. A throughput figure without the peak beside it is not a measurement.

P.2 Stage 1: your machine

  1. Measure achievable bandwidth with a large copy. Compare with the specification.
  2. Measure peak arithmetic with a large matrix multiplication.
  3. Compute your ridge point.
  4. Place five operations you actually run on the plot, including a transcendental.

Deliverable: your roofline with your kernels on it. Step four is where the rule of thumb breaks: check whether the transcendental lands where "elementwise means memory-bound" predicts.

P.3 Stage 2: fusion and blocking

  1. Time an elementwise chain separately and fused, preallocating in both arms.
  2. Confirm the speedup tracks the chain length and stays near its bound.
  3. Implement blocked multiplication and sweep the block size.
  4. Predict the best block from your cache size before running the sweep, then check.
  5. Break a compiled function with a data-dependent branch and time it again.

Deliverable: the two speedup curves and your predicted-versus-measured block size. Step four is the point of knowing the hierarchy: if you can only find the answer by searching, you have not used it.

P.4 Stage 3: many machines

  1. Compute volume per worker for naive, ring and tree all-reduce.
  2. Add per-hop latency and find the message size at which the ranking changes.
  3. Simulate a synchronous step with one slow worker in a hundred.
  4. For a model you train, compute parameters, gradients, optimiser state and activations separately.
  5. Assign parallelism axes to a topology by traffic.

Deliverable: the crossover message size and the axis assignment. Step five is arithmetic done before any code is written, and it decides more than tuning does.

P.5 Stage 4: privacy

  1. Implement per-example clipping and the noised gradient step.
  2. Confirm both parts are necessary by removing each in turn.
  3. Account the total budget across a training run, not one step.
  4. Compute the guarantee for a person contributing a thousand records.
  5. Sweep the noise and measure utility, on a task hard enough that the noise costs something.

Deliverable: the utility curve and your total budget. Step five needs care: on an easy, well-separated problem the cost is invisible, which tells you about your task rather than about privacy.

P.6 Stage 5: attacks with ground truth

  1. Write down your predictions first, in a file, before running anything.
  2. Train a model, score members and non-members by loss, and compute the attack's AUC.
  3. Vary regularisation and plot the attack against the generalisation gap.
  4. Extract a model through its own API and sweep the query count.
  5. Restrict the output to a label and measure the factor.
  6. Plant a backdoor in a small fraction of the training data and measure clean and triggered accuracy.
  7. Score your predictions.

Deliverable: the three attack results and the scored predictions. Step six is the one to sit with: clean accuracy is unchanged, so no ordinary evaluation would have found it.

P.7 Stage 6: a threat model

  1. For a system you actually run, write down the adversary's capability, goal and knowledge.
  2. List what a compromised component could reach.
  3. Identify one architectural control that would bound it, and one that already exists.

Deliverable: three paragraphs. This and Part XXXI's safety claim are the only deliverables in this book that are writing rather than measurement, and both are the artefact a deployment actually turns on.

P.8 Stretch

  • A hand-written fused kernel for a chain from a model you run.
  • A roofline for a real training step, with every significant kernel placed.
  • A ring implementation over simulated workers, with bytes counted exactly.
  • Verbatim extraction from a model you trained, before and after deduplication.
  • Breaking a gradient-obscuring defence with an estimated gradient.
  • An audit of a system you run, on all four axes of §40.

P.9 Done when

  • You know your machine's bandwidth, peak arithmetic and ridge point.
  • You have placed your own kernels on your own roofline.
  • You have found a kernel where the elementwise rule of thumb fails.
  • Your fusion speedup tracks the chain length and stays near its bound.
  • You predicted your best block size before sweeping, and were right.
  • You have seen a compiled function get slow from a data-dependent branch.
  • You know the message size at which your collective should switch algorithm.
  • You have your model's memory broken into four parts.
  • You have assigned parallelism axes to a topology by traffic.
  • You have removed each half of the private step and seen what breaks.
  • You have a total privacy budget, not a per-step one.
  • You have a membership attack scored against ground truth.
  • You have cloned a model through its API and measured the label-only factor.
  • You have planted a backdoor and confirmed clean accuracy is unchanged.
  • You wrote predictions down before Stage 5 and scored them afterwards.
  • You have a written threat model for something you run.
Why this project

Stage 1 redirects more work than anything else in this book. Most performance effort goes into the wrong half of the roofline, and the measurement that would have prevented it takes twenty minutes and is almost never done.

Stage 5 step six is the security counterpart. A backdoor that leaves clean accuracy untouched is the clearest possible demonstration that an accuracy metric is not a safety property, and having planted one yourself changes how much weight you give a benchmark number for the rest of your career.

The framework, and what is underneath it

Dataset, model family, loss, optimiser. Every part of this book has chosen among those on the assumption that the machine underneath is neutral and that nobody is trying to break the result. Both assumptions are wrong, and this part is what they were hiding.

Underneath, the machine has a shape: arithmetic is nearly free and moving data is not, so a hundred-fold difference in speed can separate two implementations of identical mathematics, and the number that predicts which is not one the framework mentions. In front, an adversary changes the question from the average case to the worst one, and against that, what protects a system is not a property of the model at all but the architecture around it. The model is the part of the system you chose; the other two are the parts you inherited, and they set what your choices can be worth.
