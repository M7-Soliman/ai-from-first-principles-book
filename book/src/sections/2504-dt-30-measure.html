Part CMeasuring it

Part B was a list of things that go wrong. This movement is the instruments, because "data quality" is not a property you assess by looking, it is a set of quantities you either measure or do not.

§14 Quality has to be operationalised

"High-quality data" is used constantly and means nothing until it is turned into something you can compute. The useful move is to refuse the abstraction and name the specific property you want.

Five properties, each with a measurement
PropertyHow you measure it
Label accuracyRelabel a random sample carefully; report the disagreement rate with an interval (Part V §8)
CoverageCounts along the axes of §3, against a target distribution you wrote down first
RedundancyNear-duplicate rate, §15
ContaminationOverlap with every evaluation set, §16
ProvenanceFraction of records whose source and licence are recorded, §19

Each is a number you can put on a dashboard and watch move. "Quality" is not.

Why the sample-and-relabel is worth more than it costs

Take 500 random records and have someone competent label them carefully, blind to the existing label. You now have an estimate of your label accuracy with a confidence interval, which is the single most useful number about a dataset and one that almost nobody has.

It costs a day. It tells you the ceiling on any model you will train, it tells you whether a reported improvement is inside the noise of your own labels, and (Part V §20) it is the only estimate of irreducible error you are going to get.

§15 Finding near-duplicates at scale

Go and read

Broder (1997), On the resemblance and containment of documents. Where MinHash comes from, written for the problem of finding duplicate web pages. Short, and the identity in §15 is derived in a paragraph. Then Lee et al. (2022), Deduplicating training data makes language models better, for what happens when you actually apply it to a modern corpus: better models from fewer tokens, and substantially less memorisation.

Exact duplicates are a hash set. Near-duplicates are the problem, and the naive approach is $O(n^2)$ pairwise comparisons, impossible past a few hundred thousand documents.

Shingles, Jaccard, MinHash

Shingle each document into the set of its overlapping $k$-grams. Similarity is then the Jaccard index of two sets:

$$J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}$$

Computing that for every pair is still $O(n^2)$. The trick: for a random permutation $\pi$ of the universe of shingles,

$$\Pr\big[\min \pi(A) = \min \pi(B)\big] = J(A, B)$$

This is because the minimum over $A \cup B$ is equally likely to be any of its elements, and it lands in the intersection exactly $J$ of the time. So take $k$ permutations, record the $k$ minima as a signature, and the fraction of matching entries estimates the Jaccard index.

Why $1/\sqrt{k}$, and why that makes this practical

Each permutation is a Bernoulli trial with success probability $J$. The signature match rate is their mean, so by Part V §3 its standard error is $\sqrt{J(1-J)/k}$, the same $1/\sqrt{n}$ that governs every estimate in this book.

Which is why a few hundred permutations suffice whatever the corpus size. The accuracy depends on the signature length, not on how many documents you have.

The remaining problem is that comparing all pairs of signatures is still $O(n^2)$. Locality-sensitive hashing solves it: split each signature into bands, hash each band, and only compare documents that collide in at least one band. Similar documents collide with high probability; dissimilar ones almost never do. The band structure gives you an explicit knob on the precision–recall trade, and the whole thing is one pass over the corpus.

§16 Detecting contamination

Contamination is a search problem: for each evaluation item, is it in the training corpus?

Three levels, in ascending cost and descending certainty

Exact match. Hash the evaluation items and scan. Catches copies, misses everything reformatted.

$n$-gram overlap. Flag a training document containing, say, a 13-gram from an evaluation item. This is the standard method and its threshold is arbitrary: too short and you flag common phrases, too long and paraphrase escapes.

Semantic. Embed both and search for near neighbours. Catches paraphrase, and has no natural threshold at all, so it produces a ranked list a human must adjudicate.

Pitfall: contamination is not binary and the report should not be either

The instinct is to report "the corpus is decontaminated". Nothing supports that claim: you removed what your method found, at the threshold you chose.

Report the method, the threshold, the count removed, and (this is the informative part) the score on the contaminated subset against the clean one. If they differ, you have measured the contamination's effect rather than asserting you removed it. If they do not, that is evidence the leakage was not doing much, which is also worth knowing.

§17 Filtering

§3 established that filters are sampling decisions. Here is what the standard ones actually select for.

The usual pipeline, and what each stage removes
  • Language identification. Removes text the classifier does not recognise, which includes code-switching, minority languages and dialects it was not trained on.
  • Heuristics. Length bounds, punctuation ratios, repetition detection, blocklists. Cheap, transparent, and they remove more than intended at the margins.
  • A quality classifier. Usually trained to distinguish a "good" reference corpus from crawled text. This is the consequential one, and it inherits every judgement in the choice of reference.
  • Deduplication. §15.
  • Contamination removal. §16.
Why the quality classifier deserves the most scrutiny and gets the least

A classifier trained to separate a reference corpus from the open web learns "resembles the reference", not "is good". If the reference is encyclopaedic and edited prose, the filter removes conversational text, regional varieties, and anything written by people whose writing does not resemble the reference, which is a sampling decision about people, made by a model, on a threshold nobody chose deliberately.

The honest procedure is cheap and rare: sample what the filter removed and read it. A hundred rejected documents, read by a person, will tell you more about your corpus than any aggregate statistic, and it is the only way to discover that the filter is removing a category you wanted.

§18 Diversity

Coverage (§3) asks whether the axes you named are represented. Diversity asks a harder question: is the data varied, along axes you did not name?

Measurements that are actually computable
  • Type–token ratio and its variants: vocabulary richness per unit length.
  • Embedding dispersion. Embed a sample and measure the mean pairwise distance, or the effective rank of Part XII §26. A corpus concentrated in one region has low effective rank.
  • Cluster occupancy. Cluster the embeddings and look at the size distribution. A corpus that is 70% one cluster is not what its size suggests.
  • Near-duplicate rate (§15), which is diversity's crudest and most reliable proxy.
Pitfall: every one of these is measured through a model

Embedding dispersion measures diversity as represented by the embedding model. Two documents that model considers similar are similar by its lights, and its lights come from its own training corpus.

So a diversity metric computed with an embedding model trained on English web text will report low diversity for a corpus it represents poorly, which is the opposite of the truth and points you at removing exactly the material you were short of.

This is a general hazard with model-based data metrics and it has no clean fix. Use several instruments, prefer the model-free ones where they exist, and treat a surprising number as a hypothesis rather than a finding.

§19 Writing it down

Go and read

Gebru et al. (2021), Datasheets for datasets. The template this section follows. Read it for the questions rather than the format, particularly the ones about collection and about what the dataset should not be used for, which are the sections most often left blank and the ones that would have prevented the most trouble.

The last instrument is not a measurement. It is documentation, and it is the one that makes the others usable by anyone else (including you, later).

What a dataset document has to contain
  • Motivation. What was it built for? What was it not built for?
  • Composition. How many records, of what, from where, over what period.
  • Collection. The frame (§2), stated explicitly. How records were sampled. What was excluded and by what rule.
  • Labels. Who annotated, under what guideline, with what agreement (§5). Whether the label is a proxy (§6) and for what.
  • Preprocessing. Every filter, with its threshold and its measured effect.
  • Provenance and licence. §32.
  • Known gaps. The most valuable section and the one most often empty.
Why this is a technical artefact, not a compliance exercise

Every failure in Part B is invisible in the data and obvious in the documentation, if the documentation exists. Selection bias is a sentence about the frame. Shortcut risk is a sentence about collection. Contamination is a sentence about what was checked.

The test of whether a dataset document is real: can someone use it to predict where your model will fail? If it lists counts and licences but does not tell a reader what is missing, it is describing the dataset without documenting it, and the "known gaps" section is where the difference shows.
