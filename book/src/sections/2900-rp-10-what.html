Part AWhat a representation is

§1 The question

In 60 seconds

Information is easy or hard to use depending on how it is written down. A representation is a way of writing an input down, and a good one is one that makes the next task easy. That is the whole subject, and the difficulty is that "the next task" is often not known when the representation is learned.

Divide 210 by 6. Now divide CCX by VI. The quantities are identical and the second is substantially harder, because the place-value system supports long division and Roman numerals do not. Nothing about the numbers changed; the notation did.

The same effect has a formal version. Inserting into a sorted list is $O(n)$ if the list is a linked list and $O(\log n)$ if it is a red–black tree: the same set of numbers, the same operation, a different cost, decided entirely by representation.

The definition, and why it is circular

A representation is a function $f$ mapping an input $\mathbf{x}$ to a vector $\mathbf{h} = f(\mathbf{x})$. A representation is good for a task if that task is easy to solve given $\mathbf{h}$.

The circularity is not a flaw in the definition; it is the actual situation. Goodness is relative to a task, so a representation learned without knowing the task is a bet about which tasks will come. The rest of this part is about how to place that bet well, and §41 is about how to know whether it paid.

§2 Every supervised network is already doing this

Before introducing anything new, notice that representation learning has been going on throughout this book without being named.

Why the last layer being linear is the whole observation

A classifier ends in a softmax over a linear map. That final layer can only draw hyperplanes. So if the network works at all, everything before the last layer has learned to place the classes in a position where hyperplanes suffice.

Classes that were tangled in the input are linearly separable in the last hidden layer, because that is the only way the network could have succeeded. The representation was not requested and no term in the loss mentions it. It is what the network had to build in order to finish with a linear map.

Part VII §7's hidden units are this, seen from the other end. This part changes only one thing: it asks what happens when you shape $\mathbf{h}$ deliberately, and when you learn it with no labels at all.

Pitfall: "the features are in the penultimate layer"

Repeated everywhere, and true only relative to the training task. A representation trained to separate a thousand object categories has been actively rewarded for discarding everything irrelevant to those categories: pose, lighting, exact position, texture detail.

If your downstream task needs one of the discarded factors, the penultimate layer is the worst place to look and an earlier layer will do better. §37 measures this. The general statement: supervised training is a representation learner that has been told exactly what to throw away, and it obeys.

§3 Three properties, and the fact that they conflict

What is asked of a representation

Informative. It retains what is needed. The limiting case, $\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{x}$, is perfectly informative and useless.

Compact. It is smaller, or sparser, or lower-rank than the input. The limiting case, $\mathbf{h} = 0$, is perfectly compact and useless.

Structured. Its coordinates mean something separately: directions correspond to factors, distances correspond to similarity, a linear model on top suffices.

Why the first two conflict, and why that is productive

Informativeness and compactness pull in opposite directions by construction, and every method in this part is a particular way of resolving the tension. An autoencoder resolves it with a bottleneck (§8) or a penalty (§10, §13). A contrastive objective resolves it by declaring which differences do not count (§19). A masked objective resolves it by requiring prediction of what was hidden (§22).

The third property is different in kind: it is not forced by any of these objectives, it is hoped for. Movement E is about finding out whether it happened, and §39 is about a decade of trying to make it happen on purpose.

§4 Distributed representations, and the counting argument

Concepts can be written down as one symbol each, or as a pattern across many features. The difference in what the two can express is not a matter of degree.

Distributed and non-distributed

A distributed representation describes an input by many features that can be set independently. $n$ binary features take $2^n$ configurations.

A non-distributed or symbolic representation assigns each input one symbol. One-hot vectors, $k$-means assignments, decision-tree leaves, $k$-nearest-neighbour templates and $n$-gram contexts are all of this kind: many entries, but no meaningful separate control over each.

The exact count for linear-threshold features: $n$ hyperplanes in general position in $\mathbb{R}^d$ cut it into $\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{n}{j} = O(n^d)$ regions (the figure's left panel). The general-position count goes back to Schläfli; Zaslavsky's arrangement theorem is the later generalisation to hyperplanes in any position, general position being the special case that maximises the count.

Pitfall: the exponent is on the wrong symbol

The advantage is routinely stated as "exponential in the number of features". It is not. The count $O(n^d)$ is polynomial in $n$, the number of features, and exponential in $d$, the input dimension.

The figure shows it: going from 20 features to 40 in ten dimensions multiplies the regions by about 1,980, large, and nothing like $2^{20}$. The $2^n$ figure counts configurations of the code, and most of them are never realised, because $n$ hyperplanes cannot carve $2^n$ regions out of a $d$-dimensional space once $n > d$.

The correct statement is still overwhelming and it is worth stating correctly.

Why generalisation is the real point, not the count

Counting regions is the argument's setup. The consequence is about sharing.

As symbols, cat and dog are exactly as far apart as cat and Tuesday: one-hot vectors are equidistant, all of them. Give them distributed codes with entries that mean something (has-fur, has-legs, is-animate) and what is learned about one transfers to the other for free, because they share entries.

This is why the feature that mattered most for language was learned representations of words rather than indices, and it is the mechanism behind every embedding in movement D. A distributed representation induces a similarity space. A symbolic one does not have one.

§5 The manifold assumption

In 60 seconds

Data does not fill its space. Images of faces occupy a vanishing fraction of the space of pixel arrays, and that fraction is connected and low-dimensional. If that is true, a representation's job is to find coordinates on that region rather than in the space it sits inside.

The assumption, and the evidence for it

Probability mass concentrates on a small number of low-dimensional connected regions (manifolds) embedded in a much higher-dimensional ambient space.

The informal evidence is decisive: sample a $256\times256$ RGB image uniformly at random and you get noise, every time, for as long as you care to try. Natural images are a set of measure approximately zero. The formal evidence is that you can move continuously between two faces through images that all look like faces, which is what "connected" means.

The tangent space at a point is the set of directions you can move while staying on the manifold: for an image, the directions corresponding to small translations, rotations, changes of pose or lighting. Its dimension is the manifold's dimension, and it is far smaller than the number of pixels.

Why this reframes the whole part

If the assumption holds, a good representation is a coordinate system on the manifold: sensitive to the directions along it, insensitive to the directions off it.

Read that sentence twice, because it is the design brief for movement B. An autoencoder's bottleneck forces coordinates. A denoising objective (§11) explicitly teaches the map from off-manifold back to on. A contractive penalty (§13) explicitly asks for insensitivity in the off-manifold directions. Three methods that look unrelated are three ways of stating the same requirement.

§6 Why local methods are not enough

If the data lies on a manifold, one could try to map it directly: build a graph on nearest neighbours, estimate a tangent plane in each neighbourhood, and stitch the patches into a global coordinate system. This is classical manifold learning, and understanding why it was not enough explains why the rest of this part exists.

Why interpolating between neighbours fails

A non-parametric method generalises by interpolating between nearby training examples. So it needs enough examples to cover every twist of the manifold, and the number required grows with how much the manifold bends, not with its dimension.

Take one image and translate it by one pixel at a time. This traces a one-dimensional curve through a 784-dimensional space. Watch a single pixel as the image slides past: its value goes up and down once for every stripe of brightness in the image. A texture with fifty stripes gives fifty oscillations, in one coordinate, along a one-dimensional manifold.

So the manifold's dimension is one and its curvature is enormous, and a local method needs examples in proportion to the curvature. That is the curse of dimensionality reappearing where low dimension had seemed to remove it.

The way out is to represent the manifold with a parametric function that shares parameters across all locations, so that what is learned about translation at one point applies at every other. That is what a neural encoder is, and it is the reason the field went this way.

Where movement A leaves you

Four commitments, and the rest of the part is consequences.

A representation is good relative to a task, so learning one without labels is a bet.

Distributed beats symbolic, because sharing entries is what makes generalisation to new combinations possible at all.

Data lies on manifolds, so the job is a coordinate system on one.

The coordinate system has to be a shared parametric function, because local interpolation cannot cover the curvature.
