PracticeDrills

Thirty-nine problems. This part is mostly about measuring distributions, so most of these build an instrument and then find the case where it reports something untrue.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · What a sampler costs

A diffusion sampler taking 50 steps, on a model whose forward pass costs 20 ms. Write down the time for one image, and for a thousand.

Answer. 1 second and 17 minutes. The sampler multiplies the model's cost by the step count, which is why every distillation and few-step method, Part XIX §29, is aimed at exactly this number and why a 20× reduction in steps matters more than a 20% faster model. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

WarmW1 · The three things

Name the three things a generative model might give you, and say which a GAN gives.

Answer. Sampling, density evaluation, inference. A GAN gives sampling only: no density, so no anomaly detection, no model comparison, no likelihood for itself.
WarmW2 · Why $Z$ is the problem

Write down $Z$ and say why it cannot be computed once and reused.

Answer. $Z(\theta) = \int \tilde{p}(\mathbf{x};\theta)\,d\mathbf{x}$ over the whole space. It depends on $\theta$, so it changes at every gradient step.
WarmW3 · Positive and negative phases

Give both terms of an energy-based model's log-likelihood gradient and say which is expensive.

Answer. Push energy down at the data; push it up wherever the model puts mass. The second is an expectation under the model, so it needs samples from a model that is currently wrong, at every step.
WarmW4 · Which failure from which KL

Match blurry samples and mode collapse to the two KL directions, and explain each.

Answer. Forward KL (maximum likelihood) is infinite where data has mass and the model has none, so it covers, and hedging between sharp alternatives is blur. Reverse KL is infinite where the model has mass and the data has none, so it may abandon regions: collapse.
WarmW5 · The score

Why does $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p$ not contain $Z$?

Answer. $Z$ is a function of $\theta$ and not of $\mathbf{x}$, so its gradient with respect to $\mathbf{x}$ is exactly zero. Not approximated, differentiated away.
WarmW6 · Why one noise level cannot work

State the two failures at the two extremes.

Answer. Small $\sigma$: the target is nearly right and almost none of the space gets training signal, so a sampler cannot start. Large $\sigma$: coverage is good and the target is the score of a badly blurred distribution. Use every level at once.
WarmW7 · The sampling asymmetry

Why is autoregressive sampling slow, and why does more hardware not fix it?

Answer. Position $i+1$ needs $x_i$, which does not exist until position $i$ is sampled. It is a data dependency in the model's definition, not a shortage of arithmetic. Caching lowers the constant; the chain remains.
WarmW8 · What the ELBO's gap is

Name it exactly, and say why it cannot be measured directly.

Answer. $D_{KL}(q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) \parallel p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}))$. It needs the true posterior, which is the object $q$ was introduced to avoid.
WarmW9 · Why looking at samples fails

A model produces 5,000 flawless samples. What can you still not rule out?

Answer. That it dropped most of the distribution. Every collapsed sample is a real mode. Detecting absence requires remembering what should be there, which is what a human inspecting a grid cannot do.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Both KL directions

Fit a single Gaussian to a well-separated bimodal target, minimising each direction. Report the fitted mean and standard deviation for both.

Answer. Forward centres between the modes and stretches; reverse locks onto one and stays narrow. The forward fit has a closed form (match the first two moments), and the reverse needs a search.
CoreC2 · Energy and shift invariance

Build a density from an energy on a grid. Then add 137 to every energy and confirm the density is unchanged. What did $Z$ do?

Answer. $Z$ absorbed it exactly, by a factor $e^{-137}$. Energies are defined only up to a constant, which is why an energy-based model can never report an absolute probability without computing $Z$.
CoreC3 · Score matching by hand

Implement the integration-by-parts objective for a 1-D model and confirm it recovers the parameters of a Gaussian from samples, without ever normalising.

Answer. It works, and counting the operations shows why it stopped working: the objective needs $\partial^2 \log p/\partial x_j^2$ for every $j$, which is $O(n)$ backward passes per example.
CoreC4 · Coverage

For a mixture in 2-D, compute the fraction of a plotted region whose density exceeds 1% of the maximum, at $\sigma \in \{0, 0.3, 0.8, 2\}$. Then say where a sampler initialised from wide noise begins.

Answer. Roughly 16%, 29%, 73%, 100%. The sampler begins in the region that at $\sigma = 0$ received no training signal at all, which is why plain score matching cannot generate.
CoreC5 · Langevin on an exact score

Run Langevin dynamics using the analytic score of a mixture with very unequal weights. Compare the recovered weights to the true ones.

Answer. The modes are found and the weights are wrong. The score between well-separated modes is near zero, so the chain carries almost no information about their relative mass. Even an exact score is not enough; the noise schedule is what fixes it.
CoreC6 · The denoising identity

Verify $(r(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{x})/\sigma^2$ against the analytic score, at several $\sigma$, reporting cosine and magnitude ratio separately against both the smoothed and unsmoothed score.

Answer. Against the smoothed score, both near 1. Against the unsmoothed one the direction survives and the magnitude does not. Reporting only cosine hides the error entirely.
CoreC7 · The forward process

Implement the cosine schedule, recover the per-step $\beta_t$ from $\bar{\alpha}_t$, and confirm that one-shot corruption at step $t$ matches applying $t$ single steps.

Answer. They match, which is the property that makes training affordable: training at step $t$ costs one draw rather than $t$.
CoreC8 · Why $\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}$

Corrupt unit-variance data with and without the scaling. Track the variance across the schedule.

Answer. With the scaling it stays at 1 and the endpoint is a standard Gaussian; without it, variance grows without bound and "start from noise" has no well-defined meaning.
CoreC9 · Train a diffusion model

On a mixture whose weights you chose, train an $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$-prediction network across the schedule and sample by ancestral sampling. Report the recovered mixing weights, not a picture.

Answer. They should match to within the sampling error of a real draw of the same size. The weights are the part a mode-seeking method gets wrong, which is why they are the number to report.
CoreC10 · The three parameterisations

Convert between $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, $\mathbf{x}_0$ and the score, and confirm each round-trips. Then plot the magnitude of each target against $t$.

Answer. The $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ target is unit-variance at every noise level, so one loss weighting works throughout; the $\mathbf{x}_0$ target's difficulty varies enormously with $t$. That is the whole argument for the usual choice.
CoreC11 · Fewer steps

Sample the same model with ancestral sampling at $T$ steps and deterministically at $T/10$. Compare recovered weights and wall clock.

Answer. Similar distribution, an order of magnitude less time. The deterministic sampler also makes the map from noise to sample invertible, which is what gives diffusion an interpolatable latent space at all.
CoreC12 · Guidance, swept

Train a class-conditional model with label dropout. Sweep the guidance weight and report three things: adherence to the requested class, mean log-density under the truth and within-mode spread.

Answer. Adherence saturates early. Density peaks and then collapses, while the spread grows: past the peak the samples are not more concentrated, they have left the distribution. "Reduce $w$ if samples look too similar" describes a symptom that does not occur.
CoreC13 · The ELBO's gap

Train VAEs across a range of $\beta$. For each, report the ELBO and an importance-weighted estimate of $\log p(\mathbf{x})$ at large $k$. Find a pair the two rank differently.

Answer. They exist, and the part's figure is one. The bound is loose by different amounts for different models, so comparing bounds compares the encoders as much as the generative models.
CoreC14 · Posterior collapse

Report the KL per latent dimension across your $\beta$ sweep, and count the dimensions with KL near zero. Then check whether the ELBO flagged the problem.

Answer. Dimensions go dead and the ELBO does not obviously worsen. The per-dimension KL is the detection, and it is one line that almost nobody logs.
CoreC15 · Reparameterisation against REINFORCE

Estimate the same gradient both ways and compare the variance across 1,000 draws.

Answer. Orders of magnitude apart. Reparameterisation uses the derivative of the thing being averaged; the score-function estimator uses only its value. That gap is why VAEs use continuous latents essentially always.
CoreC16 · The optimal discriminator

On a 1-D grid with two known densities, compute $d^*$ in closed form, substitute it into the GAN objective, and confirm you get the Jensen–Shannon divergence up to a constant.

Answer. You do. The discriminator is estimating a divergence the loss cannot compute: a learned loss function, which is why GANs produced sharp images while maximum-likelihood models produced blurry ones.
CoreC17 · The vanishing gradient

Plot the gradient of both generator losses against $d(g(\mathbf{z}))$ as it approaches zero.

Answer. The saturating loss's gradient vanishes exactly when the discriminator is winning; the non-saturating one's does not. Note the sequel: C18 finds this is not what causes mode collapse.
CoreC18 · Where collapse comes from

Train a GAN on eight modes in a ring under four conditions: standard, saturating loss, discriminator over-trained 5:1, and Adam $\beta_1 = 0.9$. Three seeds each. Count modes produced.

Answer. The saturating loss, the textbook culprit, is fine. What collapses it is the balance between the players. This is consistent with which fixes worked historically: they controlled the discriminator rather than changing what the loss measures.
CoreC19 · Likelihood against quality

Build two models of a known mixture: one that drops a mode, one that is correct plus 20% broad noise. Report test log-likelihood and the fraction of plausible samples for each.

Answer. The mode-dropper has the worst likelihood and the best samples; the noisy model has nearly the best likelihood and visibly wrong samples. The two measurements disagree in both directions, by construction.
CoreC20 · Precision and recall

Implement both for distributions, from samples, and run them on C19's two models.

Answer. High precision and low recall for the dropper; the reverse for the noisy one. The pair separates exactly what a single distance conflates (Part XIV §3's lesson, arriving in a new setting).
CoreC21 · Preprocessing changes the number

Compute a model's test log-likelihood, then multiply every input by 0.1 and recompute.

Answer. It rises by $n\log 10$. Densities are not invariant to a change of units, so two likelihoods are comparable only under identical preprocessing, which is why researchers once shared the exact binarised dataset file.
CoreC22 · The memorisation check

Build a "model" that samples uniformly from the training set. Give it your full evaluation suite.

Answer. Perfect sample quality, excellent precision, plausible recall, and a nearest-training-distance of exactly zero. Only the last one catches it, and it is the check most often omitted.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · The schedule matters more than the model

Train the same architecture with linear and cosine schedules at matched compute. Compare recovered weights. Then vary the width instead, by the same factor of effort.

Answer. The schedule usually wins. In this family the corruption process is part of the model, and a field trained on architecture search took several years to internalise that.
StretchS2 · Flow matching

Train a velocity field along straight paths from noise to data, and sample with a fixed-step solver. Compare to diffusion at matched step counts.

Answer. Comparable quality, usually fewer steps and a much simpler derivation with no Markov chain and no schedule. Worth doing after C9 so the relationship is visible rather than asserted.
StretchS3 · Contrastive divergence, and its bias

Train a small energy-based model with $k$-step contrastive divergence for $k \in \{1, 5, 100\}$. Compare the learned density to the truth.

Answer. Small $k$ trains fast and converges to the wrong thing. This is the compromise that made Boltzmann machines trainable and kept them from being correct (§7's mixing problem, paid for in bias instead of time).
StretchS4 · Mixing, measured

Run a Gibbs sampler on a mixture and measure the time to cross between modes as you increase their separation.

Answer. It grows exponentially with the depth of the valley. Then note the consequence: a better-trained model has sharper modes, so the method's difficulty grows with its own success.
StretchS5 · Latent diffusion, in miniature

Train a small autoencoder, then a diffusion model in its latent space. Compare against diffusion directly in data space at matched compute.

Answer. The two-stage version is far cheaper per step and its quality ceiling is set by the autoencoder. This is the architecture behind most deployed image generators, and the split is exactly Part XV's compression followed by this part's density model.
StretchS6 · Nucleus sampling changes the distribution

For a trained autoregressive model, measure the total variation between the model's distribution and what top-$p$ actually samples from, at several $p$.

Answer. Substantial and deliberate. The model assigns more mass to the tail than we want realised, and standard practice overrules it, which is a strange position for a family whose selling point is an exact likelihood.
StretchS7 · FID's sample-count bias

Compute FID between two disjoint halves of the same real dataset, at sample counts from 1,000 to 50,000.

Answer. It is not zero and it falls steadily with sample count. Two FIDs at different counts are not comparable, and the true answer for identical distributions is 0.
