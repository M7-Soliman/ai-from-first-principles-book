Part CThe objective, and where to start

§9 The cost function is a log-likelihood

In 60 seconds

You do not choose a loss function for a network. You choose what distribution the output represents, and the loss is its negative log-likelihood. Part II §26 established this; this section notes that nothing about depth changes it.

$$J(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\hat{p}_{\text{data}}}\big[\log p_{\text{model}}(y \mid \mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\big]$$

That is Part II's maximum likelihood, with the model now being a network instead of a linear map. The derivation is identical because it never depended on the model's form, which is exactly why the same loss serves logistic regression and a fifty-layer network.

§10 Why cross-entropy and not squared error

For classification you could, in principle, use squared error on the sigmoid output. It is a legitimate loss and it trains badly. The reason is worth seeing, because it is the clearest case in this book of a loss function's gradient mattering more than its shape.

Worked: where the difference comes from

With $a = \sigma(z)$ and target $y = 1$:

Squared error $L = (a-1)^2$. By the chain rule, $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z} = 2(a-1)\cdot\sigma'(z) = 2(a-1)\,a(1-a)$.

That $a(1-a)$ factor is the sigmoid's derivative, and Part 0 §18 showed it collapses to zero when the unit saturates. A confidently wrong unit has $a \approx 0$, so the gradient is $\approx 0$; the unit is stuck.

Cross-entropy $L = -\log a$. Working through, $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z} = a - 1$.

The $\sigma'$ factor cancels exactly. A confidently wrong unit has gradient $\approx -1$: the largest correction available. The log in the loss undoes the exponential in the sigmoid, which is not a coincidence; it is why the pairing in §6's table is the right one.

Which statistic of the answer are you asking for?

There is a second, less obvious consequence of choosing a loss, and it is one of the most useful facts in this part. Section 9 said the loss follows from the distribution you claim the output has. But suppose you do not want a whole distribution; suppose you just want a number. Which number you get is decided entirely by the loss.

The two you will meet

Minimising squared error over all possible functions gives, at each input, the mean of $y$ given $\mathbf{x}$:

$$f^*(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}\big[y \mid \mathbf{x}\big]$$

Minimising absolute error gives the median instead.

Both follow from a short optimisation argument. The value $c$ minimising $\mathbb{E}[(y-c)^2]$ is the mean: differentiate and set to zero and you get $c = \mathbb{E}[y]$ immediately. The value minimising $\mathbb{E}[|y-c|]$ is the median, because the derivative of $|y-c|$ is $\pm 1$ and the total balances when half the mass sits either side.

Your network is doing this separately at every input. It is not fitting a curve through the data; it is estimating a summary statistic of the conditional distribution, and the loss chooses which summary.

Why this explains blurry generated images

Train a model to predict the next video frame under squared error. For a genuinely ambiguous input there are many plausible next frames, and the model returns the average of them, because that is what squared error asks for.

The average of several sharp images is a blurry image. Not a slightly worse image: a qualitatively different kind of object, one that may resemble no plausible frame at all. Part II §18's mixture drill made exactly this point: the mean of a bimodal distribution can sit where the distribution has essentially no mass.

So the blurriness so often blamed on model capacity is frequently the loss doing precisely what it was asked. It is also why generative models in Part XVI abandon per-pixel squared error entirely: if you want a sample rather than an average, no choice of network fixes a loss that requests the average.

Two practical corollaries. Absolute error gives the conditional median, which for images is usually sharper, a real and cheap improvement. And when the conditional distribution is genuinely multimodal, the honest move is to model the distribution rather than a summary of it, which is what a mixture density output does.

§11 Initialisation

In 60 seconds

Weights cannot start at zero; every unit would compute the same thing forever. They cannot be large or small either: the forward signal is multiplied once per layer, so any systematic scaling compounds and either explodes or dies. The fix is to scale the initial weights by $1/\sqrt{n_{\text{in}}}$, which keeps the variance roughly constant with depth.

Go and read

Glorot & Bengio (2010), Understanding the difficulty of training deep feedforward neural networks. The variance argument of this section, in its original form, and note the title, which is a fair description of what the field was doing at the time. Then He et al. (2015) for the factor of two that ReLU requires. Part IX §16 returns to both, because the derivation here uses only the forward pass and there is a second half.

Intuition: keep the variance from compounding

A unit sums $n$ incoming terms. If each weight has variance $\sigma^2$ and each input has variance 1, the sum has variance $n\sigma^2$. To keep that at 1 you need $\sigma^2 = 1/n$, so weights scale as $1/\sqrt{n_{\text{in}}}$.

With ReLU, roughly half the units are zero at any moment, which halves the variance. The correction is a factor of two: $\sigma^2 = 2/n$. That is the whole derivation of what is usually called He initialisation, and it is the reason the number 2 appears in it.

Pitfall: zero initialisation breaks symmetry

Initialise every weight to zero and every unit in a layer computes the identical function, receives the identical gradient, and updates identically, forever. The layer behaves as a single unit no matter how wide it is.

Biases may safely start at zero, because the incoming weights already differ. It is only the weight matrices that need randomness, and its purpose is symmetry breaking rather than anything statistical.

Part DBackpropagation

§12 Computational graphs

In 60 seconds

Any expression can be drawn as a graph: nodes are operations, edges carry values. Once you have that graph, differentiating is mechanical: attach a local derivative to each edge and multiply along paths. Backpropagation is that procedure, organised so shared work is computed once.

It is worth being explicit about what redrawing an expression as a graph buys, because on first sight it looks like notation rather than progress.

The problem with differentiating a neural network is not that any individual step is hard. It is that the composed expression is enormous: fifty layers of matrix multiplications and nonlinearities, written out in full, is a formula nobody could hold, let alone differentiate by inspection. Attacking it as one expression is hopeless.

The graph dissolves that difficulty by refusing to form the expression at all. Every node is a single elementary operation (an add, a multiply, a sigmoid) whose derivative is something you learned in Part 0 §16 and could write down in a second. The complexity was never in the operations; it was in their composition, and composition is exactly what the chain rule already knows how to handle.

Intuition: the question changes shape

Before the graph, the question is how do I differentiate this enormous function? After it, the question is how do I walk this graph?

That is a much better question, because walking a graph is mechanical. Each node needs to know only two things: its own local derivative, and the gradient arriving from the node in front. Neither requires any knowledge of the network as a whole; a node does not know whether it sits in layer 2 or layer 50, and does not need to.

This is why a framework can differentiate an architecture nobody has invented yet. It never analyses your model. It only ever composes local rules, and locality is what makes the procedure indifferent to what you built.

§13 Forward mode and reverse mode

Go and read

Baydin, Pearlmutter, Radul & Siskind (2018), Automatic differentiation in machine learning: a survey. The clearest modern account of what this section covers and what frameworks actually do with it, including the distinction, routinely muddled, between automatic differentiation, symbolic differentiation and numerical differencing. Read it before attempting §21's engine, not after.

There are two ways to apply the chain rule to a graph, and choosing correctly is worth a factor of a million.

Forward mode propagates derivatives with respect to one input forward through the graph. One pass gives you $\partial(\text{everything})/\partial x_i$, the effect of one input on all outputs.

Reverse mode propagates derivatives of one output backward. One pass gives you $\partial L/\partial(\text{everything})$, the effect of all inputs on one output.

Intuition: count the ends

The rule is simply which end is narrower. Many inputs, one output: go backward. One input, many outputs: go forward.

Training a network is the extreme case of the first: millions of parameters, one scalar loss. Reverse mode is not a clever optimisation here; it is the only tractable option, and "backpropagation" is just its name in this field.

Why one backward pass is enough

The claim in Figure 9 is strong enough to deserve an argument: a single traversal yields the derivative of the loss with respect to every parameter, however many there are. That sounds like getting a million answers for the price of one, and it is worth seeing where the saving comes from.

It comes from sharing. Consider a weight buried in the first layer. Its influence on the loss travels forward through every subsequent layer, and so does the influence of every other first-layer weight. They all traverse the same path through layers 2 onward. The chain rule says each weight's gradient is a product of factors along its route, and those routes overlap almost entirely.

Reverse mode exploits that overlap directly. Working backwards, it computes the gradient at each node once, and every parameter feeding that node then reuses it. The shared tail of the journey is computed a single time rather than once per parameter.

Intuition: distances to one city, not between all pairs

Suppose you want the distance from every town in a country to one capital.

The naive method takes each town in turn and traces its route, retracing the same motorway every time, because near the capital all routes coincide. That is forward mode: one pass per starting point, with the shared portion repeated.

The efficient method starts at the capital and works outward, recording each junction's distance once. Any town beyond that junction just adds its own last leg. That is reverse mode, and the loss is the capital.

The saving is exactly the amount of route the towns have in common, which, in a deep network where every early parameter must pass through every later layer, is nearly all of it.

Which also explains the asymmetry in the rule above. Reverse mode is efficient when there is one destination and many origins, because then everything shares. With many outputs it loses that advantage; you would need one backward pass per output, and forward mode becomes the better choice. Training has exactly one output, the scalar loss, and that single fact is what makes the whole enterprise affordable.

§14 Backpropagation, derived

Now the derivation. For a network of layers, write $\mathbf{z}^{(k)} = W^{(k)}\mathbf{a}^{(k-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(k)}$ and $\mathbf{a}^{(k)} = g(\mathbf{z}^{(k)})$.

Define the quantity that does all the work, the gradient of the loss with respect to a layer's pre-activation:

$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(k)}}$$
The four equations

1. At the output. With the matched pairing of §6 (softmax or sigmoid with cross-entropy), this is simply

$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)} = \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}$$

2. Propagate backward. Push through the weights, then through the activation:

$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)} = \big(W^{(k+1)\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k+1)}\big) \odot g'\big(\mathbf{z}^{(k)}\big)$$

3. Weight gradients.

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(k)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)} \mathbf{a}^{(k-1)\top}$$

4. Bias gradients.

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(k)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)}$$

That is the entire algorithm. Everything else is bookkeeping.

Where the four equations come from

They are not postulated. Each is the chain rule applied to a specific question, and the only technique involved is one you have had since Part 0 §17: to find how something affects the loss, sum over every route by which it does. Take them in order of difficulty rather than in the order they are numbered.

One preliminary. Everything below hinges on knowing which quantities a given variable can reach. Look at the two lines defining a layer:

$$\mathbf{z}^{(k)} = W^{(k)}\mathbf{a}^{(k-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(k)}, \qquad \mathbf{a}^{(k)} = g\big(\mathbf{z}^{(k)}\big)$$

and note that $b^{(k)}_i$ and the weights in row $i$ of $W^{(k)}$ influence only $z^{(k)}_i$, one component, not the whole vector. That is why the sums below collapse to single terms, and it is the entire reason these equations are simple.

Equation 4: the bias

Ask how $b^{(k)}_i$ reaches the loss. Its only route is through $z^{(k)}_i$, so the chain rule is a product of two factors rather than a sum over many:

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(k)}_i} = \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z^{(k)}_i}\cdot\dfrac{\partial z^{(k)}_i}{\partial b^{(k)}_i}$

The first factor is $\delta^{(k)}_i$, by the definition we just made. The second is the derivative of $\;W^{(k)}\mathbf{a}^{(k-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(k)}\;$ with respect to a bias that appears in it exactly once, added, so it is 1.

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(k)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)}$$

The bias gradient is $\delta$. Nothing was computed; $\delta$ was already the answer, which is why it was worth defining.

Equation 3: the weights

Same question for $W^{(k)}_{ij}$, the weight from input $j$ into unit $i$. Again it reaches the loss only through $z^{(k)}_i$:

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(k)}_{ij}} = \delta^{(k)}_i \cdot \dfrac{\partial z^{(k)}_i}{\partial W^{(k)}_{ij}}$

Now $z^{(k)}_i = \sum_n W^{(k)}_{in} a^{(k-1)}_n + b^{(k)}_i$. Differentiating that sum with respect to one particular $W^{(k)}_{ij}$ kills every term except $n = j$, leaving the coefficient it multiplied:

$\dfrac{\partial z^{(k)}_i}{\partial W^{(k)}_{ij}} = a^{(k-1)}_j$

So $\;\partial L/\partial W^{(k)}_{ij} = \delta^{(k)}_i\, a^{(k-1)}_j$, an entry indexed by $i$ from one vector times an entry indexed by $j$ from another, which is precisely the outer product of Part I §6:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(k)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)}\,\mathbf{a}^{(k-1)\top}$$

Read it as a sentence: how much this weight matters is how wrong its output was, times how active its input was. A weight carrying a large signal into a badly wrong unit gets a large gradient; a weight whose input was zero gets none at all, whatever the error, which is the mechanism behind the dead unit of §19.

Equation 2: propagating backward

The only one requiring a genuine sum. Ask how $z^{(k)}_i$ reaches the loss. It first becomes $a^{(k)}_i = g(z^{(k)}_i)$, and that single activation is then fed to every unit of the next layer. So there are many routes, and the chain rule sums over all of them:

$\delta^{(k)}_i = \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z^{(k)}_i} = \sum_j \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z^{(k+1)}_j}\cdot\dfrac{\partial z^{(k+1)}_j}{\partial z^{(k)}_i} = \sum_j \delta^{(k+1)}_j \cdot \dfrac{\partial z^{(k+1)}_j}{\partial z^{(k)}_i}$

For the remaining factor, expand the next layer and apply the chain rule once more: through the weight, then through the activation:

$z^{(k+1)}_j = \sum_n W^{(k+1)}_{jn}\, g\big(z^{(k)}_n\big) + b^{(k+1)}_j \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{\partial z^{(k+1)}_j}{\partial z^{(k)}_i} = W^{(k+1)}_{ji}\; g'\big(z^{(k)}_i\big)$

Substituting, and pulling $g'(z^{(k)}_i)$ out of the sum because it does not depend on $j$:

$\delta^{(k)}_i = \Big(\sum_j W^{(k+1)}_{ji}\,\delta^{(k+1)}_j\Big)\, g'\big(z^{(k)}_i\big)$

That bracket sums over the first index of $W$ while the free index $i$ is the second, which is exactly what $W^\top$ means. Hence:

$$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)} = \big(W^{(k+1)\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k+1)}\big) \odot g'\big(\mathbf{z}^{(k)}\big)$$

The transpose was not a choice. It fell out of the index bookkeeping, and it means what Part I §6's box said it means: the same wires, read from the far end. Gradients travel back through the connections they came forward through.

Equation 1 needs no work here, because §10 already did it. There we found that pairing a sigmoid output with cross-entropy makes the $\sigma'$ factor cancel exactly, leaving $\partial L/\partial z = a - y$. The same cancellation happens for softmax with cross-entropy, giving $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)} = \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}$, the prediction minus the truth, and nothing else.

Why the derivation is worth having done once

Three things become visible that the equations alone conceal.

Why $\delta$ exists. It is not a notational convenience. Equations 3 and 4 turn out to be trivial given $\delta$, so the whole problem reduces to computing one vector per layer. Defining the right intermediate quantity is most of the work, and it is the move you would have to reinvent for any new architecture.

Why only equation 2 has a sum. Weights and biases each touch one unit, so their routes are unique. An activation is broadcast to the entire next layer, so its routes are many. The presence or absence of a sum is a fact about the wiring, readable off the graph before any calculus.

Why the two factors in equation 2 are the two failure modes. The derivation produces exactly $W^\top$ and $g'$, one from the wiring and one from the nonlinearity, and §18 shows that repeated multiplication by either is what makes gradients vanish or explode. The problem was visible in the derivation before anyone trained a deep network.

Intuition: read equation 2

It says the gradient arriving at layer $k$ is the gradient at layer $k+1$, sent back through the same weights it came forward through (transposed, because it is travelling the other way), and then multiplied by how responsive this layer's activation was.

Both factors are the whole story of §18. Multiply by $W^\top$ repeatedly and the scale depends on the weights. Multiply by $g'$ repeatedly and the scale depends on the activation. Depth means doing both many times, and products of many factors do not stay put.

History: invented repeatedly, connected slowly

Part 0 §24 noted that reverse-mode differentiation was arrived at independently several times. The detail worth adding here is the gap.

Seppo Linnainmaa published the general method in 1970. Paul Werbos proposed applying it to networks in his 1974 thesis. It became widely known only through a 1986 paper by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams.

So the mathematical technique existed for sixteen years while the field that needed it most sat in a winter caused by not having it. The obstacle was never the mathematics; it was that the two communities did not overlap and neural networks were unfashionable enough that a result about them could be published and not read.

§15 A whole network, by hand

Part 0 §24 did one unit. Here is a two-layer network, with the same discipline.

Worked: forward and backward, with numbers

Setup. Two inputs, two hidden ReLU units, one sigmoid output. Weights are stored output by input, the convention of §14 and of Part I §8, so each row of $W^{(1)}$ is one hidden unit's incoming weights.

$W^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{b}^{(1)} = (0, -1)$, $W^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$, $b^{(2)} = 0.5$

Input $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)$, target $y = 1$.

Forward.

$\mathbf{z}^{(1)} = W^{(1)}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)} = (1(1) + 0.5(2),\; -1(1) + 2(2)) + (0,-1) = (2,\; 2)$

$\mathbf{a}^{(1)} = \text{ReLU}(2, 2) = (2, 2)$, both units active.

$z^{(2)} = 1.5(2) + (-1)(2) + 0.5 = 1.5$

$\hat{y} = \sigma(1.5) = 0.8176$

$L = -\log(0.8176) = 0.2014$

Backward. Equation 1:

$\delta^{(2)} = \hat{y} - y = 0.8176 - 1 = -0.1824$

Equation 3 and 4 at the output layer:

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(2)}} = \delta^{(2)}\mathbf{a}^{(1)} = -0.1824 \times (2,2) = (-0.3648,\ -0.3648)$

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(2)}} = -0.1824$

Equation 2, propagating back. ReLU's derivative is 1 where $z > 0$, and both are:

$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} = \big(W^{(2)\top}\delta^{(2)}\big) \odot (1,1) = (1.5,\ -1)\times(-0.1824) = (-0.2736,\ 0.1824)$

And the first-layer weights, by equation 3:

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(1)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}^\top = \begin{bmatrix} -0.2736 \\ 0.1824 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.2736 & -0.5472 \\ 0.1824 & 0.3648 \end{bmatrix}$

Check the shape against the invariant below: $W^{(1)}$ is $2\times2$ and so is its gradient. Row $i$ of that matrix is how the loss responds to hidden unit $i$'s incoming weights, which is $\delta^{(1)}_i$ scaled by each input in turn.

Sanity check. The output was 0.82 against a target of 1, so the network should increase its output. $\partial L/\partial b^{(2)}$ is negative, so gradient descent increases $b^{(2)}$, which raises $z^{(2)}$ and raises $\hat{y}$. Correct.

Watch the shapes

$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)}$ always has the shape of $\mathbf{z}^{(k)}$. $\partial L/\partial W^{(k)}$ always has the shape of $W^{(k)}$. Those two invariants catch nearly every backpropagation bug you will write, and they are why §0.3 asked you to track shapes in both directions.

§16 What a framework actually does

Nobody writes §14's equations for each new architecture. Frameworks apply reverse-mode automatic differentiation to whatever graph you build, and it is worth knowing that this is neither symbolic differentiation nor numerical approximation.

ApproachWhat it doesProblem
Numericalfinite differencesone pass per parameter, and inaccurate (Part III §3)
Symbolicmanipulates formulasexpressions blow up in size
Automaticapplies the chain rule numerically at each nodenone of the above

Automatic differentiation gets exact derivatives (to floating point) at a cost proportional to the forward pass, by never forming a symbolic expression and never approximating. Each operation knows its own local derivative; the framework composes them.

What the graph is made of, and why it matters

There are two ways to build the backward pass, and the difference decides what else the framework can do.

Symbol-to-number
Take the graph and a set of input values, and return numbers: the gradients. This is what the engine of §21 does, and what a reader would naturally write.
Symbol-to-symbol
Take the graph and return another graph: the one that computes the gradient. The numbers come later, when you run it.

The second sounds like an indirection with no benefit, and it has one large one. If the derivative is itself a graph, you can feed it back into the same machinery and differentiate it again. Higher derivatives cost nothing extra in machinery, because there is no separate mechanism; differentiation is closed over its own output.

Higher-order derivatives, without ever forming the Hessian

Which resolves a problem Part III raised and left open. Section 12 there established that Newton's method needs the Hessian, that an $n$-parameter model has an $n \times n$ one, and that at $n = 10^6$ this is $10^{12}$ numbers, several terabytes, rebuilt every iteration. The conclusion was that curvature is unaffordable at scale.

That conclusion is right about the matrix and wrong about the curvature, because almost no algorithm actually needs $H$. What they need is $H\mathbf{v}$ (the Hessian applied to one direction), and that is available for the price of a couple of backward passes:

$$H\mathbf{v} = \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} f \cdot \mathbf{v}\big)$$
Intuition: read it right to left

Take the gradient. Dot it with a fixed direction $\mathbf{v}$, giving a scalar, the rate of change of $f$ along $\mathbf{v}$. Now differentiate that scalar with respect to the parameters.

You get a vector: how the directional derivative changes as the parameters move, which is exactly the Hessian's action on $\mathbf{v}$. And because the intermediate quantity was a scalar, §13's argument applies: reverse mode delivers its full gradient in one pass.

Cost: about two backward passes. Memory: one vector. Never $n^2$ anything.

This is why second-order methods have not disappeared from deep learning despite Part III §12's objection. Conjugate gradient, Hessian-free optimisation and the Krylov methods all work by probing $H$ through products with chosen directions, never assembling it; and they are only implementable because differentiation composes with itself.

The practical trace of this in a framework is the create_graph=True argument to backward(): it says build the backward pass as a graph rather than as numbers, because I intend to differentiate it again. The default is off, since most runs never need it and building the extra graph costs memory.

§17 The cost of remembering

One consequence of §14 that surprises people: the backward pass needs the forward pass's intermediate values. Equation 3 uses $\mathbf{a}^{(k-1)}$, equation 2 uses $\mathbf{z}^{(k)}$.

So every activation must be kept alive from the forward pass until it is consumed by the backward pass, and the activation term therefore scales with depth times batch size times width. In practice it is memory rather than compute that caps model size on a given device.

One qualification, because the product above is often quoted as though it were the whole story. Equations 2 and 3 also need $W$ itself, so the weight matrices are retained too, and those do not scale with the batch. Part IV §6 measures the two terms separately and shows that on a wide model at a modest batch the parameters dominate, which is why halving the batch sometimes frees almost nothing.

Why this matters: gradient checkpointing

The standard remedy is a direct compute-for-memory trade. Store only a subset of activations, and recompute the rest during the backward pass by re-running short stretches of the forward pass.

With checkpoints spaced every $\sqrt{L}$ layers, memory drops from $O(L)$ to $O(\sqrt{L})$ at the cost of roughly one extra forward pass. That trade is what makes training very deep models on ordinary hardware possible, and it is a direct application of knowing why the backward pass needs what it needs.
