Part DDistillation

§24 The idea

Where this comes from

The source book has this, as model compression: a large model's size is often needed only to prevent overfitting during training, so once it has fitted a function, generate as much data as you like by evaluating it and train a smaller model to match.

That framing is exactly right and it undersells the mechanism. The refinement, train the small model to match the large one's full output distribution rather than its answer, is what made it work, and §25 is why.

The construction

A teacher and a student. The student is trained on

$$\mathcal{L} = (1-\alpha)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{hard}}(y, \hat{y}) \;+\; \alpha\, T^2 \cdot D_{KL}\big(p_T^{\text{teacher}} \,\|\, p_T^{\text{student}}\big)$$

where $p_T$ is the softmax at temperature $T$. The $T^2$ factor compensates for the gradient magnitudes shrinking as $T$ grows, so $\alpha$ means the same thing at different temperatures.

§25 Why soft targets carry more

Go and read

Hinton, Vinyals & Dean (2015), Distilling the knowledge in a neural network. Short, and its framing is the part that transfers: a teacher's distribution over the wrong answers is a statement about the structure of the problem that the label does not contain. That is also why distillation needs unlabelled data rather than labelled.

The information is in the wrong answers

A hard label says "this is a 7". The teacher's distribution says "this is a 7, and it looks somewhat like a 1, and nothing like an 8". That relative ordering over the incorrect classes is a statement about the structure of the problem that the label does not contain.

So a soft target carries more bits per example, and the gradient it produces has lower variance: every class contributes rather than one. Both effects mean the student needs fewer examples, which is the practical claim.

The name for this is the dark knowledge in the teacher's output, and it explains the otherwise odd fact that a student can be trained on data with no labels at all: the teacher supplies the targets, so distillation needs unlabelled data rather than labelled.

§26 Temperature

What it controls

A trained teacher is confident, so its distribution is nearly one-hot and the dark knowledge is numerically tiny. Raising $T$ flattens it and makes the relative sizes of the small probabilities visible to the loss.

Too low and the soft target is a hard target. Too high and it approaches uniform, which carries nothing. Values between 2 and 5 are typical, and it is worth sweeping, since the right value depends on how confident the teacher is (Part XIV §5's calibration, deciding a hyperparameter).

§27 What is actually transferred

Three variants, transferring different things

Response distillation matches the output distribution. Simple, architecture-agnostic, and what the term usually means.

Feature distillation matches intermediate representations, which requires the shapes to correspond or a learned projection between them. More signal, more constraint.

Sequence distillation, for generative models, trains the student on the teacher's generated outputs rather than on its per-token distributions, which sidesteps exposure bias (Part XVI §12) by making the student's training distribution the teacher's output distribution.

§28 Self-distillation

A puzzle worth sitting with

Distil a model into a student of the identical architecture and the student is often better than the teacher. Nothing was compressed, no new data was used, and the result improves.

The explanations proposed are partial. The soft targets are a form of label smoothing, which regularises. They also encode which examples are ambiguous, so the student is not pushed to be confident where the teacher was not, an implicit per-example weighting. And the student's optimisation problem is smoother, since it fits a continuous function rather than a set of hard labels.

Worth flagging as genuinely not fully understood, because it is usually presented as though it were. A technique that improves a model by training it to imitate itself is strange, and the strangeness has not been explained away.

§29 Distilling a sampler

Go and read

Salimans & Ho (2022), Progressive distillation for fast sampling of diffusion models. Read for the halving argument itself: a student trained to reproduce two teacher steps in one, then re-run as the new teacher for the next round, which is what turns a thousand-step sampler into a handful without ever retraining from scratch. The paper is also candid about what is lost, which is the source for this section's closing point about diversity.

The version that mattered most recently

Part XVI §29's diffusion sampler needs tens of network evaluations per sample. Progressive distillation trains a student to take one step where the teacher took two, then repeats, halving the step count each round, from 1,000 to a handful.

This is distillation of a procedure rather than of a function, and it is the technique that made real-time image generation possible. The same idea appears as consistency models, which train the student to map any point on the trajectory directly to its endpoint.

Note what it gives up. A few-step sampler is measurably less diverse than the many-step teacher: it has learned the teacher's typical output rather than its distribution, which is Part XVI §42's precision-and-recall trade arriving through the back door.

§30 When it works

The honest summary

Works well when a large teacher exists, unlabelled data in the domain is plentiful, and the student is not too much smaller: factors of two to ten are routine, factors of a hundred are not.

Works badly when the student lacks the capacity to represent the teacher's function at all, in which case it fits the easy part and fails the rest; and when the teacher is wrong, since distillation transfers errors faithfully and with confidence.

The comparison that is often skipped: a student distilled from a teacher against the same student trained directly on the same data. Distillation should win, and by how much is the number that says whether the teacher was worth training.
