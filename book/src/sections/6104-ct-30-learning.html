Part CLearning to act

Movement B assumed you know the dynamics. When you do not, there are three things to learn instead (the expert's behaviour, the dynamics, or the value function), and each fails in a way the others do not.

§17 Three things you could learn

And what each needs

The policy, from demonstrations. Needs an expert and no reward. Supervised learning, which is why it is by far the easiest to make work and §19 is what goes wrong.

The dynamics, then plan. Needs interaction and no expert. Sample-efficient, because a model can be trained on every transition regardless of reward, and limited by §21.

The value or policy, from reward. Part XVIII. Needs interaction and a reward function, and is the least sample-efficient of the three by a wide margin.

Deployed systems use combinations, and the combination is usually chosen by what data exists rather than by what would work best.

§18 Behavioural cloning

The simplest thing, and it mostly works

Record an expert acting, fit a policy mapping observation to action by supervised learning. No reward function, no exploration, no interaction: every technique in Parts VI to XIV applies unchanged.

It is how most deployed driving policies are trained, because fleet data is exactly this and nothing else is available at that scale. And it has one structural defect that no amount of data of the same kind repairs.

§19 Why a cloned policy drifts

What follows, practically

More expert data does not fix it, because more expert data is more of the same distribution. What fixes it is data from the states the learner visits, labelled by the expert, which requires the expert to be available during training rather than only in a log.

An intervention log is the cheap version. Every time a safety driver takes over, that is a state the policy reached and an expert action for it. Which is why disengagement data is worth disproportionately more than ordinary driving data, and why a fleet is a data engine rather than a dataset.

§20 Inverse reinforcement learning

Learning the reward instead of the policy

Infer the reward function the expert appears to be optimising, then solve for a policy against it. The appeal is generalisation: a reward transfers to a new vehicle or a new road where a cloned policy does not.

The difficulty is that the problem is badly ill-posed: many rewards explain the same behaviour, including the constant one, under which everything is optimal. Every method resolves this with an extra assumption, usually that the expert is noisily optimal in a specific way, and the recovered reward is only as meaningful as that assumption.

§21 How far a learned model can be trusted

The number that decides your architecture

How long you may roll out a learned model is set by the system, not by the model. The same accuracy that supports a sixty-step plan on a stable plant supports about ten on an unstable one, and no improvement in the model changes the exponent; it only shifts the curve down.

Which is the honest reason model-based methods are used for short-horizon control and long horizons are handled some other way. It is also why a stable inner loop is worth building before anything is learned on top: stabilising the plant does not merely make control easier, it extends how far any learned model can see.

§22 Offline reinforcement learning

Learning from a fixed log

Part XVIII §40's subject, and the setting that matters here because interaction with a real vehicle is expensive and dangerous. The characteristic failure is extrapolation: the value function is queried at actions the log never contains, over-estimates them because nothing contradicts it, and the policy moves toward exactly those.

Every method is a way of not trusting the value function off the data: constrain the policy to the log's support, penalise the value of unseen actions, or estimate uncertainty and be pessimistic. Part XXIV's calibration question, load-bearing rather than decorative.

§23 Simulation, and the gap

Four ways across, and what each assumes

Domain randomisation. Vary the simulator's parameters so widely that reality is one sample from the training distribution. Works, needs no real data, and produces a conservative policy because it must handle every variation at once.

System identification. Measure the real parameters and simulate those. Precise, and fails when the missing physics is structural rather than parametric.

Learned residuals. Simulate, then learn the correction from real data. Data-efficient and inherits §21's rollout limit.

Close the loop fast. A high-rate stabilising controller makes the gap matter less, because errors are corrected before they compound. This is movement A being worth more than it looks.

§24 What movement C establishes

Three things

Cloning fails on its own distribution shift, and more expert data does not repair it; data from the states the learner reaches does.

A learned model's usable horizon is set by the plant. Figure 6 measures error growing at 1.0667× per step on an unstable system and shrinking at 0.9651× on a stable one.

So stabilise first. A fast inner loop makes the simulation gap smaller, extends how far a learned model can be trusted, and does both without any learning at all.
