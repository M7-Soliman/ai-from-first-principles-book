Part CEstimating an effect

Movement B says when an effect is identifiable. Identification is not estimation: knowing that a quantity is a function of the observable distribution leaves the question of how to compute it from a finite sample, and the answer has failure modes of its own. This movement builds the estimators and measures where each one stops working.

§16 The randomised experiment

What randomisation actually does

Assigning treatment by a coin flip deletes the equation that produced it and replaces it with noise independent of everything else. That is $\mathrm{do}(X)$ performed physically rather than symbolically: every backdoor path is severed at the source, including paths through variables nobody thought to measure or knew existed.

This is why the randomised experiment is the standard against which everything else in this movement is judged. It does not need the graph to be right. It does not need the confounders to be measured. It does not even need them to be known.

What it still does not give you

External validity. The effect is estimated on whoever was enrolled, under the conditions of the trial. Generalising to another population is a separate assumption, and Part XIII §2's frame problem is the same issue.

Compliance. Randomising assignment is not randomising receipt. If people do not take what they were assigned, the clean quantity is the effect of being assigned, and §26's instruments are the standard route from one to the other.

Anything about individuals. A trial estimates an average. §33 is what that conceals.

An A/B test, Part XIV §25's leaderboards and the deployment machinery of Part XXI §35, is a randomised experiment, and inherits all three limitations along with the guarantee.

§17 Adjustment, and the estimator that follows

From the formula to a procedure

The backdoor formula $p(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) = \sum_z p(y \mid x, z) p(z)$ becomes, for the average effect of a binary treatment,

$$\tau = \mathbb{E}_Z\big[\, \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=1, Z] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=0, Z] \,\big]$$

which suggests an algorithm: fit a model of the outcome given treatment and covariates, predict each unit both ways, and average the difference. This is g-computation, or the outcome-model approach.

Every regression tool in this book applies to that inner model, and Part XIV's diagnostics apply to fitting it. What none of them check is whether $Z$ satisfies the backdoor criterion, which is the part that decides whether the number means anything.

Pitfall: the coefficient is not the effect

Reading the treatment coefficient off a regression of $Y$ on $T$ and $Z$ gives the right answer only when the model is linear and correctly specified with no treatment–covariate interaction. With interactions the coefficient is a weighted average of subgroup effects with weights nobody chose, and it can differ from the quantity you wanted.

Predicting both ways and averaging is barely more work and does not have this problem. Do that.

§18 The propensity score

One number instead of many

The propensity score is $e(z) = p(T = 1 \mid Z = z)$: the probability of treatment given the covariates.

Its theorem is that it is a balancing score: conditioning on $e(Z)$ alone suffices whenever conditioning on all of $Z$ would. A high-dimensional adjustment problem collapses to a one-dimensional one.

The catch is that $e(\cdot)$ must be estimated, and the guarantee is about the true score rather than your estimate of it.

§19 Matching, weighting, and what each assumes

Three ways to use the score

Matching. Pair each treated unit with a control of similar score and compare within pairs. Transparent, discards data, and the estimand shifts to whoever could be matched.

Stratification. Bin on the score, estimate within bins, average. Crude and robust.

Inverse-probability weighting. Weight each unit by $1/e$ if treated and $1/(1-e)$ if not, reconstructing the population that would have existed under random assignment. Uses all the data, and has the variance problem §20 measures.

All three assume the same thing: that the score was estimated from a set satisfying the backdoor criterion. None of them repairs an unmeasured confounder, and the apparent sophistication of the machinery is a common route to forgetting that.

§20 Overlap, and where adjustment stops working

Positivity

Positivity (or overlap) is the requirement that every unit have a genuine chance of either treatment: $0 < e(z) < 1$ for all $z$ that occur.

It is one of the three assumptions any adjustment estimate rests on, alongside no unmeasured confounding (§12) and consistency: that the treatment is well enough defined for "the outcome under treatment" to name one thing.

Unlike the other two, positivity is checkable. Plot the score by treatment group and look, which is the left panel. Doing so should be automatic and mostly is not.

A reader arriving from the potential-outcomes tradition (the $Y_1, Y_0$ notation already used from §33 on) will know this trio under different names: no unmeasured confounding is ignorability, and consistency together with no interference between units' outcomes is the SUTVA (stable unit treatment value assumption) that the whole notation presupposes. Same three assumptions, graph vocabulary on one side and potential-outcomes vocabulary on the other.

The good news in the right panel

Variance rising before bias is the friendly failure mode. It means a positivity problem announces itself in the confidence interval rather than hiding in the point estimate: the opposite of confounding, which is silent.

This is also why the standard fix is dangerous. Clipping the weights, or trimming units with extreme scores, restores a tight interval by discarding the units that were carrying the warning. The next section measures what that trade costs.

§21 Doubly robust estimation

Two chances to be right

The augmented inverse-probability weighted estimator combines the outcome model $\hat{m}_t(z)$ and the propensity model $\hat{e}(z)$:

$$\hat{\tau} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \left[ \hat{m}_1(z_i) - \hat{m}_0(z_i) + \frac{t_i\,(y_i - \hat{m}_1(z_i))}{\hat{e}(z_i)} - \frac{(1-t_i)\,(y_i - \hat{m}_0(z_i))}{1 - \hat{e}(z_i)} \right]$$

The first term is g-computation. The rest is a correction weighted by the propensity, applied to the outcome model's residuals. The property, which the name refers to: it is consistent if either model is correct, not both.

What the right panel was not built to show

It exists because the left panel's third bar refused to come out right. With the original design the correct-propensity arm carried a bias of +0.109, and the property says it should carry none. Increasing the sample size twenty-five-fold did not move it, which ruled out sampling noise and pointed at something structural.

It was the weight clip. About 1.6% of units had a true propensity above 0.98, clipping them reweighted the population, and the reweighting was the bias. Loosening the clip removed it and quadrupled the variance. That trade-off is now the right panel, and it is more useful than the clean version of the figure would have been: the estimator's theoretical property survives, and the thing every practitioner does to make it usable is what breaks it.

Pitfall: "doubly robust" is not "robust"

Two chances is better than one and it is not a guarantee. The fourth bar, both models wrong, is the common case in practice, where the covariates are many, the functional forms unknown, and neither model was ever going to be exactly right. The estimator carries a bias of +0.98 there and reports it with a standard deviation of 0.042, which is to say confidently.

The honest reading: doubly robust buys you a second chance at a specification you cannot verify. It does not buy you a way to skip §12.

§22 Sensitivity analysis

No unmeasured confounding is untestable. That does not make it unexaminable: you can ask how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to overturn your conclusion, and report the answer.

The question to answer, and how to report it

For an estimated effect and an assumed confounder $U$, sweep two quantities (how strongly $U$ predicts treatment, and how strongly it predicts outcome) and find the contour at which the effect crosses zero or loses significance.

The output is a statement of the form: an unmeasured confounder would need to be about as strongly associated with both treatment and outcome as $Z_3$ is, to explain this result away. That is checkable against domain knowledge in a way that the raw estimate is not.

This is the same move as Part XXI §32's safety case: when an assumption cannot be verified, state what would have to be true for it to fail, and let a reader who knows the domain judge.

§23 What goes wrong in practice

In roughly the order of frequency

Adjusting for a descendant of the treatment. Mediators and colliders included in the covariate set because they were available and predictive. §12's first condition, violated.

Positivity ignored. Nobody plotted the score. The interval is tight because the estimator quietly extrapolated into regions with no data.

The estimand left unstated. Average effect over whom? Everyone, the treated, the matched? Matching changes it, weighting changes it, trimming changes it, and the three answers can differ substantially.

The model fitted on the outcome. Selecting covariates by how much they improve prediction of $Y$ selects colliders and mediators efficiently. This is the exact point at which machine-learning instincts are actively harmful.

The assumption never written down. Most common of all, and the one that makes the others undetectable in review.

§24 What movement C establishes

Three things

Identification and estimation are separate problems. Movement B decides whether a number exists; this movement decides whether you can compute it from a finite sample, and the answer can be no for reasons of overlap even when identification holds.

Positivity is the assumption you can check, and it fails loudly: Figure 3's variance rises twelvefold while the bias stays flat. Plot the propensity distributions. It takes one line and it is the highest-yield diagnostic in the movement.

Two chances at a specification is not a guarantee. Figure 4's estimator is exact when either model is right and carries a bias of +0.98 when neither is, and the practical fix for its variance reintroduces bias of its own. Every route here still runs through an assumption no estimator can test.
