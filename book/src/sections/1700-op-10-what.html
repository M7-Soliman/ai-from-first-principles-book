Part AWhat we are actually optimising

§1 Learning is not optimisation

In 60 seconds

An optimiser is handed a function and told to make it small. That is not the situation here. You care about a quantity you cannot measure, so you minimise a second quantity that stands in for it, using a third quantity that is cheap to differentiate, and then you stop before any of them is minimised. Every difficulty in this part descends from that arrangement.

Part III treated optimisation as a self-contained problem: here is $f$, find its minimum, mind the conditioning. That was the right way to learn the machinery and it is the wrong way to think about training a model.

The difference is worth stating precisely, because it is not a philosophical point. It changes what counts as success, which algorithms are appropriate, and when you should stop. In pure optimisation, minimising $J$ is the goal; you have converged when the gradient vanishes, and a smaller $J$ is always better. In learning, minimising $J$ is a means, and there are three distinct quantities in play that are routinely conflated.

The three quantities

1. What you want. Performance on data you have not seen, under whatever measure the application cares about: accuracy, revenue, a clinician's judgement. Call it $P$. It is frequently not even a function of the model's parameters in any usable way.

2. What you would minimise if you could. The risk, the expected loss under the true data-generating distribution:

$$J^*(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[L(f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}), y)\big]$$

You cannot evaluate this, because you do not have $p_{\text{data}}$. If you did, you would have a solved optimisation problem and no machine learning problem.

3. What you actually minimise. The empirical risk, the same expectation taken over the empirical distribution defined by your training set:

$$J(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim \hat{p}_{\text{data}}}\big[L(f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}), y)\big] = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} L\big(f(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\boldsymbol{\theta}), y^{(i)}\big)$$

The whole enterprise is the hope that pushing down the third moves the second, which moves the first. Part VI §4 named the gap between the second and the third the generalisation gap and spent a part on it. This part is about the gap on the other side: the one between the function your optimiser sees and the function you would have wanted it to see.

That gap is not a defect to be engineered away. It is load-bearing. A model that minimised the empirical risk perfectly would memorise the training set, which Part VI §6 showed is the one outcome guaranteed to be useless. You are relying on the optimiser to fail, in a specific and controlled way. No branch of pure optimisation has anything to say about that, and it is why importing optimisation results wholesale into learning goes wrong.

§2 Empirical risk minimisation, and why almost nobody does it

The obvious move (replace $p_{\text{data}}$ with $\hat{p}_{\text{data}}$ and minimise what remains) has a name, empirical risk minimisation, and a respectable theory. It also has two problems severe enough that deep learning essentially never does it.

The first is overfitting, and Part VI covered it. A model with enough capacity can drive the empirical risk to zero by memorisation, and the theory guaranteeing that low empirical risk implies low true risk requires capacity control that modern networks do not satisfy in any obvious way.

The second is more immediate and less discussed. The loss you actually care about is usually not differentiable. Classification error (the 0–1 loss, counting mistakes) has derivative zero wherever it is defined and is undefined at the boundaries. Gradient descent on it does nothing at all: every step sees a slope of zero and stays put.

Nor is this a limitation of gradient methods that a cleverer algorithm would sidestep. Minimising expected 0–1 loss exactly is intractable, exponential in the input dimension even for a linear classifier. There is no algorithm waiting to be found.

Intuition: why counting mistakes gives you nothing to follow

Picture the loss as a landscape you are trying to descend. Under 0–1 loss the landscape is a flight of stairs: flat treads with vertical risers, and you are standing in the middle of a tread.

Everything within a small step of you has exactly the same height, so the local slope is zero and there is no direction to prefer. Move a hair in any direction and nothing changes. Move far enough to reach a riser and the loss jumps discontinuously by a whole unit.

Gradient descent needs the landscape to tell it something local about where lower ground lies. A staircase refuses to answer. The fix is to replace the staircase with a ramp that descends in the same overall direction, which is exactly what a surrogate loss is.

§3 Surrogate losses, and the bonus nobody expected

A surrogate loss is a differentiable function you optimise in place of the one you care about, chosen so that making it small tends to make the real one small too.

You have been using one since Part VII §9 without the label. The negative log-likelihood of the correct class is the standard surrogate for 0–1 loss, and the argument for it is short: a model that estimates $p(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ well can pick the most probable class, and picking the most probable class minimises expected classification error. So doing well at the surrogate is sufficient for doing well at the target, even though they are different functions.

What is not obvious, and is the interesting part, is that the substitution can make you better off than optimising the target directly would have.

Panel (a) is the phenomenon. Once every training example is on the correct side of the boundary, the 0–1 loss is zero and has nothing further to ask for. The surrogate is not satisfied. It keeps pushing the examples further from the boundary, because $-\log p$ keeps falling as $p$ rises toward one, and it will keep pushing forever.

That extra work is not wasted. A boundary with more room around it classifies unseen points more reliably, so panel (b) keeps improving long after the training error has flatlined. The model extracted information from the training set that the 0–1 loss could not express: how confidently each point is classified, not merely whether it is. Optimising the thing you wanted would have stopped at iteration 280 with a worse model.

Why this matters: the loss you report is not the loss you train

This is the first of several places in this part where two numbers that look interchangeable are not, and mixing them up produces confident nonsense.

A training curve that has flattened in accuracy may still be improving in loss, and the improvement may be real. A model with lower cross-entropy may have identical accuracy and be materially better calibrated, Part II §25's reading of cross-entropy as a measure of the whole predicted distribution, not just its argmax.

The practical rule: plot both, always. Accuracy tells you what the user gets; loss tells you what the optimiser is doing. When they disagree, the disagreement is information, not noise.

§4 Why training stops, and why that is not convergence

Here is the difference from pure optimisation that is easiest to state and hardest to internalise.

Training halts on a criterion the optimiser cannot see

An optimisation algorithm has converged when its gradient is negligible. A training run stops when a validation measure (often the very 0–1 loss the surrogate replaced) stops improving.

These are unrelated events. Training routinely halts while the surrogate still has large derivatives, and would have gone on descending for a very long time.

Part VIII §13 introduced early stopping as a regulariser, and derived in §14 the sense in which it is equivalent to weight decay. That was the regularisation reading. The optimisation reading is separate and worth having: early stopping is the mechanism by which a learning algorithm refuses to solve the optimisation problem it was given.

Panels (b) and (d) of Figure 1 are the two outcomes, on the same model and the same loss. In (b) the classes are genuinely separated, the extra confidence the surrogate buys is real information, and running longer helps for thirty-one times as long as you might have guessed. In (d) ten coordinates of pure noise have been added; the surrogate spends its effort fitting those, and test error is best at iteration 80 and 46% worse by the end.

You cannot tell which case you are in by looking at the training loss. Both fall smoothly and monotonically. The only instrument that distinguishes them is a held-out set, which is why Part VI §9's validation set is not bureaucracy but the sole source of the stopping signal.

Pitfall: "the loss is still going down, so keep training"

The training loss is always still going down. On a separable problem it will go down for as long as you let it and never reach a minimum, because there is not one, panel (c), and §12 explains why this is the normal case rather than a curiosity.

So "training loss still falling" carries no information about whether to continue. It is compatible with panel (b), where continuing gains you 9%, and with panel (d), where continuing costs you 46%. The two runs look identical from the training curve.

The corresponding error in the other direction: reporting that a model "converged" because you stopped it. Almost no deep network is trained to convergence in the optimisation sense, and §6 shows that the gradient norm at the point where you stop is often larger than where you started.

§5 Minibatches: what the batch size actually buys

In 60 seconds

Part III §14 established that gradient estimates from a sample are unbiased and that noise falls like $1/\sqrt{b}$. That is one of four forces setting the batch size. The others are hardware, memory, and (the one most often missed) how badly your update amplifies the estimation error. Gradient methods tolerate $b = 100$. Second-order methods need $b = 10{,}000$, and this section measures why.

The reason a minibatch works at all is structural: the cost is an average over examples, so its gradient is an average over per-example gradients, and an average is precisely the thing that can be estimated by sampling. Part II §10 gives the unbiasedness in one line.

The returns are poor and it does not matter. Part V §3's standard error of a mean falls like $\sigma/\sqrt{b}$, so a hundredfold increase in compute buys a tenfold reduction in noise. Figure 2's left panel confirms the exponent on real data. Spending that compute on more steps instead is almost always the better trade, and it is why essentially all large-scale training is stochastic.

Intuition: redundancy is the deeper reason

The $1/\sqrt{b}$ argument makes small batches tolerable. Redundancy is what makes them efficient.

Take the extreme case: a training set consisting of the same example repeated a million times. The exact gradient is the gradient of one example, and computing it a million times is a million times more work for an identical answer.

Real datasets are not that degenerate, but they are far from the opposite extreme. Large numbers of examples make nearly the same contribution to the gradient, because they are handled correctly for the same reason or wrongly for the same reason. What a minibatch estimates cheaply is not a fine-grained quantity; it is a heavily redundant one, and the redundancy is what you are exploiting.

The four forces

The middle panel is the hardware constraint made concrete, and it is the one that surprises people who reason only about statistics. A batch of one is not cheap; it is thirteen times more expensive per example than a batch of 256, because a matrix–vector product leaves almost all of the machine idle. There is an absolute minimum batch size below which you get faster gradients for free, and on this machine it is around 32.

Against that, memory scales linearly with batch size when the batch is processed in parallel, and on most hardware that is the binding constraint. Powers of two are conventional because array libraries and GPU kernels are tuned for them; 32 to 256 is the usual range, with 16 attempted for very large models.

And small batches regularise. Generalisation error is often best at a batch size of one, Part III §14's observation that gradient noise appears to prefer wide minima, restated. The cost is that you may need a smaller learning rate to stay stable, so the wall-clock price is paid twice.

Why second-order methods need enormous batches

The right panel of Figure 2 measures something the $1/\sqrt{b}$ argument alone would never surface, and it explains a fact §19 will need.

Different algorithms use the minibatch's information differently, and some amplify its error. A gradient method steps along $\hat{\mathbf{g}}$, so an $\epsilon$ error in the estimate is an $\epsilon$ error in the step. A second-order method steps along $H^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{g}}$, and Part III §5 says that multiplying by an ill-conditioned matrix multiplies relative error by up to $\kappa$.

So even with $H$ known exactly, the same gradient noise produces a far noisier step. The measurement above: a tenfold amplification throughout, at $\kappa \approx 10^4$. And $H$ is never known exactly; it is estimated from the same small sample, so the real situation is worse than the figure shows.

This is the honest reason second-order methods are rare in deep learning, and it is independent of their cost. Even if $H^{-1}$ were free you would need batches two orders of magnitude larger to use it, which gives back the advantage that made stochastic methods worth having.

Two requirements that are easy to violate

The sample must be random. Unbiasedness requires independence, and many datasets arrive in an order that destroys it: five blood samples from one patient, then three from the next; all the cats, then all the dogs. Drawing consecutive examples from such a file gives minibatches that are each a biased picture of one subpopulation, and successive gradients that are correlated with each other rather than independent.

Shuffling once and storing the shuffled order is enough in practice, even though it fixes the set of possible minibatches for every model trained thereafter. Never shuffling is seriously damaging. This is the cheapest correctness win in the whole pipeline and it is routinely skipped.

The first epoch is different from every epoch after it

Here is a fact with an odd consequence. On the first pass through a shuffled dataset, every example is a fresh draw from $p_{\text{data}}$ that the model has not seen. So each minibatch gradient is an unbiased estimate not of $\nabla J$, the empirical risk gradient, but of $\nabla J^*$, the gradient of the true generalisation error.

For one epoch, SGD is performing gradient descent directly on the quantity §1 said you could never evaluate. On the second pass this stops being true, because you are resampling values you have already used rather than drawing fair new ones.

This is not merely cute. It is why training on a stream (where examples are never repeated) has no overfitting problem at all, and why, as datasets grow faster than compute, it is becoming ordinary to make a single pass or even an incomplete one. In that regime underfitting and throughput are the only concerns, and most of Part VIII becomes irrelevant.

Try it

You are training with $b = 32$ and consider $b = 1024$ to "get better gradients". Using Figure 2, what do you gain, what do you pay, and what would you do with the compute instead?

Answer. A 32-fold increase in compute per step, for a $\sqrt{32} \approx 5.7$-fold reduction in gradient noise. That is the trade, and stated that way it rarely sounds attractive.

What you gain beyond the noise reduction: nothing, unless you were below the hardware threshold, and at $b = 32$ you are near it, the middle panel is already flat, so the 32× compute is a real 32×, not partly free. What you pay: 32× the memory, and 32× fewer parameter updates per epoch.

The alternative use of that compute is thirty-two more steps at $b = 32$. Since the gradient is only a local model of the surface anyway and you are about to recompute it, more steps at a rougher direction almost always wins, which is §5's whole argument, and Part III §14's.

The exception is the one Figure 2's right panel identifies. If you are running a second-order or natural-gradient method, the noise reduction is not a luxury: your update amplifies gradient error tenfold, and at $b = 32$ your step direction is mostly noise.
