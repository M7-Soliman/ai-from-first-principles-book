Part GKeeping and changing what a model knows

Movements D and E treated post-training as an event: take a pretrained model, tune it, ship it. It is not an event. It is instruction tuning, then preference tuning, then a safety pass, then a domain adaptation, then a fix for something that shipped wrong: each one a training run on a model that already knows things. This movement is what a sequence of those does, and what the four available remedies actually buy.

§46 The shape of the problem

Nobody trains a model once

A deployed model's weights are the result of a chain of training runs, most of which were not planned when the previous one happened. The chain has a property nobody designs for: each link optimises one objective on one dataset, and the model that comes out is judged on everything it was ever supposed to do.

Four things can be done about it, and they are the four sections that follow. Rehearse: keep some of the old data in the mix. Constrain: do not let the weights move far, which is what a small learning rate, a KL penalty and a low-rank adapter all are. Average: train separately and combine the weights afterwards. Edit: change one specific thing and leave the rest alone. Each has a measurable cost and one of them has a much worse one than its literature suggests.

A note on scale, because it decides what these figures are worth

Everything measured here is a small network on a synthetic task, plus a linear associative memory standing in for a fact store. These figures measure mechanisms (interference between weight updates, the geometry of averaging, the ripple from a rank-one edit), not the behaviour of a model a million times larger.

What transfers is the shape of each relationship and the reason for it. What does not transfer is any particular number. Where a section makes a claim that rests on scale rather than mechanism, it says so.

§47 Forgetting

Go and read

French (1999), Catastrophic forgetting in connectionist networks. The problem stated clearly, long before anyone was doing sequential post-training on production models. Worth reading to see how little the diagnosis has changed and how much the setting has.

Two things the measurement changes

Forgetting is a distance, not a schedule. What predicts how much of A survives is how far the weights moved, not how long training ran or how many examples it saw. That single fact explains why a lower learning rate, fewer steps, a KL penalty toward the old model, and a low-rank adapter all mitigate forgetting: they are four ways of writing down the same constraint.

The information is not gone. Re-exposure brings task A back to within 2% of its original accuracy in 50 steps, against the 3,000 it took to learn, 60× faster. What was lost is access rather than content, which is why a model that appears to have lost a capability after fine-tuning often has not, and why measuring "capability lost" without measuring recovery cost overstates the damage.

§48 Rehearsal

Go and read

Kirkpatrick et al. (2017), Overcoming catastrophic forgetting in neural networks. Elastic weight consolidation: penalise movement in the directions the old task cared about. Read it as the principled version of §47's finding that forgetting is a distance, with the Fisher information deciding which directions the distance should be measured in.

The cheapest thing in this movement, by a wide margin

A few per cent of old data recovers most of what would otherwise be lost, and at these rates it costs the new task nothing measurable. If you take one operational thing from this movement, it is that any fine-tuning run should carry a small replay fraction by default, and that the fraction should be chosen from the flat part of this curve rather than from an argument.

What it requires is the data, and that is the real constraint. Replay presumes you still have the earlier training set, in a form you can sample. Organisations routinely lose that: the instruction-tuning mixture from two quarters ago, the safety data owned by another team, the licensed corpus whose contract expired. When people say continual learning is hard in practice, the binding difficulty is usually data availability rather than the algorithm, and the algorithm is one line.

§49 Averaging weights

Go and read

Frankle et al. (2020), Linear mode connectivity and the lottery ticket hypothesis. Where the barrier in Figure 7 comes from, and the observation that sharing an early portion of training is what puts two models in the same basin. The precondition for everything in this section. Then Wortsman et al. (2022), Model soups: averaging weights of multiple fine-tuned models. Averaging fine-tunes of one base model, at scale, for a better model at no extra inference cost. Note how carefully the second satisfies the shared-ancestor condition the first establishes; the result does not survive relaxing it.

Averaging is an operation on relatives

The left panel is the precondition for everything else in this section. Two models can be averaged when they are connected by a path along which the loss does not rise, and sharing an ancestor is what puts them there. Independently initialised models are not, and averaging them produces something worse than either, by a lot.

This is why model soups work and why they work only in the situation they are described in: several fine-tunes of the same pretrained model, differing in hyperparameters or data order, averaged. It is why EMA works: the average is over points along one trajectory, which are as related as models get. And it is why merging two models from different pretraining runs is not a thing you can do, however similar they look.

The middle panel is a bonus and connects to Part XXIII §46: width flattens the barrier. At width 8 the barrier is 0.344 and at 128 it is 0.026, which is one reason merging is more forgiving at frontier scale than a small-scale experiment suggests, and a reason to be careful generalising a negative result about merging from a small model.

§50 Task arithmetic

Go and read

Ilharco et al. (2023), Editing models with task arithmetic. Task vectors, added and negated. Figure 8 reproduces the effect and adds the control the claim needs: what each fine-tune already scores on the other task. The interference-against-angle relationship is the part that generalises.

Behaviour as a direction

Let $\tau = \theta_{\text{finetuned}} - \theta_{\text{pretrained}}$: the displacement a fine-tune produced. The claim is that $\tau$ behaves like a vector: that $\theta_0 + \tau_A + \tau_B$ is competent at both tasks, and that $\theta - \tau_A$ has had A removed.

The baseline is the whole measurement

Task arithmetic is a genuine effect: the sum beats the cross-task baseline by 22 points, and that is capability composed rather than inherited. It is also lossy: about a quarter of each specialist's advantage does not survive the addition, and negation is not surgical, costing 16 points of an unrelated capability to remove one.

And the interference is predictable. The correlation between how aligned the two task vectors are and how much the addition gains is $-0.729$: near-orthogonal vectors compose cleanly, aligned ones fight. This is the same accounting as Part XVII's superposition: capacity is directions, and two behaviours that want the same directions cannot both have them.

That correlation is $+0.895$ (the opposite sign, and larger) if you plot raw accuracy instead of lift over the baseline, because when the task vectors align, either model alone is already good at both. A convincing plot supporting the reverse conclusion was one missing control away, which is worth remembering whenever a merging result is reported without one.

§51 Editing

Go and read

Meng et al. (2022), Locating and editing factual associations in GPT. ROME: the rank-one update Figure 9 abstracts. Read it with the follow-up work on sequential editing, which finds the accumulation problem the figure measures — a single edit is exact and a thousand are a slow deletion. Part XXI §10 returns to its first half as an interpretability method.

An exchange rate, not a budget

The rate is 1.3% per edit and 1/90 is 1.1%, so each edit destroys roughly one fact's worth of the store. Editing does not have a budget you discover by experiment; it has an exchange rate you can compute in advance, and the store is ruined after about as many edits as it holds facts, far below its 128-dimensional nominal capacity.

That reframes what direct weight editing is for. As a way to fix one thing before a deadline it is exact and cheap. As a maintenance strategy (the natural thought, since facts change constantly), it is a slow deletion of the model, and the arithmetic says so before you try it. This is the argument for putting changeable knowledge in retrieval (§34–§39) rather than in weights: not that editing does not work, but that it works at a price per edit that does not amortise.

§52 What to do about it

In the order you should reach for them

Replay a few per cent. Figure 6: 1% recovers most of what is lost and costs the new task nothing measurable. Free, if you still have the data, and keeping the data is the part to plan for, since it is what actually goes missing.

Do not move far. Figure 5 says forgetting is a distance. Lower learning rate, fewer steps, a KL term toward the previous model, a low-rank adapter: four spellings of one constraint. Measure the distance travelled, not the epochs.

Train separately and average, if and only if the models share an ancestor. Figure 7: barrier −0.001 with a shared initialisation, +0.336 without. Averaging fine-tunes of one base model is sound; averaging across pretraining runs is not.

Edit only what you will not have to edit again. Figure 9: about one fact of damage per edit. Fine for a hotfix, wrong as a process.

And measure the sequence, not the step. The failure mode of a post-training pipeline is that each stage is evaluated on its own objective and passes, while the composition regresses on something no stage owns. Keep a fixed evaluation set from every earlier stage and run all of them after every stage: the cost is a few minutes and it is the only thing that catches this.

§53 What to carry forward

Five things

Forgetting is fast, and it is a distance. Figure 5 measures a fall to chance with half the loss in the first 3% of the new task's training, and a 0.968 correlation with distance moved in parameter space.

What is lost is access, not content. Recovery took 50 steps against 3,000 to learn, 60× faster. Report recovery cost alongside any claim that fine-tuning destroyed a capability.

A few per cent of replay is the best value in the movement. Figure 6 measures 1% buying most of the retention at a cost of 0.002 to the new task. The hard part is having kept the data.

Averaging weights works between relatives and not otherwise. Figure 7 measures a barrier of −0.001 from a shared initialisation and +0.336 from distinct ones, and width flattens it, so a negative merging result from a small model does not transfer upward.

Editing has an exchange rate. Figure 9 measures about one fact of collateral damage per edit, linear in the edit count. A hotfix is fine; a maintenance strategy is a slow deletion, and that is the case for retrieval over weights.
