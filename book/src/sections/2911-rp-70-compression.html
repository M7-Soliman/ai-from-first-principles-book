Part GLearning as compression

Movement B treated compression as something an autoencoder does. This one treats it as the theory it came from. Part II gave entropy, KL and cross-entropy and stopped; what follows is the part that turns them into a criterion: how many bits a representation is allowed to cost, what it must give up to fit in them, and why the model that describes its training data in the fewest bits is usually the one that generalises.

§43 Rate and distortion

The two numbers, and the curve between them

Given a source and a measure of error, the rate–distortion function $R(D)$ is the least number of bits per sample needed to reconstruct within average distortion $D$. It is a property of the source, not of any codec: the boundary of what is possible.

For a Gaussian source of variance $\sigma^2$ under squared error it has a closed form:

$$R(D) = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2\frac{\sigma^2}{D}, \qquad D \le \sigma^2$$

Half a bit per halving of distortion, forever. Every representation in this part sits somewhere relative to a curve like this, and the useful question about a representation is not "is it good" but "where is it on the trade, and how far from the boundary".

Two things this measures that are easy to state wrongly

The gap is an overhead, not a drift. A quantiser that works one sample at a time pays a fixed quarter of a bit per sample, at every rate, forever. That is the price of not coding long blocks together, and no amount of cleverness inside a scalar quantiser removes it. Going from one dimension to two recovers about a twelfth of it: the rest needs dimensions you do not have.

Optimising the wrong constraint costs more than the whole space-filling gain. Lloyd–Max is the optimal quantiser when the rate is the *number of levels*. Entropy-code its output and it is worse than a uniform quantiser by 0.139 bits, five times the two-dimensional gain. The general lesson is not about quantisers: a method optimal under one accounting can be beaten by a simpler one under a different accounting, and which accounting you are actually paying under is the question to ask first.

§44 Description length

A model is a code for its data

To transmit a dataset to someone who has neither, send a model and then send the residuals under it. The total is a two-part code:

$$L(\text{data}) = \underbrace{L(\text{model})}_{\text{complexity}} + \underbrace{L(\text{data}\mid\text{model})}_{\text{misfit}}$$

The minimum description length principle says: choose the model minimising that total. A more complex model costs more to send and leaves smaller residuals, so the sum has an interior minimum, and finding it requires no held-out data at all.

Why this is more than a coincidence

Cross-validation and MDL are answering the same question from opposite directions. Held-out error asks what the model does on data it has not seen; description length asks how much of the training data the model failed to explain, having charged for the explanation. Both punish a model that fits noise, because noise is incompressible and paying parameters to encode it is a bad trade.

The 63% per-dataset agreement is the honest number and worth stating plainly: MDL is a good selector, not an oracle, and on a third of draws the two criteria pick differently. What makes it worth knowing anyway is that it is available when held-out data is not: in an online setting, or when every example is precious, or when the thing being selected is a model of the whole dataset you have.

§45 The information bottleneck

Compressing for a purpose

Rate–distortion asks for a representation that reconstructs. Prediction asks for one that predicts, which is a different constraint. The information bottleneck writes it down: find $Z$ minimising

$$I(X;Z) - \beta\, I(Z;Y)$$

That is, keep as few bits about the input as possible while retaining as many as possible about the label. Sweeping $\beta$ traces the frontier of what is achievable.

Compressing for prediction is not compressing for reconstruction

The gap between the two curves is the movement's central point, and it is why an autoencoder and a classifier's penultimate layer are not interchangeable representations. Reconstruction keeps what is *large*; prediction keeps what is *relevant*; and a direction can carry most of the variance and none of the signal. Part XV movement B's autoencoder is optimising the first, Part XV §31's probe is reading the second, and the difference measured here is nearly a factor of two.

What the bottleneck does not settle. It has been proposed as an explanation of why deep networks generalise: that training has a compression phase in which irrelevant information is discarded. That claim is contested and depends on how mutual information is estimated in a deterministic network, where it is either infinite or an artefact of the binning. The framework is a good way to state what a representation is for; treat it as a description rather than as an explanation of training dynamics.

§46 Does compression imply generalisation

The part that is usually left out

"Compression implies generalisation" is true when the compression is fully accounted: when the code charges for the model as well as the residuals. Drop the model's own cost and select on residual bits alone, and the criterion picks degree 7 where the full code picks degree 3, at a worse test error. The parameters are where the charge for complexity lives, and removing it turns a selection criterion into a fitting criterion.

This is the same accounting error as reporting a compression ratio without the decompressor. A model that stores the training set verbatim compresses it perfectly, and its description length is enormous once you charge for the model. Whenever you see a claim that some system compresses data better than another, the question is what was billed and to whom.

Where this frame pays off elsewhere in the book

A tokenizer is a code. Part XX §1–§8 chooses one by vocabulary size and fertility; under this frame it is choosing a point on a rate–distortion curve, where rate is bits per character and distortion is zero (it is lossless) and the real trade is between the vocabulary's own description cost and the sequence length it buys. That is why the good vocabulary size is an interior optimum rather than "as large as you can afford".

A scaling law is a compression curve. Cross-entropy in nats per token is a code length. Part XVII's loss-against-compute curves are statements about how many bits of the corpus a given amount of computation buys, and the irreducible term in those fits is the corpus's own entropy: the part no model compresses because there is nothing there to compress.

And a representation is a lossy code with a purpose. Which is §45, and is the sentence this whole part has been circling.

§47 What the frame does not do

Three honest limits

Code lengths depend on the code. Every number in §44 rests on a choice of how to encode parameters, and a cruder code biases selection toward simpler models. MDL is not parameter-free; it moves the arbitrariness from a regularisation constant to a coding scheme, where it is at least interpretable.

Mutual information in a deterministic network is not well defined without noise or binning, so §45's quantities are clean on a discrete joint distribution and much less so on a trained network. Claims about "the compression phase of training" live exactly on that fault line.

And the bound is not a method. $R(D)$ says what is achievable, not how, the same distinction Part XXIII draws about generalisation bounds and Part XXXIII draws about branch-and-bound. Figure 11's quarter-bit gap is what a real method costs, and the gap between what is possible and what is implementable is where all the engineering is.

§48 What to carry forward

Four things

Rate and distortion trade along a curve that belongs to the source. Figure 11 measures a quarter-bit overhead for coding one sample at a time, 0.2576 against a theoretical 0.2546, fixed at every rate, and mostly not recoverable by going to two dimensions.

The right accounting decides which method wins. Lloyd–Max is optimal at a fixed level count and 0.139 bits worse than uniform under entropy coding, more than the entire two-dimensional gain. Ask what you are actually being billed for before optimising.

A model is a code for its data, and the shortest code is usually the right model. Figure 12 measures MDL selecting the true complexity on 80% of datasets with no held-out data, and agreeing with cross-validation only 63% of the time, which is the honest limit.

Compressing for prediction is not compressing for reconstruction. Figure 13 measures 1.373 bits about the label against 0.746 for a reconstruction-first rule at the same rate. A direction can hold most of the variance and none of the signal.
