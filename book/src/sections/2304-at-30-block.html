§14 The block

Attention alone is not a network. It mixes information across positions and does almost nothing at a position: strip away the projections and the output is a convex combination of values, which is a weighted average and no more. Something has to do the per-position computation.

The transformer block

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{x} &\leftarrow \mathbf{x} + \text{MHA}\big(\text{LN}(\mathbf{x})\big) \\ \mathbf{x} &\leftarrow \mathbf{x} + \text{FF}\big(\text{LN}(\mathbf{x})\big) \end{aligned}$$

Two sublayers, each wrapped in a residual connection and a normalisation. The first mixes across positions; the second mixes across features, independently at each position. A transformer is this block, repeated.

Intuition: mix across positions, then think

The alternation is the whole design and it is worth naming plainly.

Attention moves information. It decides what each position should know about the rest of the sequence, and gathers it. It is almost linear: the only nonlinearity is in computing the weights.

The feedforward layer processes it. Same weights at every position, no communication between positions, one hidden layer with a nonlinearity. This is Part X §19's $1\times1$ convolution exactly, a dense layer applied independently everywhere.

So a block is gather, then compute, and depth is that alternation repeated. A position's representation after $L$ blocks has been informed by the whole sequence $L$ times over, with $L$ rounds of per-position processing interleaved.

§15 Where the model actually is

The feedforward sublayer is usually described in a sentence and then ignored. The parameter count says it should not be.

The feedforward layer

$$\text{FF}(\mathbf{x}) = W_2\,\phi\big(W_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1\big) + \mathbf{b}_2$$

with $W_1$ of shape $(4d, d)$ and $W_2$ of shape $(d, 4d)$. The expansion factor of four is convention rather than derivation; it has survived a great deal of searching.

Why two thirds of the model is the part nobody discusses

The ratio is exact: $8d^2$ against $4d^2$, with no dependence on $d$. Every transformer ever trained has had twice as many parameters in its feedforward layers as in its attention.

Three consequences, and each is somewhere the field spent years learning the hard way.

Efficiency work aimed at attention addresses a third of the model. §13's crossover said the same thing about compute. This is why mixture-of-experts (§33) targets the feedforward layer: that is where the parameters are, so that is where conditional computation pays.

It is a plausible site for factual storage. A widely-supported reading treats the feedforward layer as a key–value memory: $W_1$'s rows detect patterns, $W_2$'s columns write associated content. Interventions on these layers can change specific facts a model reports, which is suggestive rather than conclusive, and is where Part XXI's interpretability work starts.

It is where the nonlinearity lives. Attention's only nonlinearity is the softmax over weights. Remove the feedforward layers and a transformer's per-position computation collapses to something close to linear, which §26's rank collapse makes precise.

§16 The residual stream

The residual connections were introduced in Part X §23 as an optimisation device: shorten the gradient's path. In a transformer they support a second reading that has become the standard way to think about what these models do.

Intuition: a channel every layer reads from and writes to

Unroll the block: $\mathbf{x}_{L} = \mathbf{x}_0 + \sum_{\ell} f_\ell(\mathbf{x}_{\ell-1})$. The representation at depth $L$ is the input embedding plus the sum of every sublayer's output.

So the residual path is not a shortcut around the computation. It is a running total: a communication channel that every sublayer reads from, computes on, and adds back into. Nothing is ever overwritten; layers only contribute.

That reading explains several observations at once. Why layers can often be removed or reordered with surprisingly little damage: they are addends, not stages. Why the norm of the stream grows steadily with depth: you are summing. And why interpretability work talks about what a head writes into the stream rather than what it outputs: the useful question is what a component contributes to a shared total.

§17 Where the normalisation goes

Go and read

Xiong et al. (2020), On layer normalization in the transformer architecture. Where pre-norm was analysed rather than merely adopted, with the gradient-scale argument worked through. Read it against Figure 7, which finds the one-line summary of this paper ("pre-norm removes the need for warmup") too strong at aggressive learning rates.

The original transformer put the normalisation after the residual addition. Almost everything since puts it before the sublayer. The change is two characters of code and it decides whether the model trains.

Post-norm and pre-norm

Post-norm, the original:

$$\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \text{LN}\big(\mathbf{x} + \text{Sublayer}(\mathbf{x})\big)$$

Pre-norm, the modern default:

$$\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} + \text{Sublayer}\big(\text{LN}(\mathbf{x})\big)$$

In pre-norm the residual path is clean: an unmodified identity runs from the embedding to the output, with every sublayer adding to it. In post-norm the normalisation sits on that path, so the identity is rescaled at every block.

Why the placement matters, and what the measurement actually shows

The original transformer required a learning-rate warmup (start near zero, rise over a few thousand steps) and without it training diverged. The standard explanation is that this is a post-norm artefact: with the normalisation on the residual path, the gradient reaching layer $\ell$ is rescaled by every normalisation above it, by factors that depend on activations which at initialisation are arbitrary. Early updates are therefore large and badly proportioned across depth, and one bad first step is unrecoverable.

That story is usually compressed to "pre-norm removes the need for warmup". Figure 7 says the compression is too strong.

At 12 blocks and a deliberately aggressive rate, pre-norm fails cold too: it reaches 2.263, essentially chance. What distinguishes it is that warmup rescues it, to 0.951, while post-norm stays at chance whether warmed up or not. And the gradient-scale gap at initialisation, which is supposed to be the mechanism, is a factor of 1.52: real, and far too small to carry the difference in outcomes on its own.

So the honest statement is narrower than the folklore and more useful: pre-norm widens the range of learning rates from which training can recover. At moderate rates that shows up as not needing warmup; at aggressive rates it shows up as warmup working at all. Both are the same property, and neither is "warmup is unnecessary".

Post-norm is not simply worse. When it trains, it often trains to a slightly better final loss, which is why it persists. It is more fragile, and at scale fragility costs more than the fraction of a point it wins.

§18 LayerNorm and RMSNorm

Part X §24 tabulated the normalisation family by which axes they average over. Transformers use the one that does not touch the batch.

Layer normalisation, and the term it drops

LayerNorm standardises each position's feature vector independently:

$$\text{LN}(\mathbf{x}) = \gamma \odot \frac{\mathbf{x} - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} + \beta$$

with $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ over the $d$ features of that position.

RMSNorm drops the mean subtraction and the offset:

$$\text{RMS}(\mathbf{x}) = \gamma \odot \frac{\mathbf{x}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_i x_i^2 + \epsilon}}$$

Cheaper, one fewer pass over the data, and empirically as good, which is the evidence that the re-centring was never the part that mattered. Most recent large models use it.

Why not batch norm

Part X §24 gave the reason and it applies with extra force here. Batch norm's statistics come from the batch, so their error falls like $1/\sqrt{N}$, and it makes each example's output depend on the others in its batch, which for autoregressive generation is simply wrong: at inference you have one sequence.

LayerNorm's statistics come from within a single position's feature vector. No batch dependence, identical behaviour at train and test time, no running statistics to maintain, and no train/eval mode to forget to set. Part IV §14's silent bug does not exist here.

§19 The activation function

Part VII §7 argued for the rectifier. Transformers mostly do not use it, and the reasons are worth a paragraph because they are unusually empirical.

GELU multiplies the input by the probability that a standard normal falls below it, $x\,\Phi(x)$, a smooth gate rather than a hard one. It is a rectifier with the corner rounded off and a small negative lobe, and it consistently beats ReLU here by a small margin.

SwiGLU is the current default in large models and has a different structure: a gated unit, where one linear map produces content and another produces a multiplicative gate.

$$\text{SwiGLU}(\mathbf{x}) = \big(W_1\mathbf{x} \odot \sigma(\beta \, W_g\mathbf{x})\big)$$
Pitfall: the parameter count changes, and comparisons often forget

A gated unit needs a third matrix (content, gate, and the output projection) where an ordinary feedforward layer needs two. At the same hidden width, SwiGLU has 1.5× the parameters.

The convention is to shrink the hidden width to $\tfrac{2}{3} \times 4d$ so the count matches. A comparison that does not do this is measuring parameters, not architecture: Part X §26's complaint, in the same architecture family, a few years later.

The honest summary of §19 is that these are small empirical wins with weak theory. The gating story, that a multiplicative interaction lets the layer express something additive units cannot, is plausible and is not established. Part VII §7's rectifier argument was derived; this is not, and the difference is worth noticing.

§20 Encoder, decoder, and why one of them won

Three arrangements exist, and the field converged on the least obvious one.

The three configurations

Encoder-only. Bidirectional self-attention, every position sees every other. Good for classification and tagging, where you have the whole input. Cannot generate.

Encoder–decoder. A bidirectional encoder, a causal decoder, and cross-attention between them. The original transformer, and the natural shape for translation.

Decoder-only. One stack, causally masked, trained to predict the next token. The input and the output are the same sequence, and because the vocabulary at both ends is identical, most implementations tie the input embedding matrix to the final projection back to logits: one $|V| \times d$ table, read forwards to embed and transposed to predict, rather than two.

Why decoder-only won, and it is not because it is better at translation

Four reasons, in rough order of weight.

One objective, unlimited data. Next-token prediction needs no labels and no pairing. Every text ever written is a training example. Encoder–decoder training wants matched pairs, and pairs are scarce where raw text is not.

Every task is a sequence. Translation, summarisation, question answering and classification can all be written as "here is some text, continue it". One architecture, one objective, and the task specification moves into the input, which is §38's subject and was not anticipated.

Simplicity scales. One stack has one set of hyperparameters, one shape, and no cross-attention to place. When you are running experiments that cost a great deal, fewer choices is a real advantage.

Every position gets a gradient. A causal decoder predicts a token at every position, so a sequence of length $n$ supplies $n$ training signals. An encoder–decoder gets gradient only from the output side.

Encoder-only models remain better per parameter at classification, because bidirectional context is genuinely more informative when you have it. They lost anyway, and the reason is the first point rather than the fourth: the winning move was to train on everything.
