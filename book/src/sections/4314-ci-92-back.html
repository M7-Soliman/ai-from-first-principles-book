BuildThe project

Ten to fourteen hours. You build the estimators, then use them on a system whose true effect you set, and finish by breaking every one of them on purpose, because knowing the failure mode is the part that transfers.

P.1 What you are building

because, a small causal toolkit: a graph with d-separation and the backdoor criterion, four estimators, three quasi-experimental designs, and a simulation harness that lets you set the truth and check whether the method found it.

The rule that governs the project: you set the effect, so you always know the answer. That is the only setting in which a causal method can be scored at all, and it is why this project is simulation-first rather than dataset-first.

P.2 Stage 1: the graph

  1. Represent a DAG as node → parents. Implement descendants, paths, colliders, blocking.
  2. Implement d-separation. Verify the fork, chain and collider behave as §10 says, including that conditioning on a collider's descendant also opens it.
  3. Implement the backdoor criterion. Check it rejects a mediator.
  4. Now draw the graph for a model you have actually deployed, encode it, and run the criterion over your real covariate set.

Deliverable: the tools, and a written list of the descendants of the treatment that were in your feature set. Step four is the point of the stage.

P.3 Stage 2: confounding, and what adjustment recovers

  1. Write a simulator that takes a true effect and a confounding strength and returns data.
  2. Estimate naively and by stratified adjustment. Sweep the confounding and plot both against the truth.
  3. Find the strength at which the naive estimate changes sign.
  4. Now hold the confounding fixed and sweep $n$ from 103 to 106. Plot the naive estimate's error.

Deliverable: the two plots. The second is flat, and it is the one to keep: the error does not shrink, so no diagnostic that looks for variance will ever find it.

P.4 Stage 3: propensity, overlap and double robustness

  1. Implement logistic regression by Newton–Raphson. Check it recovers a known assignment rule.
  2. Implement IPW and AIPW. Confirm they agree with stratification when overlap is good.
  3. Sweep the confounder's effect on treatment. Plot the propensity distributions by arm, and track bias, spread and effective sample size.
  4. Run all four combinations of correct and incorrect outcome and propensity models.
  5. Break positivity, then sweep the weight clip and plot bias and spread separately.

Deliverable: the overlap panel, the four-arm result, and the clip trade-off. Predict all three before running them; the third surprises most people.

P.5 Stage 4: designs, and how each one breaks

  1. Implement 2SLS, difference-in-differences and regression discontinuity.
  2. Verify each recovers a known effect on data built for it.
  3. Then break each on purpose: weaken the instrument until it is unusable, violate exclusion with a small direct path, give the difference-in-differences groups diverging pre-trends, and let units manipulate the running variable.
  4. For each breakage, record whether it was detectable from the data alone.

Deliverable: a table of four designs against four breakages, with a column for "would I have noticed". Two of the eight are silent, and knowing which two is the deliverable.

P.6 Stage 5: the machine-learning consequence

  1. Build a target with a causal parent and a spurious child whose relationship varies by environment.
  2. Train a predictor on the cause, one on the effect, one on both. Test across environments including a reversed one.
  3. Now evaluate all three with a random train/test split and compare what you conclude.
  4. Implement off-policy evaluation and score a target policy from logged data. Then remove the support and see what the estimator reports.

Deliverable: the environment table, the random-split table that contradicts it, and the unsupported off-policy estimate. Step three is the one that changes how you validate models.

P.7 Stretch

  • Sensitivity analysis: the two-parameter contour at which your effect crosses zero.
  • CATE estimators: S-, T- and X-learners, and an honest attempt to validate them.
  • Causal discovery on data from a graph you generated, compared with the truth.
  • Synthetic control with a placebo distribution.
  • Front-door on a simulation where the confounder is genuinely unobserved.
  • Log the probability in a system you run, and answer a counterfactual about last month.

P.8 Done when

  • Your backdoor criterion rejects a mediator, and you have run it over a real feature set.
  • You have a naive estimate that reports the opposite sign of a truth you set.
  • You have shown that error is flat in $n$.
  • You can manufacture a correlation between independent variables by conditioning, and by selection, and can say why they are the same thing.
  • Your AIPW is exact when either model is right and biased when neither is.
  • You have measured what clipping the weights costs.
  • You have broken all four designs and know which breakages are silent.
  • You have a model that is best on a random split and worst on an environment split.
  • Your README names one covariate you would have adjusted for before this project and would not now.
Why this project

Every method in this part is normally applied where the answer is unknown, which means a practitioner can use them for years without ever finding out that one of them failed. Setting the truth inverts that: the methods can be scored, and several of them fail in ways that are invisible from the estimate alone.

Stage 5 is the reason the part is in a machine-learning book. The predictor that wins your validation is the one that fails when the world moves, and the standard evaluation protocol is structurally incapable of noticing.

The framework, extended

A model has been four choices throughout this book: a dataset, a model family, a loss, an optimiser. Part XXI added a fifth, a claim about what the result establishes. This part adds the sixth, and it comes before all of them: the estimand. What quantity, about which population, under what intervention.

Everything downstream is machinery for computing it. Choosing the machinery before naming the quantity is how a technically flawless analysis ends up answering a question nobody asked, and it is the most common failure in applied work of every kind.
