Part DTesting, and how it goes wrong

§11 Hypothesis testing

In 60 seconds

Assume nothing is going on: the null hypothesis. Compute how surprising your data would be under that assumption. If it is surprising enough, reject the assumption. That is the whole procedure, and its logic is proof by contradiction with the certainty replaced by a probability.

The structure is always the same:

  1. State a null hypothesis $H_0$, typically "there is no difference".
  2. Choose a test statistic that measures departure from it.
  3. Work out that statistic's distribution assuming $H_0$ is true.
  4. Compute where your observed value falls in that distribution.

That structure has a peculiar shape, and it is worth asking why before accepting it. You are made to assume the opposite of what you believe, and then permitted only to argue against it. Why not simply estimate whether the effect is real?

Intuition: you can only compute probabilities under a specific claim

The reason is arithmetic rather than philosophy. To calculate anything you need a distribution, and "there is no difference" pins one down exactly: the two groups came from the same population, so you know precisely how the statistic behaves. It is a single, fully specified world you can compute in.

"There is a difference" specifies nothing. A difference of what size? The alternative is not one hypothesis but an infinite family, and you cannot compute a probability under a family. So the test is built on the only hypothesis concrete enough to calculate with, and the calculation runs in the direction that hypothesis allows.

The logic is proof by contradiction with the certainty softened. In mathematics you assume the opposite and derive an impossibility. Here you assume the opposite and derive something merely very unlikely, and treat that as grounds to abandon the assumption.

Which forces an asymmetry that causes more misreading than anything else in this part. The procedure can reject the null. It can never accept it. A contradiction refutes an assumption; the failure to find one establishes nothing, because you may simply not have looked hard enough.

So "we did not reject $H_0$" means only that this experiment did not produce evidence against it. It does not mean the null is true, and it does not mean the effect is absent. It is the verdict "not proven" rather than "innocent", and the reason it gets read as the stronger claim is that people want an answer to a question the procedure was never built to answer. Section 13 gives that failure its proper name: an underpowered study returns "not proven" almost regardless of what is true.

Definition: p-value

The p-value is the probability of observing a result at least as extreme as yours, if the null hypothesis were true.

Every clause in that sentence is load-bearing, and §12 is about what happens when they are dropped. Note in particular the direction of the conditioning: it is the probability of the data given the hypothesis, never the hypothesis given the data. Part II §7 warned that those two are different quantities, and Part II §22 measured how far apart they can be. The p-value sits on the side you can compute; the number you wanted is on the side you cannot reach without a prior.

§12 What a p-value is not

In 60 seconds

Run one test at the 5% level and you have a 5% false-positive rate. Run twenty independent tests and the chance of at least one false positive is about 64%. Run enough and a false positive is essentially guaranteed. Hyperparameter search is multiple testing, and almost nobody treats it that way.

Go and read

Wasserstein & Lazar (2016), The ASA statement on p-values. Two pages in which a professional body states plainly what a p-value is not, having concluded that the misuse was serious enough to warrant an official position. Read it and then reread any paper you have recently trusted.

Pitfall: four wrong readings of $p = 0.03$

"There is a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true." No. The p-value is computed assuming the null is true; it cannot also be a probability about it. Getting from one to the other requires a prior and Bayes' rule (Part II §21, and §17 below).

"There is a 97% chance the effect is real." Same error, restated.

"The effect is large." No. A p-value conflates effect size (the actual magnitude of a difference, often expressed in standard deviations, e.g. Cohen's $d = (\mu_A - \mu_B)/\sigma$) with sample size. A trivial difference becomes significant with enough data; an enormous one stays non-significant without it. Always report the effect size separately.

"$p = 0.06$ means there is no effect." No. Failing to reject is not evidence of absence; it may only mean the study was too small to see anything, which is §13.

History: the 0.05 threshold was a casual suggestion

The convention that $p < 0.05$ constitutes evidence traces to Ronald Fisher in the 1920s, and he proposed it far more loosely than its later career suggests, as a convenient rule of thumb, roughly the two-standard-deviation point, with the explicit expectation that researchers would use judgement and that a single experiment settles nothing.

It hardened into a bright line anyway, largely through textbooks and journal policy. By the late twentieth century, results on one side of 0.05 were publishable and results on the other side frequently were not, which created an obvious and enormous incentive to end up on the correct side.

The consequences arrived in the 2010s as the replication crisis: large coordinated efforts to repeat well-known published findings, across psychology, medicine and economics, succeeded well under half the time. In 2016 the American Statistical Association took the unusual step of issuing a formal statement on the misuse of p-values, and in 2019 hundreds of statisticians signed a call to abandon the notion of statistical significance as a threshold entirely.

The relevance to this field is direct. Machine learning has the same incentive structure (a number must beat a benchmark to be publishable), and considerably weaker statistical hygiene.

§13 Power, and the studies that cannot see

Definition: power

Power is the probability of detecting an effect that is genuinely there. It rises with the size of the effect and with the sample size, and it is the property most often neglected.

There are two ways for a test to be wrong, and they trade against each other:

§14 Multiple comparisons

Go and read

Benjamini & Hochberg (1995), Controlling the false discovery rate. The practical alternative to Bonferroni when you are screening hundreds of hypotheses and cannot afford its severity. Then Ioannidis (2005), Why most published research findings are false, which combines this section, low power and publication incentives into an argument about an entire literature. It applies to benchmark-driven fields exactly as written.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. With $k$ independent tests each at level $\alpha$, the probability of at least one false positive is

$$1 - (1-\alpha)^k$$

which for $\alpha = 0.05$ reaches 40% by $k = 10$, 64% by $k = 20$, and 92% by $k = 50$.

Why this matters: you are already doing this

Every one of these is multiple testing wearing a different name:

  • Hyperparameter search. Two hundred configurations, report the best. That is two hundred tests and one selected maximum.
  • Architecture search. Same, with a larger space.
  • Trying several random seeds and reporting the good one. Same, and harder to defend.
  • Benchmark leaderboards. Hundreds of groups evaluating on one test set. The field as a whole is running an enormous uncorrected search, and the leader is partly selected for luck.

The corrections are simple. Bonferroni (divide your threshold by $k$) is crude and conservative but takes one line. Better: hold out a fresh evaluation set for the selected model only, so the reported number was not part of the search.

§15 Test set reuse

Go and read

Dwork et al. (2015), The reusable holdout: preserving validity in adaptive data analysis. The formal treatment of this section's problem, and unusually it comes with a mechanism rather than a warning: a way of answering queries against a holdout set that survives adaptive reuse, at a stated cost in accuracy.

The most common form of this failure in practice, and the one with the least excuse.

The rule is that a test set is used once. In reality it is used to compare architectures, then to tune a learning rate, then again after a bug fix, then once more for the final table. Each look leaks a little information from test set into model, and the reported number drifts upward while true performance does not move at all.

Intuition: selection is a channel

Every time you look at the test set and let it change a decision, you have moved information from it into your model. Do that enough and the test set stops measuring generalisation and starts measuring how hard you searched.

The clean discipline: validation set for every decision, test set once at the very end. If you must consult the test set repeatedly, treat the final number as an upper bound rather than an estimate, and say so.

§16 Comparing two models honestly

The practical procedure, assembled from the sections above.

A defensible comparison
  1. Report the effect size. Not just "A beat B"; by how much.
  2. Report an interval on the difference. Bootstrap it if no formula exists (§10).
  3. Use a paired comparison where possible. Evaluate both models on the same examples and analyse the per-example differences, which removes the variance due to which examples you drew, which is usually the dominant term.
  4. Account for the search. Say how many configurations were tried, and evaluate the final choice on data that took no part in choosing it.
  5. Vary the seeds. Run each model several times. If the seed-to-seed spread exceeds the gap between models, you have not shown a difference.
Pitfall: the unpaired comparison wastes most of your data

Comparing two accuracy numbers as though they were independent samples throws away the fact that both were measured on the same examples.

Some test examples are simply hard, and both models fail them; some are easy, and both succeed. That shared difficulty is variance common to both measurements, and a paired analysis cancels it exactly. In practice, pairing frequently shrinks the interval on the difference by enough to change the conclusion, from the same data, at no cost.

Try it

You try 40 hyperparameter configurations and report the best validation accuracy. Under a null in which all 40 are genuinely identical, is that number an unbiased estimate of the configuration's true accuracy? If not, which direction is it wrong?

Answer. It is biased upward, and substantially.

Each configuration's measured accuracy is its true accuracy plus noise. Taking the maximum over 40 selects for large positive noise as much as for genuine quality, and with all 40 identical, it selects purely for noise. The expected maximum of 40 draws sits well above their common mean, which is exactly the right panel of Figure 8.

This is why the winner of a large search reliably underperforms its validation score when finally tested. The fix is not to search less; it is to evaluate the selected model on data that had no part in selecting it.
