§23 Principal components analysis, derived from nothing

In 60 seconds

Centre the data, ask which direction it varies along most, and the answer falls out of the spectral theorem: the top eigenvector of the covariance matrix, with the eigenvalue as the variance along it. No optimizer, no training loop; it is a closed form. This is the first real machine learning algorithm in this book, and it is an eigendecomposition asked the right question.

Go and read

Pearson (1901), On lines and planes of closest fit to systems of points in space. PCA arriving first as a geometry problem: find the line that fits a cloud of points best, with no covariance matrix in sight. Then Hotelling (1933), which derives it the way this section does, from the eigenvectors. Two people, thirty years apart, reaching the same object from opposite directions is the strongest evidence that it was worth finding.

Everything in this chapter has been building a toolkit. Here we spend it. PCA is the first genuine machine learning algorithm in this book, and it turns out to be nothing more than an eigendecomposition asked the right question.

The problem

You have $n$ data points, each with $d$ features; say 1,000 images of 784 pixels each. That is a lot of numbers, and most of them are redundant: neighbouring pixels are nearly identical, and the images occupy a tiny sliver of the space of all possible pixel arrangements. You would like to describe each point with far fewer numbers while losing as little as possible.

Concretely: find a $k$-dimensional subspace such that projecting the data onto it loses the least information. That is the whole problem, and now we will discover that linear algebra answers it exactly.

Step 1: centre the data

Subtract the mean of each feature, so the cloud sits around the origin:

$$\boldsymbol{\mu} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{x}_i, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i = \mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}$$

This is not cosmetic. Everything ahead concerns directions through the origin: spans, eigenvectors, subspaces. If the cloud sits far off to one side, the dominant direction found will simply be the direction of the mean, which tells you nothing about how the data varies. Forgetting to centre is the single most common way to get PCA wrong, and the results look plausible enough that the error can go unnoticed.

Step 2: decide what "information" means

We need to make "loses the least information" precise. The standard choice is variance: a direction along which the data spreads out a lot is informative, because points are distinguishable along it; a direction along which every point has nearly the same value is not, because it fails to separate anything.

So: find the direction of maximum variance. Let $\mathbf{u}$ be a candidate direction, constrained to unit length so the question stays about direction rather than scale. The projection of point $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i$ onto $\mathbf{u}$ has signed length $\mathbf{u}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i$, so the variance of the projected data is

$$\text{Var}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(\mathbf{u}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i\right)^2$$

Step 3: recognise the quadratic form

Now watch this expression turn into something we already understand. A squared scalar equals itself times its own transpose, so $(\mathbf{u}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i)^2 = \mathbf{u}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i^\top \mathbf{u}$. Since $\mathbf{u}$ does not depend on $i$, it slides outside the sum:

$$\text{Var}(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{u}^\top \underbrace{\left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i^\top \right)}_{C, \text{ the covariance matrix}} \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}^\top C \mathbf{u}$$

That is a quadratic form, §18 exactly. And $C$ is symmetric, since each $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i^\top$ is symmetric and sums of symmetric matrices are symmetric. If the data is stacked as rows of a matrix $\tilde{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, then $C = \frac{1}{n}\tilde{X}^\top\tilde{X}$, which is how you compute it.

Watch the shapes

$\tilde{X}$ is $n \times d$, so $\tilde{X}^\top\tilde{X}$ is $(d \times \underline{n})(\underline{n} \times d) \to d \times d$. The covariance matrix is feature by feature, not example by example; its size depends on how many features you have, not how much data. Entry $C_{i,j}$ measures how features $i$ and $j$ vary together.

Step 4: maximise it

The problem is now fully stated:

$$\text{maximise } \mathbf{u}^\top C \mathbf{u} \quad \text{subject to } \|\mathbf{u}\|_2 = 1$$

And §17 already solved this, though it did not announce it. Because $C$ is symmetric, the spectral theorem gives $C = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with orthonormal eigenvectors. Write $\mathbf{u}$ in that eigenvector basis as $\mathbf{y} = Q^\top\mathbf{u}$, a rotation, so $\|\mathbf{y}\|_2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|_2 = 1$ by §16, and the objective becomes the weighted sum of squares from §18:

$$\mathbf{u}^\top C \mathbf{u} = \sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2 \quad \text{with} \quad \sum_i y_i^2 = 1$$

You have one unit of weight to distribute across the $y_i^2$, and each unit placed on index $i$ pays $\lambda_i$. Obviously you put it all on the largest eigenvalue. That means $\mathbf{y} = (1, 0, \dots, 0)$, which back in the original coordinates means $\mathbf{u}$ is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue.

The result

The direction of maximum variance is the eigenvector of $C$ with the largest eigenvalue, and the variance along it is that eigenvalue. The best $k$-dimensional subspace is spanned by the top $k$ eigenvectors. These are the principal components.

That is PCA, derived rather than asserted. No optimizer, no training loop, no iteration; a closed-form answer that falls out of the spectral theorem. Notice also that the eigenvectors are automatically perpendicular, so each successive component captures variation genuinely independent of the earlier ones. We did not have to require that; symmetry gave it to us.

Step 5: how much did we keep?

Each eigenvalue is the variance along its component, so the fraction of total variance retained by keeping $k$ components is

$$\text{explained variance} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{d} \lambda_i}$$

The denominator is the total variance; by §21 it is also just $\tr(C)$, so you can compute it by summing the diagonal without touching an eigenvector. Plotting this ratio against $k$ is the standard way to choose how many components to keep: look for where the curve flattens.

The whole algorithm, in code

import numpy as np

def pca(X, k):
    """Project X (n samples x d features) onto its top k principal components."""
    mu = X.mean(axis=0)                     # step 1: centre
    Xc = X - mu

    C = (Xc.T @ Xc) / len(X)                # step 3: covariance, (d, d)

    w, V = np.linalg.eigh(C)                # step 4: eigh -- C is symmetric
    order = np.argsort(w)[::-1]            # eigh returns ASCENDING; we want largest first
    w, V = w[order], V[:, order]

    components = V[:, :k]                   # (d, k) -- the top k directions
    Z = Xc @ components                     # (n, k) -- the compressed data
    explained = w[:k].sum() / w.sum()       # step 5
    return Z, components, mu, explained


# a cloud that genuinely lives near a line
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
latent = rng.normal(size=(260, 2)) * [1.9, 0.42]
R = np.array([[.857, -.515], [.515, .857]])      # rotate by 31 degrees
X = latent @ R.T + [3.1, 2.0]

Z, comps, mu, explained = pca(X, k=1)
print(f"kept {explained:.1%} of the variance with 1 of 2 dimensions")
### kept 95.5% of the variance with 1 of 2 dimensions

# reconstruct: back-project and re-add the mean
X_approx = Z @ comps.T + mu
print(f"mean reconstruction error: {np.abs(X - X_approx).mean():.4f}")
### mean reconstruction error: 0.2133
Pitfall: three ways to get PCA wrong

Forgetting to centre. Without it, the first component points at the mean and the analysis is meaningless. Silent failure: the code runs fine.

Forgetting to sort. np.linalg.eigh returns eigenvalues in ascending order, so the top components are at the end, not the beginning. Slicing V[:, :k] without sorting gives you the $k$ least informative directions: a bug that produces plausible-looking garbage.

Not standardising when units differ. If one feature is measured in metres and another in milligrams, the milligram feature has numerically enormous variance and dominates every component. When features are in different units, divide each by its standard deviation before starting.

The SVD route, which is what you should actually use

History: from skull measurements to the value of a search engine

Karl Pearson introduced the method in 1901 in a paper about fitting lines and planes to clouds of points. He was working in biometrics, where the data of the era was physical measurement: heights, limb lengths, skull dimensions, and the practical problem was that such measurements are heavily correlated and unmanageably numerous.

Harold Hotelling rediscovered and reframed it in 1933, gave it the name principal components, and connected it to the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, which is the derivation in this section.

The same mathematics has since had an unusually eventful career. Turn the technique on faces and the components are the famous eigenfaces of early computer vision. Turn it on a matrix of who watched which film and you get the recommender systems that dominated the Netflix Prize. And the ranking algorithm that made Google (PageRank, 1998) is at bottom the computation of a single dominant eigenvector of an enormous matrix of links.

Pearson was trying to summarise anatomical measurements. The linear algebra did not care what the numbers meant.

There is a second path to the same answer that avoids forming $C$ at all. Take the SVD of the centred data directly, $\tilde{X} = U\Sigma V^\top$. Then

$$C = \frac{1}{n}\tilde{X}^\top \tilde{X} = \frac{1}{n} V \Sigma^\top U^\top U \Sigma V^\top = V \left(\frac{\Sigma^\top\Sigma}{n}\right) V^\top$$

using $U^\top U = I$ from orthogonality. Compare with $C = Q\Lambda Q^\top$: the right singular vectors $V$ are the principal components, and $\lambda_i = \sigma_i^2 / n$. Same answer, obtained without ever building the covariance matrix.

This is preferable in practice for two reasons. Forming $\tilde{X}^\top\tilde{X}$ squares the condition number, which as §13 warned amplifies numerical error. And when $d$ is large (784 pixels, or many thousands of features) the $d \times d$ covariance matrix may be expensive or impossible to hold, while the SVD can be computed directly.

# PCA via SVD: fewer steps, better conditioned, and what real libraries do
Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)

components = Vt[:k].T              # already sorted descending -- no argsort needed
Z = Xc @ components
explained = (s[:k]**2).sum() / (s**2).sum()

Note the small bonus: the SVD returns singular values in descending order already, so the sorting pitfall above simply cannot occur.

Why deep learning cares

PCA itself is used constantly: for visualising high-dimensional data in two dimensions, for compression, for removing correlations before training, for detecting outliers. But the deeper reason it opens this book is that it is the simplest complete instance of the pattern the entire field follows: state a goal precisely, express it in linear algebra, and solve.

PCA is also the honest baseline for representation learning. An autoencoder with linear activations and squared error learns exactly the PCA subspace; it cannot do better. Everything deep learning achieves beyond PCA comes from the nonlinearities, which is why Part XV introduces autoencoders by first explaining what PCA cannot do. You cannot appreciate that argument without this section.

Try it: the last one

A dataset has covariance eigenvalues $[12, 6, 1.5, 0.4, 0.1]$. How many components do you need to retain at least 90% of the variance? And what does the shape of this spectrum suggest about the data?

Answer. Total variance is $12 + 6 + 1.5 + 0.4 + 0.1 = 20$. One component gives $12/20 = 60\%$. Two give $18/20 = 90\%$, just reaching the threshold, so two components suffice. Three would give $97.5\%$.

The spectrum drops sharply after the second eigenvalue and then flattens into a small tail. That pattern says the data is genuinely close to two-dimensional, with the remaining three directions contributing little more than noise. Five features, two real degrees of freedom, a 60% reduction at a cost of 10% of the variance. Real datasets very often look like this, and that is precisely why dimensionality reduction earns its keep.
