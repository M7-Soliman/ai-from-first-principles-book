Part DWhen you cannot randomise

Movement C's estimators all require the confounding to be measured. When it is not, and §14 says that case is often provably hopeless by adjustment alone, the remaining move is to find something in the world that assigns treatment for reasons unrelated to the outcome. This movement is four such designs and an honest accounting of what each one assumes.

§25 Borrowing randomness from the world

The common structure

Every design here locates a source of variation in the treatment that is plausibly independent of the outcome's other causes (a lottery, a rule, a date, a threshold) and uses only that variation.

The gain is that unmeasured confounding stops mattering, because you are no longer comparing the treated with the untreated. The cost is always the same shape: you now depend on an assumption about the source of variation, and that assumption is untestable in exactly the way the one it replaced was.

The trade is worth making when the new assumption is more defensible than the old one. That is a judgement about the domain, and it is the whole skill.

§26 Instrumental variables

The instrument, and the three things it must satisfy

An instrument $Z$ for the effect of $X$ on $Y$ must be:

Relevant: $Z$ actually moves $X$. The only one of the three that is testable.

Exogenous: $Z$ is unconfounded with $Y$.

Excluded: $Z$ affects $Y$ only through $X$. No other path, at all.

Given all three, the effect is recovered by two-stage least squares: regress $X$ on $Z$, then regress $Y$ on the fitted values. The first stage keeps only the part of $X$ that $Z$ explains, which is, by assumption, the unconfounded part.

Pitfall: the exclusion restriction is where instruments die

Relevance is testable and gets tested. Exclusion is untestable and gets asserted, usually in one sentence, and it is a strong claim: this variable affects the outcome through no route other than the treatment.

Weather as an instrument for attendance also affects mood. Distance to a hospital also proxies for wealth and for urbanity. Genetic variants also do other things. Every plausible instrument has a candidate second path, and the question is never whether one exists but whether it is small enough to ignore, which is a judgement, and belongs in the write-up as one.

And the estimand narrows

With a binary treatment and heterogeneous effects, two-stage least squares does not estimate the average effect. It estimates the local average treatment effect: the effect among the units whose treatment status the instrument actually changed: the compliers.

Who those are depends on the instrument, they cannot be identified individually, and the number can differ substantially from the population average. An instrument that answers a narrower question correctly is often better than adjustment that answers the broad one wrongly, but the narrowing has to be stated.

Go and read

Angrist & Pischke (2009), Mostly Harmless Econometrics. The design tradition: instruments, differences, discontinuities, and an unusually honest running commentary on when each one is not credible. Read it alongside Pearl rather than instead: the two fields solved the same problem in different languages and are still translating.

§27 Difference-in-differences

Two groups, two periods

One group is treated between period one and period two; another is not. Take the change in the treated group and subtract the change in the control group.

$$\hat{\tau} = (\bar{Y}^{\text{treat}}_{\text{after}} - \bar{Y}^{\text{treat}}_{\text{before}}) - (\bar{Y}^{\text{ctrl}}_{\text{after}} - \bar{Y}^{\text{ctrl}}_{\text{before}})$$

Differencing within group removes any confounder that is fixed over time, however large and however unmeasured. Differencing across groups removes any trend common to both.

The assumption, and it is about a counterfactual

Parallel trends: absent the treatment, the two groups' outcomes would have moved together. This is a statement about a world that did not happen, so it cannot be tested.

What can be checked is whether the trends were parallel before treatment, and that check should always be run and shown. It is evidence, not proof: pre-treatment parallelism is consistent with post-treatment divergence for reasons unrelated to the treatment, and the usual culprit is that whatever caused the treatment to happen when it did also affected the outcome.

§28 Regression discontinuity

A rule with a threshold

When treatment is assigned by whether a continuous score crosses a cutoff (a test mark, an income limit, a date of birth), units just above and just below are comparable in everything except treatment. Compare them.

The identifying assumption is that everything else varies smoothly through the cutoff, so any jump in the outcome at the threshold is the treatment. This is unusually credible, because the cutoff is typically arbitrary and known.

What to check, and what it costs

Manipulation. If units can move themselves across the cutoff, those who did are selected. Check the density of the running variable for a jump at the threshold.

Other discontinuities. If a second programme uses the same cutoff, the design measures both together and cannot separate them.

Bandwidth. The estimate depends on how wide a window around the cutoff you use, and the result should be reported across a range rather than at one convenient choice.

The estimand, again. The effect is identified at the cutoff. Extrapolating to units far from it is an assumption, not a result.

§29 Synthetic controls

When there is exactly one treated unit

One region adopts a policy; others do not. No single comparison region is right, so build a weighted combination of the untreated units that matches the treated one's pre-treatment trajectory, and use its post-treatment path as the counterfactual.

The weights are chosen to fit the pre-period and are then held fixed, which is the train/test discipline of Part VI applied to a causal design, and the reason the method has credibility that eyeballing a comparison does not.

Pitfall: a good pre-period fit is not validation

With enough donor units and a short pre-period, a close fit is guaranteed and means nothing: it is Part XIV §20's biased maximum, chosen over a large space of weightings. The pre-period must be long relative to the number of donors, and the standard inference is a placebo test: apply the method to each untreated unit in turn and see whether the real effect stands out from the distribution of fake ones.

§30 The four designs, compared

What each buys and what each assumes

Instrumental variables: removes unmeasured confounding entirely. Assumes exclusion, which is untestable. Estimates the effect on compliers. Fails loudly when the instrument is weak (Figure 5) and silently when exclusion is violated.

Difference-in-differences: removes any time-invariant confounder. Assumes parallel trends. Estimates the effect on the treated. Partially checkable pre-treatment.

Regression discontinuity: removes everything that varies smoothly. Assumes no manipulation and no coincident policy. Estimates the effect at the cutoff. Well checkable.

Synthetic control: handles a single treated unit. Assumes the donor pool spans the counterfactual. Estimates the effect on that unit. Checkable by placebo.

Notice that none of them removes an assumption. Each replaces "the confounders are measured" with a different untestable claim, chosen because it is easier to defend. That is what causal design is: not the elimination of assumptions but their relocation to somewhere you can argue about them.

§31 What movement D establishes

Three things

Unmeasured confounding is not the end. Figure 5 recovers a true effect of +1.00 from data where the confounder is never observed and adjustment is provably unavailable, because the design supplied variation that adjustment could not.

Every design relocates an assumption rather than removing one. The four differ in which untestable claim they need, and choosing among them is a question about the domain rather than about statistics.

The estimand narrows every time. Compliers, the treated, the cutoff, that one unit. A narrower question answered credibly beats a broader one answered by assumption, and the narrowing belongs in the sentence reporting the result.
