Part FValidation, and practice

A system that acts can hurt someone, which changes what counts as evidence. This movement is the arithmetic of establishing that a rare failure is rare, and it is unforgiving in a way no accuracy metric prepares you for.

§40 Why a test set is not enough

Three reasons the usual evidence does not transfer

The failures are rare. A metric averaged over a test set is dominated by the common case, and the question is about the tail.

The distribution is yours. A policy's own actions determine what it encounters, so an evaluation on logged data from a different policy is answering a different question: Part XXII's estimand problem, with consequences.

The events are correlated. Failures cluster by weather, location and time, so a thousand miles in one city is not a thousand independent trials.

§41 Stating the requirement

Three things a claim must contain

The operational design domain. Where, when and under what conditions the claim applies. A claim without one is not falsifiable.

The failure being bounded. A collision, an injury, a disengagement, a departure from the lane, these differ by orders of magnitude in rate and are routinely conflated.

The rate and the confidence. "Fewer than one per $N$, with 95% confidence" is a statement; "safer than a human" is not, until each of the three is filled in.

§42 What that costs, measured

The rule of three, and why it surprises people

With zero events in $n$ trials the 95% upper bound on the rate is $3/n$. That is the whole calculation, and its consequence is that a clean record is weak evidence: to bound a rate you must drive several times the reciprocal of it.

The alternative is to model the tail (fit a distribution to near-misses and extrapolate), which gives a far tighter bound at the cost of an assumption about the tail shape that the data cannot check. That is a real trade and not a trick, and the honest form of the argument states which assumption is carrying the conclusion.

§43 The safety case

What an argument has to contain

A structured claim, the evidence for each part, and the assumptions each piece of evidence rests on. Distance driven is one input among several, and on its own it is the weakest.

Decomposition is what makes it tractable: bound the perception failure rate, the planner's failure rate given correct perception, and the controller's given a correct plan, then compose. That composition is only valid if the failures are independent, which they usually are not, and stating that dependence is where an honest case does its work.

Note what this requires: intermediate representations, so that the pieces can be bounded separately. Which is §38's argument arriving as a regulatory constraint rather than an engineering preference.

§44 What is unsolved

Four, distinguished by kind

Not yet measured. How much a stabilising inner loop is worth to a learned outer one. §21 and §23 both say it should be substantial and neither is a measurement of a deployed system.

Not currently measurable. Whether a policy will fail on a situation nobody has thought of. Every evaluation is on scenarios someone assembled.

Genuinely open. Validating a system whose components cannot be bounded separately. This is not a question about accuracy but about whether the argument of §43 can be made at all.

Not empirical. What risk is acceptable, and to whom. Part XXI's subject, and no measurement in this part touches it.

§45 What to carry forward

Five things

Feedback substitutes for knowledge and buys robustness rather than accuracy. Figure 1 measures a 20% model error becoming 4% at high gain, and a cautious gain being worse than no feedback at all.

If you know the dynamics, solve rather than learn. Figure 3 measures hand tuning within 0.1% on two states, 78× worse on three, and unable to stabilise five.

A learned model's usable horizon is set by the plant, not the model. Figure 6 measures error compounding at 1.0667× per step on an unstable system and shrinking at 0.9651× on a stable one, so stabilise before you learn.

Exhaustive planning stops at about six degrees of freedom. Figure 7 measures 12× per joint against 1.32×.

A clean record is weak evidence. Figure 8 measures a million failure-free miles bounding the rate only below one per three hundred thousand, and a claim at one per 108 needing of order 108 miles.

Sixty seconds

Perception ends with a belief; everything after it is deciding what to do, and the field that has been answering that since the 1940s starts from a different place than machine learning does. Its central discovery is that you do not need an accurate model to act accurately: measure the error and correct it, and the system's own parameters stop mattering. Where you do know the dynamics, solving for the controller beats tuning one, and beats it by more the more states there are. Where you do not, you can learn the expert's behaviour and drift off its distribution, or learn the dynamics and find your horizon set by the plant's own stability rather than by your model's accuracy. And a system that acts has to be shown safe, which is arithmetic rather than engineering: a million miles without a failure bounds the failure rate only below one in three hundred thousand, and no amount of good driving changes that.
