Part DMeasuring

§14 Norms

So far we can build vectors and transform them. We cannot yet say how big one is, and almost everything in machine learning is ultimately a size question. How large is this error? How far apart are these two points? How big are these weights? A norm is the tool that answers all of them.

Definition: the $L^p$ family

For $p \geq 1$, the $L^p$ norm of $\mathbf{x}$ is $$\|\mathbf{x}\|_p = \left( \sum_i |x_i|^p \right)^{1/p}$$ Raise every entry's magnitude to the $p$, add them up, take the $p$-th root.

That formula defines a whole family, but three members do essentially all the work.

The first thing to make peace with is that there is a family at all. It is reasonable to expect "how big is this vector" to have one answer, the way "how many entries does it have" does. It does not, and the reason is that size is not a property of the vector; it is a question you ask about the vector, and different questions have different answers.

Consider getting from one street corner to another in a city laid out on a grid, three blocks east and four blocks north. How far is it? A bird flies five blocks. A taxi drives seven, because it cannot pass through buildings. A tired pedestrian who only cares about the longest single stretch says four. All three numbers are correct; they answer different questions, and the questions differ because the constraints differ. Those three answers are exactly $L^2$, $L^1$ and $L^\infty$ of the vector $(3,4)$.

So choosing a norm is choosing what you mean by "big", and the choice has consequences rather than being a matter of taste. As §15 shows, penalising a model's weights under $L^2$ and under $L^1$ does not produce a slightly different model; it produces a qualitatively different one, dense in the first case and sparse in the second. The norm is part of the specification of the problem, not a detail of how you measure the answer.

$L^2$: Euclidean length, the default

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}$$

Ordinary straight-line distance. In two dimensions it is the Pythagorean theorem: $\|(3,4)\|_2 = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$. This is so common that the subscript is usually dropped; a bare $\|\mathbf{x}\|$ means $L^2$ unless stated otherwise.

It has a compact form worth recognising instantly, since it appears constantly:

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}$$

The squared $L^2$ norm is just a dot product of a vector with itself, with no square root in sight.

Why deep learning cares: the squared norm is preferred

You will see $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2$ far more often than $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2$, and the reason is calculus rather than taste. The derivative of $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2$ with respect to $x_i$ is simply $2x_i$: each component's derivative depends only on that component, and the expression is clean everywhere.

The unsquared norm is worse behaved on both counts. Its derivative involves dividing by the norm itself, so every component's derivative depends on all the others, and at $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ the square root has a kink where the derivative does not exist. Since optimization means differentiating over and over, the squared version wins on convenience and on numerical safety. When you see a loss function squaring things, this is usually why.

$L^1$: sum of magnitudes

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = \sum_i |x_i|$$

Also called the Manhattan or taxicab norm, because it measures distance the way a car navigates a grid of streets: you cannot cut diagonally, so you add the horizontal and vertical legs. $\|(3,4)\|_1 = 7$, against $5$ for $L^2$.

$L^1$ has a property that makes it disproportionately important: it treats small values much more harshly than $L^2$ does. Moving a coordinate from $0.1$ to $0$ reduces the $L^1$ norm by exactly $0.1$, regardless of how large the other coordinates are. Under $L^2$, squaring makes $0.1$ contribute a mere $0.01$, so shrinking it is barely worth the optimizer's effort. The consequence is that $L^1$ pushes values to exactly zero while $L^2$ merely makes them small: this is the origin of sparsity, and the reason $L^1$ regularization performs feature selection while $L^2$ does not.

$L^\infty$: the largest entry

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty = \max_i |x_i|$$

Take the limit as $p$ grows and the largest term dominates everything else, so the norm converges to that single biggest magnitude. Useful when you care about the worst case and nothing else: the largest error rather than the total error.

Frobenius: the norm for matrices

To measure a whole matrix, flatten it and take $L^2$:

$$\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} A_{i,j}^2}$$

This is the $L^2$ norm wearing a different name because its argument happens to be rectangular. It is the standard way to measure the size of a weight matrix or the distance between two matrices.

Watch the shapes

Every norm eats a vector or a matrix and returns a single non-negative scalar. $\mathbb{R}^{n} \to \mathbb{R}$, or $\mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \to \mathbb{R}$. If you ever find yourself with a norm that still has axes, something has gone wrong.

What makes something a norm at all

Any function deserving the name must satisfy three conditions, all of which say something obvious about the word "size":

  1. Zero only at zero. $\|\mathbf{x}\| = 0$ exactly when $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Nothing but nothing has zero size.
  2. Absolute scaling. $\|c\mathbf{x}\| = |c|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|$. Doubling a vector doubles its size, and flipping its direction leaves size alone.
  3. Triangle inequality. $\|\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}\| \leq \|\mathbf{x}\| + \|\mathbf{y}\|$. A detour is never shorter than going direct.
Pitfall: the "$L^0$ norm" is not a norm

You will encounter "$L^0$" used for the count of non-zero entries. It is a useful quantity and a genuinely popular piece of notation, but it violates rule 2: doubling a vector does not double its number of non-zeros, it leaves the count unchanged. It is not a norm, and this is worth knowing because it explains a real practical fact: optimizing the non-zero count directly is computationally intractable, and $L^1$ is used as the tractable stand-in that produces sparsity anyway.

Try it

For $\mathbf{x} = (1, -2, 0, 3)$, compute $\|\mathbf{x}\|_1$, $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2$ and $\|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty$. Which is largest, and is that ordering a coincidence?

Answer. $\|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = 1 + 2 + 0 + 3 = 6$. $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 0 + 9} = \sqrt{14} \approx 3.74$. $\|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty = 3$. The ordering $L^1 \geq L^2 \geq L^\infty$ is not a coincidence; it holds for every vector. Larger $p$ concentrates more weight on the biggest entry and discounts the rest, so the measured size falls as $p$ rises.

§15 Norms in practice: regularization and clipping

Norms are not a detour. They are how three of the most common techniques in practical deep learning are defined, and each one is a single norm doing a specific job.

Weight decay ($L^2$ regularization)

When a model memorises its training data instead of learning the pattern, one standard remedy is to add a penalty on the size of the weights:

$$\text{total loss} = \underbrace{\text{prediction error}}_{\text{fit the data}} + \underbrace{\lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2}_{\text{stay small}}$$

The optimizer now faces a trade-off it did not have before: fit the data, but not by using enormous weights. The scalar $\lambda$ sets the exchange rate. Large weights let a model produce wild swings from tiny input changes, which is what overfitting looks like mechanically, so penalising them constrains the model toward smoother behaviour.

You will see this again in earnest in Part V §17–18, where it is derived properly and connected to a Bayesian prior. The linear algebra content is entirely contained in §14.

$L^1$ regularization and sparsity

Swap the penalty to $\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_1$ and the behaviour changes qualitatively. Rather than shrinking all weights toward small values, $L^1$ drives many of them to exactly zero, which amounts to the model deciding, on its own, that those features are not worth using. The geometric reason is visible in Figure 5: the $L^1$ ball has corners on the axes, and constrained optima have a strong tendency to land on corners.

Gradient clipping

During training, gradients occasionally explode: a single enormous update destroys a model that was training fine. The standard defence is a norm check: if the gradient's $L^2$ norm exceeds a threshold, rescale it back down to that threshold while keeping its direction unchanged.

def clip_by_norm(g, max_norm):
    """Cap the size of a gradient, preserve its direction."""
    n = np.linalg.norm(g)                # the L2 norm of the whole gradient
    if n > max_norm:
        g = g * (max_norm / n)           # rescale onto the sphere of radius max_norm
    return g

g = np.array([30., 40.])                  # norm 50 -- far too large
print(clip_by_norm(g, 5.0))            ### [3. 4.]   same direction, norm now 5

Notice that $(30, 40)$ and $(3, 4)$ point exactly the same way; dividing by the norm is precisely the operation that preserves direction while fixing length. This technique is close to mandatory when training recurrent networks, and you will meet it formally in Part XI §13.

The dot product and the angle between vectors

The dot product connects directly to the angle $\theta$ between two vectors: $$\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y} = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2 \|\mathbf{y}\|_2 \cos\theta$$ Dividing both sides by the two norms isolates the angle: $$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 \|\mathbf{y}\|_2}$$ This quantity is cosine similarity. It is 1 when the vectors point the same way, −1 when they are antiparallel and 0 when they are perpendicular. The norms cancel entirely, so it measures direction while ignoring magnitude.

Intuition: normalising to unit length

Dividing any non-zero vector by its own norm produces a vector of length one pointing the same way: $$\hat{\mathbf{u}} = \frac{\mathbf{u}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2}$$ This separates a vector's two pieces of information (which way and how far) and lets you keep one while discarding the other. Gradient clipping keeps the direction and overrides the length. Cosine similarity, used everywhere in embedding search, compares directions while ignoring lengths entirely. It is a small operation that shows up constantly.

Part ESpecial matrices

§16 Diagonal, symmetric, orthogonal

Most matrices have no structure worth exploiting. A few families have a great deal, and recognising them turns expensive operations into cheap ones. These three carry the chapter's remaining weight.

Diagonal

Zero everywhere off the main diagonal:

$$D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$$

Under the function view, a diagonal matrix independently scales each coordinate axis. It stretches $x$ by 2, flips and keeps $y$, and halves $z$. No mixing between axes at all, which is why everything about them is easy:

  • Multiplying: $D\mathbf{x}$ just scales entrywise. Cost $O(n)$ instead of $O(n^2)$.
  • Inverting: reciprocate the diagonal entries. Invertible exactly when none is zero.
  • Storing: $n$ numbers rather than $n^2$.

A $10{,}000 \times 10{,}000$ diagonal matrix takes 10,000 numbers and inverts instantly, where a general one of that size takes a hundred million numbers and a serious computation. This is why so much effort goes into making matrices diagonal, which is exactly what §17 is about.

Symmetric

Equal to its own transpose, $A = A^\top$, so $A_{i,j} = A_{j,i}$ and the matrix is a mirror image across the diagonal:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 7 & 3 \\ 7 & 4 & -2 \\ 3 & -2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

Symmetric matrices arise whenever the underlying quantity is inherently symmetric. Distance matrices are symmetric because the distance from $i$ to $j$ equals the distance from $j$ to $i$. Covariance matrices are symmetric because the covariance of $X$ with $Y$ equals that of $Y$ with $X$; covariance is the engine of PCA in §23. Hessians of twice-differentiable functions are symmetric, which is a fact Part III uses heavily.

Symmetry buys an enormous amount, as §17 will show: symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors, which is emphatically not true in general.

Orthogonal

First, two supporting definitions. Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero, $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v} = 0$, which in two and three dimensions means exactly perpendicular. A set is orthonormal when its vectors are mutually orthogonal and each has length 1.

Definition: orthogonal matrix

A square matrix whose columns are orthonormal. Equivalently, and far more usefully: $$Q^\top Q = Q Q^\top = I, \qquad \text{so} \qquad Q^{-1} = Q^\top$$

That last equality is the payoff and it is remarkable. The inverse is the transpose. Inverting a general $n \times n$ matrix is a substantial computation; inverting an orthogonal one is a relabelling of indices, essentially free and perfectly stable. Whenever you can arrange for a matrix to be orthogonal, you should.

Intuition: orthogonal matrices are rigid motions

Geometrically, an orthogonal matrix is a rotation, a reflection, or a combination of the two. It never stretches, squashes, or shears; it moves space around without deforming it. Two consequences follow directly, and both matter:

Lengths are preserved: $\|Q\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2$ for every $\mathbf{x}$. Angles are preserved too. So an orthogonal transformation changes your point of view without changing the geometry of what you are looking at.

Why deep learning cares

Length preservation is exactly the property that prevents signals from exploding or vanishing as they pass through many layers. If every layer's weight matrix were orthogonal, a signal would arrive at layer fifty with the same magnitude it had at layer one. This is the motivation behind orthogonal weight initialization, and it is one of the reasons deep networks became trainable at all: the vanishing and exploding gradient problems of Part VII §18 and Part XI §12–13 are, in part, a story about how far a network's weight matrices have drifted from orthogonality.

Try it

Verify that $Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is orthogonal. Then, without computing anything further, state $Q^{-1}$.

Answer. Column 1 is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, with length $\sqrt{\frac12 + \frac12} = 1$. Column 2 is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1,1)$, also length 1. Their dot product is $\frac12(1 \cdot -1 + 1 \cdot 1) = 0$, so they are perpendicular. Orthonormal columns, therefore orthogonal.

Hence $Q^{-1} = Q^\top = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, obtained with no arithmetic at all. Geometrically $Q$ rotates the plane by 45°, and its inverse rotates back by 45°, which is what the sign flip encodes.
