Part AFeedback

The oldest idea in this part, and the one a machine-learning training makes easiest to miss: you do not need an accurate model to control something accurately. Measuring the error and correcting it converts a modelling problem into a much smaller one, and it introduces a failure mode that has no analogue anywhere else in this book.

§1 Prediction is not control

Three things that change when the output acts

The data stops being independent of you. A predictor sees a distribution it did not cause. A controller's action determines what it observes next, so the distribution is a consequence of the policy, which is Part XVIII's premise and Part XXII's intervention, arriving as an engineering constraint.

Errors accumulate. A misclassified image is one mistake. A mistaken action moves the system somewhere, and the next decision is made from there.

There is a deadline. A controller that is right too late is wrong. Latency is part of the specification, not an implementation detail, and §7 is what it does to stability.

§2 Open loop, and why it fails

Commanding without looking

Compute the input your model says produces the outcome you want, and apply it. If the model is right and nothing disturbs the system, this is optimal and cheap.

Neither condition ever holds. The error in the outcome is proportional to the error in the model, so a 20% gain error is a 20% miss, and a disturbance is uncorrected forever because nothing is watching.

§3 Closing the loop

Proportional control

Measure the output, subtract it from the target, and drive the input in proportion: $u = k_p (r - y)$. The remarkable property is that the closed-loop response depends on the plant's gain $g$ only through $k_p g / (1 + k_p g)$, which approaches one as the loop gain grows, whatever $g$ is.

So a large loop gain makes the system's own parameters nearly irrelevant. That is the central fact of the subject and the reason a thermostat needs no thermodynamics.

§4 Measured

Two things this says

Feedback buys robustness, not accuracy. It makes the answer insensitive to what you got wrong; it does not make it right. The residual offset is a property of the loop.

A little feedback can be worse than none. This is the sentence to keep. Adding a correction term with a cautious gain, on a system you modelled well, can increase the error, and the instinct that feedback is monotonically helpful is the instinct that produces it.

§5 The integral term

Removing the offset

Accumulate the error and feed that back too: $u = k_p e + k_i \int e$. At equilibrium the integral is only constant if the error is zero, so any steady offset keeps growing the input until it disappears. The offset does not shrink; it goes to zero, exactly.

The derivative term completes the trio, responding to how fast the error is changing. It damps overshoot and amplifies measurement noise, which is why it is the term most often set to zero in practice.

§7 Delay, and why it destabilises

Pitfall: the correction arrives for a situation that has passed

Every loop has lag: sensing, computing, actuating. A correction based on an old measurement is applied to a system that has moved on, and if the lag is long relative to the dynamics the correction can be pushing the wrong way by the time it lands. Past a point the loop oscillates, and past another it diverges.

This is why inference latency is a stability question and not only a performance one. A perception stack that takes 200 ms produces a control loop with 200 ms of dead time, and no amount of accuracy compensates. It is also why Figure 2's threshold exists at all: the actuator lag is what creates a margin to lose.
