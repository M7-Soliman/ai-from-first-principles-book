PracticeDrills

Thirty-seven problems. Most of them are arithmetic you can do before requesting any hardware, which is the part's whole argument.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · The compute in a training run

A 7-billion-parameter model trained on 2 trillion tokens. Write down the total training FLOPs before applying any formula.

Answer. 8.4×1022, from C ≈ 6ND. That six (two for the forward pass, four for the backward) is the constant worth memorising, because it converts a model card into a compute budget, and a compute budget into a dollar figure, in one line. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

WarmW1 · The three quantities

Name them and give the relation between them for a dense transformer.

Answer. Parameters $N$, tokens $D$, compute $C \approx 6ND$: two FLOPs per parameter per token forward, four backward. Inference is $2N$ per token.
WarmW2 · What the irreducible term is

What is $E$ in $L = A N^{-\alpha} + E$, and why is it never checked?

Answer. The entropy of the data. It is not checked because estimating a natural corpus's entropy is as hard as modelling it (the best estimate available is the best model), so $E$ is fitted from the same data as everything else.
WarmW3 · Sixteen bytes

How much memory does a parameter need during mixed-precision Adam training, before any activations?

Answer. 16 bytes: 2 for the bf16 weight, 2 for its gradient, and 12 for the optimiser: an fp32 master copy plus two fp32 moments. Twelve of the sixteen belong to the optimiser, not the model.
WarmW4 · Why bf16 needs no loss scaling

Both are 16 bits. What is different?

Answer. bf16 keeps fp32's 8-bit exponent and spends only 7 bits on the mantissa, so it represents the same range of magnitudes more coarsely. fp16 spends 5 on the exponent, so it has more precision and cannot represent small gradients at all.
WarmW5 · The bubble

Give the pipeline bubble formula and its value at $P=8$, $M=8$.

Answer. $(P-1)/(M+P-1)$, so $7/15 \approx 47\%$. Filling and draining costs $P-1$ slots whatever $M$ is, so the fraction falls with more microbatches and never reaches zero.
WarmW6 · What an all-reduce does not depend on

State the volume, and the variable that is conspicuously absent.

Answer. About $2N$ values per device, independent of the batch size. So the communication is fixed per step while the compute is not, and the only lever is tokens per device.
WarmW7 · Where tensor parallelism lives

Why is it used within a node and pipeline parallelism across nodes?

Answer. Tensor parallelism communicates twice per layer on the critical path and cannot be overlapped; pipeline parallelism sends one activation tensor per stage boundary. The chatty form goes where the wires are fast.
WarmW8 · The schedule error

In one sentence, what did Kaplan's study get wrong and which way did it bias the answer?

Answer. A cosine schedule sized for the longest run, with shorter runs stopped partway down it, which penalises short runs, makes data look less valuable than it is, and tilts the fitted optimum toward large models and too few tokens.
WarmW9 · Why overtrain

Chinchilla says 20 tokens per parameter. Why do deployed models use hundreds?

Answer. Because that rule minimises training compute. If the model will serve traffic, inference cost scales with $N$ and recurs, so the lifetime optimum is a smaller model trained longer. The same arithmetic, a different objective.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Count a transformer

Write the parameter count by part for a given width, depth, vocabulary and context. Confirm the feed-forward is twice the attention.

Answer. $4d^2$ per layer for attention, $8d^2$ for the feed-forward, so two thirds of the blocks are not attention. Part XII's finding, as arithmetic, and the reason MoE replaces the feed-forward.
CoreC2 · Fit a law, and break it

Generate points from a known power law, recover the parameters, then re-fit with $E$ pinned to a wrong value and report the exponent you get.

Answer. The exponent moves. $\alpha$ and $E$ trade off over any finite range, so two studies quoting different exponents may differ only in how they handled the floor.
CoreC3 · Solve the allocation

Minimise $A N^{-\alpha} + B D^{-\beta}$ subject to $6ND = C$ by hand, and confirm your code agrees. What do $N$ and $D$ scale as?

Answer. $N \propto C^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}$ and $D \propto C^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}$. Equal exponents give the Chinchilla split, both $C^{1/2}$; a larger $\beta$ pushes the budget into parameters, which is the shape of Kaplan's answer.
CoreC4 · The lifetime optimum

Minimise $6ND + 2N S$ for serving volume $S$, at fixed target loss. Plot the optimal tokens per parameter against $S$.

Answer. It rises without bound as $S$ grows. This is §14 as a curve, and it explains ratios of hundreds without any appeal to a new scaling law.
CoreC5 · A scaling law with a known floor

Build a synthetic source whose entropy rate you can compute exactly. Train models across at least two orders of magnitude of size and fit the law. Compare the fitted $E$ to the truth.

Answer. The check nobody can run on real data. Also try it with a source that has no compositional structure (independent conditionals per context) and observe that no power law appears at all, because the task is memorisation rather than generalisation.
CoreC6 · Reproduce the schedule error

Train one model at several token budgets, twice: with the cosine schedule matched to each budget, and with it fixed at the longest budget and stopped early. Plot the penalty.

Answer. The penalty is largest at the smallest budget and shrinks to zero at the longest, a systematic tilt against short runs, not a constant offset. That tilt is what moves the fitted optimum.
CoreC7 · One learning rate across sizes

Fit a scaling law twice: once with a single learning rate for all sizes, once tuning it at each. Compare the exponents.

Answer. The single-rate version penalises the largest models and flattens the law. It is the same class of error as C6 at smaller stakes, and it is why §4's protocol puts per-size tuning first.
CoreC8 · The memory budget

Write a function giving GiB per device for a configuration, and use it to find the largest model that trains on 8 devices of 80 GiB at context 8192.

Answer. Do it before requesting hardware. Note which term dominates as you raise the context, and that it is the one sharding does not touch.
CoreC9 · What sharding does not fix

Plot memory per device against sharding degree for stages 0–3, with activations included. Find the degree past which more devices stop helping.

Answer. It flattens onto the activation floor. Anyone planning capacity from the sharding factor alone will be wrong by an order of magnitude, which is why recomputation and sharding are used together.
CoreC10 · Recomputation, priced

Compare stored against recomputed activations for memory, and estimate the compute cost of each. Then find the selective policy that recovers most of the memory for a fraction of the compute.

Answer. Full recomputation costs about one extra forward pass, roughly 30%. Recomputing only the cheap-and-large tensors recovers most of the memory for a few per cent, which is why nobody uses the all-or-nothing version.
CoreC11 · The formats, from the bits

Implement the range and precision of a floating-point format from its exponent and mantissa widths. Reproduce fp32, fp16 and bf16's known constants.

Answer. fp16's largest is 65504 and its smallest normal $6.1\times10^{-5}$; bf16's largest is $3.4\times10^{38}$. Getting these from the bit layout is worth doing once, because the whole mixed-precision discussion follows from them.
CoreC12 · Loss scaling, derived

Take a realistic gradient distribution. Report the fraction lost in each format, then find the smallest power of two that recovers 99.9% in fp16.

Answer. That search is what dynamic loss scaling does at run time. Note that bf16 needs none of it, and that fp8 needs per-tensor scales because one global constant cannot serve tensors with different magnitudes.
CoreC13 · Data-parallel efficiency

Plot the fraction of step time spent computing against tokens per device, at three interconnect bandwidths. Find the tokens per device needed for 90% at each.

Answer. The curves shift but do not change shape, because the communication is fixed and the compute is not. This is why weak scaling works and strong scaling does not.
CoreC14 · Overlap

Model the all-reduce as overlapping with the backward pass in buckets. How much of the communication can be hidden, and what limits it?

Answer. Most of it, except the last bucket, which has nothing left to overlap with. That residue is why bucket size is tuned and why the last layer's gradients are reduced first.
CoreC15 · Enumerate the configurations

For 512 devices, list every (tensor, pipeline, data) split with tensor $\le 8$. Score each with a cost model and rank them.

Answer. The space is small enough to search exhaustively, and doing so before the run routinely finds a configuration substantially faster than the obvious one. It is a discrete search, not an experiment.
CoreC16 · The bubble, and the memory it costs

For each pipeline depth, find the microbatches needed for a 10% bubble, then compute the activation memory that many microbatches in flight requires.

Answer. The two move against each other: the thing that shrinks the bubble is the thing that fills memory. That tension is why 1F1B schedules exist, and why interleaving trades communication for it.
CoreC17 · MoE accounting

Compute total and active parameters for a model whose feed-forward blocks are replaced by $E$ experts with top-$k$ routing. Then say which of the two determines memory and which determines compute.

Answer. Total determines memory: every expert must be resident. Active determines compute. Reporting only the total is how a model is described as far larger than its cost, and reporting only the active hides the hardware it needs.
CoreC18 · The balancing loss

Implement the auxiliary loss and confirm it is minimised at uniform routing and maximal under collapse. Then train a small MoE with and without it and report the expert load.

Answer. Without it the router collapses, because an expert receiving more tokens improves faster and is chosen more often, Part XIII §30's feedback loop, inside a model.
CoreC19 · Tokens dropped

For a measured routing distribution, plot the fraction of tokens dropped against capacity factor. Find the factor needed for under 1%.

Answer. Dropped tokens pass through the residual with no expert applied, and the loss curve shows a mild degradation with no indication of the cause. This is the number to log, and it is off by default.
CoreC20 · The checkpoint interval

Derive the optimal interval from the checkpoint cost and the mean time to failure, and confirm numerically that it minimises the expected overhead.

Answer. $\sqrt{2 T_c T_f}$. Then list what must be saved besides the weights (optimiser states, data position, RNG state, schedule step) because anything omitted makes a resumed run differ from an uninterrupted one.
CoreC21 · The cost model

Estimate wall-clock and dollar cost for a run of your choosing, then state the sustained-FLOPs fraction you assumed and how wrong the estimate is if it is 40% instead of 100%.

Answer. A factor of two and a half in both schedule and budget. The fraction is the assumption that most often makes a plan wrong, and it is measurable within the first hour of a run.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · The full surface

Fit $L(N, D)$ jointly over a grid of sizes and budgets, then predict the loss of a run outside the grid and check it.

Answer. The prediction is the whole practical value of scaling laws. Note how far you can extrapolate before the residual grows, and treat that distance as the honest limit.
StretchS2 · Emergence, deflated

Take a multi-step task and measure both exact-match accuracy and per-step loss across model sizes. Show the same runs producing a sharp curve and a smooth one.

Answer. If per-step accuracy $p$ improves smoothly and the metric is $p^k$, the metric looks flat then rises sharply while nothing discontinuous happened. Part XIV §33, at the largest scale in the field.
StretchS3 · The critical batch size

Measure the gradient noise scale during a real run and watch it change. Then verify that batches above it buy proportionally fewer steps.

Answer. It rises as training proceeds, which is the argument for ramping the batch size. And note the conflict with §22: a batch above the critical size is buying hardware utilisation with sample efficiency.
StretchS4 · Hyperparameter transfer

Sweep the learning rate at several widths under standard parameterisation and again under μP. Plot the optimum against width for both.

Answer. Under standard parameterisation it drifts; under μP it is roughly flat, which is what makes tuning small and transferring valid. Verify on your own models rather than trusting it.
StretchS5 · Induce a loss spike

Train with a rate slightly too high in fp16 and provoke one. Then fix it four ways (clipping, bf16, query–key normalisation, batch skipping) and report which worked.

Answer. Doing this once at small scale is worth a great deal, because at large scale the spike costs a restart and you will not get to experiment.
StretchS6 · The whole plan

Given a device count, memory per device, interconnect bandwidth and a compute budget, produce a full training plan: model size, tokens, parallel configuration, batch size, checkpoint interval, and a wall-clock estimate.

Answer. Every number in it comes from this part, and none of it requires running anything. That is the claim §39 makes, and this drill is the test of it.
