Part CIn-context learning

The capability nobody trained for. Show a model a few examples in its prompt and it performs the task, with no weight changing, and the mechanism is now partly understood, which is unusual for anything in this part.

§17 The phenomenon

Go and read

Brown et al. (2020), Language models are few-shot learners. The paper that made in-context learning a subject. Read §3's task list for the breadth and §5's honest limitations, which are more careful than the reception was.

What was observed

Put a handful of input–output pairs in the context, then a new input. The model produces the matching output. Nothing was updated: the same weights, one forward pass, and the "learning" happened entirely inside the activations.

This was not an objective anyone optimised. It appeared as a property of models trained to predict the next token at sufficient scale, and it is the single clearest case in this book of a capability arriving unrequested.

§18 Why it is not mysterious

Go and read

Olsson et al. (2022), In-context learning and induction heads. Ties the capability to a mechanism and to a moment in training: one of very few results here that explains rather than describes. Read it after Part XII §37, which measured the heads themselves.

The pretraining objective already required it

A large corpus contains a great many documents with internal structure: lists, tables, translations, question-and-answer pairs, code with consistent conventions. To predict the later parts of such a document you must infer the pattern from its earlier parts.

So the ability to read a rule off the context and apply it was selected for by next-token prediction, on documents that happened to be structured. In-context learning is not a separate capability that emerged alongside language modelling; it is a thing language modelling requires, which becomes visible when the context is arranged to make it the only useful strategy.

Part XII §37's induction heads are the mechanistic version: circuits that find an earlier occurrence of the current token and copy what followed it. They form over a narrow band of training and their appearance coincides with the ability appearing.

§19 What the demonstrations actually supply

Go and read

Min et al. (2022), Rethinking the role of demonstrations. The finding that randomising demonstration labels costs surprisingly little, which reads as a flat contradiction of this section's figure until you notice the difference: their tasks were ones the model could already do. Read both and keep the design lesson rather than either conclusion.

The experiment, and why it is the one that settles it

The obvious worry about any in-context learning result is that the task was in the training data: the model is not learning from the demonstrations, it is recognising the task and recalling what it knows.

The construction here removes that possibility by force. Every task uses a mapping drawn at random at test time, so there is nothing about it in the weights and no amount of memorisation helps. Then the three conditions separate what the demonstrations contribute:

  • Correct labels: the model can do the task.
  • Permuted labels: if it is reading the mapping, its answers should permute too.
  • Random labels: if the demonstrations only supplied the format, performance should survive.
Pitfall: the literature disagrees, and the disagreement is about the setup

Published results on this are genuinely mixed. Some studies find that randomising the labels costs surprisingly little, and conclude the demonstrations mainly convey the format: the label space, the shape of an answer, the register. Others find it costs a great deal.

Both are right about their own setting, and the difference is whether the mapping was learnable from the weights. With a familiar task, a large model can recover the intended mapping from the inputs alone, so corrupting the labels costs little; with a genuinely novel mapping, as here, it cannot, and the labels are all there is.

The transferable lesson is about experimental design rather than about models: a result that demonstrations "only supply the format" is a statement about tasks the model already knows. The clean question requires a task it cannot know, and constructing one is the work.

§20 Prompting

Why an instruction works at all

Once the model reads rules from its context, an instruction is a rule stated directly rather than demonstrated. Zero-shot prompting is in-context learning with the examples replaced by a description, which only works because instruction tuning (§25) made descriptions a familiar way for a task to be specified.

The techniques that reliably help are the ones that give the model more to condition on: stating the role, specifying the output format, giving a few examples, and (§21) asking for the intermediate steps.

§21 Chain of thought

Go and read

Wei et al. (2022), Chain-of-thought prompting elicits reasoning in large language models. The observation, and the scaling behaviour that made it interesting. Read it alongside the work on faithfulness, which establishes that the chain helps without being a transcript of what produced the answer.

Why writing the steps is not a prompting trick

A transformer does a fixed amount of computation per token: a fixed number of layers, whatever the question. A problem needing more sequential steps than the model has layers cannot be done in one forward pass, however large the model is.

Producing intermediate tokens changes that. Each generated token is another forward pass, and the earlier outputs are in the context for the later ones, so the sequence of tokens is the sequence of computational steps, and depth in time substitutes for depth in layers.

That is why the effect appears at all, and why it is not really about prompting. The prompt is the mechanism by which you ask for more compute; §37's test-time compute is the same observation taken seriously.

Be careful with the obvious next prediction, that the gap should therefore widen with problem length. It widens only while the direct model still has room to fall. Past the depth it can manage in one pass it is at chance and can fall no further, while the model writing its work now has more steps in which to make a mistake, so beyond that point the gap narrows with length, and both facts have the same cause. Figure 4 is entirely in that second regime.

Pitfall: the stated reasoning is not necessarily the actual reasoning

A written chain of steps is a plausible-looking derivation, produced by the same next-token prediction as everything else. It is not a transcript of the computation.

The measurement that shows this: corrupt an intermediate step and the model frequently reaches the same final answer anyway; or prompt it toward a wrong answer and the chain will often rationalise its way there. The chain helps (the figure measures that), and it is not faithful, and both are true at once.

The consequence for anything that matters: a reasoning trace is not an explanation, and reading one as evidence that the model reasoned correctly is a mistake. Part XXI returns to this, because it is the main obstacle to using these traces for oversight.

§22 The sensitivity problem

Go and read

Zhao et al. (2021), Calibrate before use: improving few-shot performance of language models. How far performance moves with the order and formatting of demonstrations, and a cheap correction. The measurement is what makes single-prompt evaluation indefensible.

Pitfall: prompt sensitivity is a measurement problem before it is a usability one

Performance varies substantially with things that should not matter: the order of the demonstrations, the exact wording, the formatting, even whitespace. Reported swings on standard benchmarks from reordering examples alone are large enough to change a paper's conclusion.

So a single-prompt evaluation measures a prompt as much as a model, and comparing two models each with its own tuned prompt compares the prompt engineering. Part XIV §20's inflation, in a setting where the search is usually undocumented.

What to do is Part XIV's answer: evaluate over a distribution of prompts and report the spread, not the best. And note the diagnostic value: a task where performance swings with formatting is one the model is doing fragilely, whatever its best number says.

§23 What prompting cannot fix

Three limits

Absent knowledge. If the fact is not in the weights, no phrasing retrieves it. That is §34's job.

Absent capability. Prompting selects among behaviours the model has; it does not create one. A model that cannot do the task produces a confident, well-formatted failure.

The context window. Everything prompting supplies must fit, is re-sent every request, and is subject to §12's position effect.

The first two are why the ordering in §39 exists, and the third is why it is not simply "prompt harder".

§24 What movement C establishes

Three things

Reading a rule from the context is something next-token prediction required, not a separate gift. It has a mechanism, it has a moment in training when it appears, and it is measurable with a task the model cannot have memorised. Figure 3 is that measurement: a mapping invented at test time, read correctly 0.442 of the time against 0.250 chance, and (the part that settles it) read into the permutation at 0.198 when the demonstrations are permuted.

Producing intermediate tokens is producing computation. Figure 4 puts the direct answer at chance at every depth and the written-out answer at 1.000 to 0.677. That reframes prompting as compute allocation, and it is the observation that the reasoning-model line of work took seriously.

Both results are about the mechanism, not about its size. Neither figure supports a clean scaling story: the demonstration curve falls rather than rises, for a reason that is a property of how the task was built rather than of in-context learning; and the chain-of-thought gap does not widen smoothly with depth. The existence of these effects replicates easily. Their shape is where the careless claims live, and where §23 said prompting research mostly goes wrong.

Sixty seconds

A model trained only to predict the next token can be handed a rule it has never seen, in the prompt, and apply it, and you can prove it is the rule and not the format by permuting the answers and watching the model follow them into being wrong. Separately, letting a model write intermediate steps gives it more forward passes, which lets it solve problems that do not fit in its layer budget at all. One is about reading; the other is about compute. Together they are why a text-completion engine behaves like something you can instruct.
