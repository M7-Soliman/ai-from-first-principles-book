PracticeDrills

Fifty-two drills. The harness in parts/23-theory/drills/ covers twenty-seven of them with thirty-nine tests. The theory here makes checkable claims, so most of these drills are the check: a bound is a number, a tail is a frequency, a VC dimension is a finite enumeration.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · What the bound asks for

A hypothesis class with VC dimension 106: a modest network. Write down how many samples the classical bound requires for a generalisation gap of 0.1.

Answer. About 108, from roughly d/ε2. Networks with far more parameters generalise from far fewer examples, every day. That gap is not a detail to be tightened; it is Part XXIII's subject, and Figure 3 measures how wide it is. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

Warm-upW1 · The two risks

Define true and empirical risk, and say which one you can compute.

Answer. You can compute the empirical one and you care about the other. The whole subject is the difference, and it is hard only because you chose the hypothesis using the same data you are evaluating it on.
Warm-upW2 · What Hoeffding does not use

Two coins, one fair and one landing heads once in twenty. What does Hoeffding say about each at $n = 200$?

Answer. The same thing. It uses only the range, so its curve is identical in both panels of Figure 1, which is the entire meaning of distribution-free, and it costs four orders of magnitude at $p = 0.05$.
Warm-upW3 · Which bound to use

At $p = 0.5$ Bernstein is looser than Hoeffding. Why, and when should you prefer it?

Answer. It pays a worse constant for the privilege of using the variance, and at $p = 0.5$ the variance is at its largest so there is nothing to gain. Prefer it when the variance is small relative to the range. Neither dominates.
Warm-upW4 · The rate

You halve the target error. What happens to the sample size?

Answer. It quadruples: $n \sim 1/\varepsilon^2$. The rate is $1/\sqrt{n}$ and it is tight, so no cleverness improves it.
Warm-upW5 · Why the logarithm matters

You double the number of models in your search. What does the bound cost you?

Answer. A constant added under the square root, not a factor. That is why searching over many candidates is affordable, and why §4 is the first theorem in this book that licenses choosing after seeing the data.
Warm-upW6 · Shattering, by hand

Four points in the plane forming a convex quadrilateral. Which labelling defeats every hyperplane?

Answer. The diagonal pairing: XOR. This is Part VII §1's opening problem and the $d=2$ case of Figure 2's collapse at $m = d + 2$.
Warm-upW7 · Existential and universal

VC dimension asks for some set of points and every labelling. Why does swapping the quantifiers give a different and useless definition?

Answer. "Every set of points" would be defeated by any degenerate arrangement: three collinear points are not shattered by a line in the plane even though the VC dimension is 3. The definition asks what the class can do, not what it does everywhere.
Warm-upW8 · Vacuous

A generalisation bound evaluates to 4.5. What have you learned?

Answer. Nothing. An error rate lies in [0,1], so the bound is satisfied by a random classifier and excludes no possibility. Figure 3's right panel reads 1.1, 4.5 and 10.0 at ordinary network sizes.
Warm-upW9 · Why sharpening the constants will not help

Someone proposes tightening the VC bound by a factor of ten. Does that rescue it for a network with more parameters than samples?

Answer. No. The bound is at least $\sqrt{d/n} > 1$ before any constant is chosen, and networks demonstrably fit random labels so the capacity really is maximal. §13's lower bound then forecloses any distribution-free repair.
Warm-upW10 · Read Figure 4

Where is the peak, and what is at that point?

Answer. At $p \approx n$, where exactly one interpolating solution exists and there is no freedom to choose a well-behaved one. Past it there are infinitely many and the solver picks the minimum-norm one.
Warm-upW11 · What regret compares against

Can regret be negative?

Answer. Yes. The comparator is the best fixed action, not the best sequence, so an adaptive strategy can beat it. That is precisely why no-regret is a weaker promise than the name suggests.
Warm-upW12 · Which shift is correctable

Covariate, label and concept shift. Which can you fix with unlabelled test data?

Answer. The first two. Concept shift changed $p(y \mid x)$ itself, which is exactly what you need labels to see, so Part XXI §35's sampled human review is not substitutable.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Simulate the tail

Implement hoeffding_bound, bernstein_bound and empirical_tail. Reproduce Figure 1 and confirm neither bound is ever violated.

Answer. Both hold everywhere, as they must. The interesting number is the ratio: roughly an order of magnitude where they do best.
CoreC2 · Where the bounds cross

Find the variance at which Bernstein overtakes Hoeffding at fixed $n$ and $\varepsilon$.

Answer. Solve the exponents for equality. Having done it once you will stop treating "use the tighter bound" as a well-defined instruction.
CoreC3 · The price of being distribution-free

Compute the $n$ Hoeffding demands for a 95% interval of width 0.02 at $p = 0.01$, and compare with the exact binomial answer.

Answer. A large multiple. That ratio is what a guarantee holding for every distribution costs when you happen to know yours.
CoreC4 · Sauer's dichotomy

Implement sauer_bound. Verify it equals $2^m$ up to the VC dimension and is polynomial after.

Answer. There is nothing between "every labelling" and "polynomially many", and that gap is what converts a combinatorial fact into a $\sqrt{d \log n / n}$ rate.
CoreC5 · Separability by linear program

Implement is_separable exactly, with an LP. Do not fit a classifier and check its training error.

Answer. A fitted classifier failing to separate is evidence about the optimiser, not about the class. The LP either finds a feasible point or proves none exists, which is what the definition requires.
CoreC6 · Measure a VC dimension

Reproduce Figure 2: enumerate all $2^m$ labellings over random point sets and find where shattering stops.

Answer. Exactly $d+1$, and the transition is total rather than gradual: 1.00 to 0.00 with nothing between.
CoreC7 · Axis-aligned rectangles

Work out the VC dimension of axis-aligned rectangles in the plane, then verify it by enumeration.

Answer. Four. Extend the harness to a second hypothesis class and the machinery you wrote for C5 stops being about hyperplanes.
CoreC8 · The bound against the gap

Reproduce Figure 3's left panel: measure the real gap for a linear classifier and plot the VC bound beside it.

Answer. 14× at $n=100$, over 100,000× by $n = 7{,}618$ where the gap has closed and the bound is still 0.112. Valid throughout, and increasingly uninformative.
CoreC9 · Make the bound vacuous

Implement vc_bound and evaluate it at network sizes. Watch what happens when the dimension exceeds twice the sample.

Answer. The naive expression takes the square root of a negative number. Floor the log at zero and it reduces to $\sqrt{d/n}$, already above 1. The first version of this book's Figure 3 printed nan for exactly the sizes the figure exists to discuss.
CoreC10 · Fit random labels

Train a network to convergence on correctly labelled data, then on randomly labelled data. Report the training error of each.

Answer. Zero in both cases. So the class can realise any labelling of the sample, its VC dimension is at least $n$, and uniform convergence over it provably says nothing, while the model trained on real labels still generalises. Both facts at once is the whole problem.
CoreC11 · Rademacher complexity, measured

Implement rademacher_linear using the closed form for norm-bounded linear functions, and check it falls as $1/\sqrt{n}$.

Answer. The supremum is $(B/n)\lVert\sum_i \sigma_i x_i\rVert$. Notice that computing it is the randomised-label experiment: the bound and the diagnostic are the same procedure.
CoreC12 · Double descent

Reproduce Figure 4. Sweep the feature count through $p = n$ with a minimum-norm solver.

Answer. Peak 4.708 at $p = 115$ against $n = 120$; second descent to 0.224, below the classical best of 0.541. Then add ridge regularization and watch the peak disappear, which tells you what the peak was.
CoreC13 · Which interpolant

Reproduce Figure 5: construct several exact fits of the same underdetermined problem and compare their norms and test errors.

Answer. Gradient descent from zero matches the minimum-norm solution to fifteen digits, and test error rises with norm from 6.2 to 109.5. The objective cannot distinguish them; the algorithm does.
CoreC14 · Change the starting point

Run C13's gradient descent from a large random initialisation instead of zero.

Answer. It converges to a different interpolant: the one nearest the start in the row space. The implicit bias is toward the initialisation, and "minimum norm" is the special case of starting at the origin.
CoreC15 · Defeat follow-the-leader

Implement follow_the_leader and regret, then construct the sequence that gives it linear regret.

Answer. Alternate which action is best. The leader flips every round and is wrong every round while the best fixed action is right half the time. The failure is instability, which is §19's condition arriving in a different theory.
CoreC16 · Multiplicative weights

Implement it and run it on C15's sequence. Sweep the learning rate.

Answer. Sublinear regret on the sequence that defeats the leader, and the learning rate trades adaptivity against stability: too large and you have rebuilt follow-the-leader.
CoreC17 · The cost of partial feedback

Run the same experts problem with full information and then bandit feedback. Compare regret.

Answer. A factor of about $\sqrt{K}$. That number is what not seeing the counterfactual costs, and it is the same missing quantity as Part XXII §32.
CoreC18 · When weighting helps and when it does not

Reproduce Figure 6. Run the shift sweep with a correctly specified model and with a misspecified one.

Answer. Nothing to gain when the model is right, 0.090 against 0.091, and a factor of ten when it is not. Then measure the variance the weights add, which is what you pay for a correction you did not need.
CoreC19 · Label shift, from unlabelled data

Implement label_shift_weights by inverting a confusion matrix, and recover a prior change you set.

Answer. Exactly, up to estimation error. It needs only unlabelled target data, which makes it the cheapest correction in the movement and the most often missed.
CoreC20 · Worst group against average

Reproduce Figure 7. Train to minimise the average and then the worst group, and report both by group.

Answer. 0.994 and 0.215 against 0.822 and 0.795, and the robust model has the better overall accuracy too, because the average was computed on a group mixture that did not survive to test. Below chance is a signature, not a bad score.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · A non-vacuous bound

Implement a PAC-Bayes bound and evaluate it on a small trained network.

Answer. With compression it can come in under 1, genuinely non-vacuous, and still far from the true error. Note carefully which object it bounds: usually a stochastic or compressed network, not the one you would deploy.
StretchS2 · Stability of SGD

Train two models on datasets differing in one point. Measure how far their predictions diverge as a function of the number of steps.

Answer. Divergence grows with training time, which is the stability bound degrading, and it is why early stopping is a generalisation guarantee and not only a heuristic. Part VIII §13 and §14, with the theory attached.
StretchS3 · Sharpness, and its reparameterisation problem

Measure a sharpness statistic at a minimum. Then rescale one layer up and the next down and measure again.

Answer. The function is unchanged and the sharpness is not. Any claim built on a non-invariant measure needs to explain why the rescaling is disallowed.
StretchS4 · Grokking

Train a small network on modular arithmetic with weight decay, far past the point of zero training error.

Answer. Test accuracy can jump long after training accuracy saturates. Whatever this is, it is not explained by anything in movements B or C, since the training error has not moved.
StretchS5 · An online learner in production

Take a deployed model and run a no-regret algorithm over a set of candidate policies on live traffic, tracking regret.

Answer. The guarantee holds without any i.i.d. assumption, which is the case for doing it this way rather than retraining on a snapshot. Log the action probabilities: Part XXII §38.
StretchS6 · Find your worst group

On a system you run, partition the data every way you can and find the partition with the largest accuracy spread.

Answer. Something will be well below the headline number. Note that this search is itself Part XIV §20's biased maximum, so confirm the gap on held-out data before believing it.
StretchS7 · Bound something you actually shipped

Take a deployed model and try to state any non-vacuous generalisation guarantee for it.

Answer. The honest outcome is usually that you cannot, and that the real evidence is a held-out set plus Part XXI §27's arithmetic. Knowing that the guarantee is empirical rather than theoretical is the point of this part.

Movement G Width

EstimateE2 · How wide is infinite

Figure 8 measures the NTK moving 4.8% at width 2,048, falling like $m^{-0.65}$. Before computing: how wide would a network have to be for the kernel to move less than 0.1%?

Answer. $(0.048/0.001)^{1/0.65}\approx 48^{1.54}\approx 400$, so about 800,000 units: two orders of magnitude beyond any hidden layer in use. The infinite-width limit is not approached by anything anyone trains, which is the practical content of §48. Within a factor of two is a pass.
Warm-upW13 · Which limit

What is taken to infinity in movements A to F, and what in movement G?

Answer. The sample size, and the width. The first gives bounds that hold with high probability and go vacuous for modern models; the second gives an identity in a limit, which is a different kind of object and fails differently: not by being loose but by describing a network that cannot learn features.
Warm-upW14 · Why the NTK makes things solvable

What does the constant-kernel assumption buy?

Answer. Gradient descent on the network becomes gradient descent on a linear model in fixed features, which converges to kernel regression: a closed form. Every question about generalisation becomes a question about the spectrum of a fixed matrix.
CoreC21 · Compute an empirical NTK

Build the Jacobian of a small network's output with respect to every parameter, form $JJ^{\top}$, and verify it is symmetric positive semi-definite. Then confirm it is the kernel of the linearised model by checking that a small parameter step changes the outputs by $J\,\Delta\theta$ to first order.

Answer. Symmetry and PSD are automatic from the Gram structure. The first-order check is the one that matters: the residual should fall quadratically as the step shrinks, and if it falls linearly your Jacobian is wrong. Doing this once is what makes §47 concrete rather than notational.
CoreC22 · Reproduce Figure 8, and match the progress

Measure the relative NTK change during training across widths. Train each width until it halves its own initial loss rather than for a fixed number of steps, and explain why.

Answer. A fixed step budget lets each width get a different distance, so the kernel change is partly measuring progress rather than width, and narrow networks fit faster here, because they can move features, which biases the comparison in the direction of the conclusion. Halving each network's own loss removes that. The exponent lands near $-0.65$, not the $-1/2$ the scaling argument suggests.
CoreC23 · Interpolate between lazy and rich

Scale a network's output by $\alpha$ and sweep it, scaling the learning rate by $1/\alpha^2$. Measure feature movement, test error, and the alignment of the first-layer update with the direction the target depends on, all at equal training loss.

Answer. Feature movement and alignment both fall; test error rises. The learning-rate correction is not optional: the loss curvature scales like $\alpha^2$, so without it you are comparing different effective step sizes and measuring that instead. And the arms must reach the same training loss, or the lazy ones are simply unconverged.
CoreC24 · Run a coordinate check

Implement standard, NTK and μP parameterisation. Take one optimiser step at four widths and plot the change in the network output against width on log axes.

Answer. Standard grows roughly linearly in width; the other two are flat. Measure the change in the output, not in an activation: $|\Delta h|$ falls like $1/\sqrt{m}$ under every scheme including the unstable one, so the activation panel shows three parallel declining lines and no instability anywhere.
CoreC25 · Get μP right

Implement μP and check it against the definition: output layer initialised at $1/m$, output learning rate $\propto 1/m$, input learning rate $\propto m$. Then delete the third of those and re-measure.

Answer. With all three, both the output change and the relative feature movement are flat in width. Delete the input-layer scaling and the features move like $1/m$, making μP lazier than NTK parameterisation, the exact opposite of what it is for. It is one solution to one system of constraints, not a list of tricks with a useful subset.
StretchS8 · Measure the transfer, and bound the drift

Sweep the learning rate at five widths under both parameterisations and locate each optimum. Report the spread, and state what your grid resolution allows you to claim.

Answer. Standard shifts by roughly the width ratio; μP does not move. The second half is the discipline: if your grid steps by a factor of 1.33, "no drift" means "drift below 1.33×", not zero. Check also that no optimum sits on an edge of the swept range: an optimum at the boundary is not a measurement of anything.
StretchS9 · Find the critical initialisation

Push two correlated inputs through a deep random tanh network and track the correlation by layer. Locate the variance where the approach to the fixed point is slowest, and separately where the gradient scale is nearest one. Then check they agree.

Answer. They agree, near $\sigma_w^2\approx1.76$ at $\sigma_b^2=0.05$. Estimate the fixed point from a much deeper stack than you plot: measuring the approach against the correlation at your plotted depth puts the slowest point in the chaotic phase instead of at criticality, because the critical curve has not converged yet.
StretchS10 · Does the phase diagram predict trainability

Train plain deep networks (no normalisation, no residuals) at ordered, critical and chaotic initialisation, sweeping the learning rate at each depth. Plot the best loss reached against depth.

Answer. The ordered phase degrades to no better than predicting the mean; the critical one trains at every depth; the chaotic one also trains fine, despite an enormous gradient scale. The phase diagram predicts one side. Sweeping the rate per depth is what makes this a measurement of trainability rather than of whether one arbitrary rate happens to be stable: at a fixed rate everything deep diverges and the answer looks symmetric.
StretchS11 · Residual scaling from the same calculation

Take a residual network with branches scaled by $\beta$ and work out how the activation variance grows with depth. Find the $\beta$ that keeps it bounded, then verify numerically.

Answer. Each branch adds variance, so $L$ blocks at $\beta=1$ give variance growing like $L$; $\beta=1/\sqrt{L}$ holds it fixed. This is §53's calculation on a different architecture, and it is why residual connections make criticality automatic rather than something to be tuned onto, which is the practical reason very deep plain networks were untrainable and very deep residual ones were not.
