Part FDecompositions

A decomposition breaks a matrix into simpler factors that reveal what it actually does. This is the mathematically deepest part of the chapter and the part where slowing down is correct. Sections 17 and 19 in particular deserve more than one reading; that is normal, not a warning sign.

§17 Eigendecomposition

In 60 seconds

Most vectors get turned by a matrix. A few only get stretched, staying on their own line; those are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factors are the eigenvalues. They are the matrix's natural axes: viewed in that coordinate system, a complicated matrix becomes a simple diagonal one. A zero eigenvalue means a direction was annihilated, which is exactly what makes a matrix non-invertible.

History: a word that is half German by accident

"Eigenvector" is a hybrid, and the seam is visible. German eigen means own, or proper, or characteristic; an eigenvector is a matrix's own vector, the direction belonging to it.

The concepts arrived in the eighteenth century through the study of rotating bodies and vibrating systems: Euler and Lagrange found that a spinning object has special axes about which it rotates without wobbling, which is precisely an eigenvector problem. Cauchy connected the idea to quadratic forms in the 1820s.

David Hilbert, working on integral equations around 1904, used Eigenwert and Eigenfunktion, and his terminology dominated. English mathematicians tried "proper value", "characteristic value" and "latent root": the last from Sylvester, who meant that the values lie latent in the matrix awaiting extraction. All three lost.

What survived is a compound in which the German half was never translated. There is no eigen in English; there is only this word.

Start with a question rather than a definition, because the definition is much easier to absorb once you know what it is answering.

A matrix $A$ takes vectors and moves them, generally changing both direction and length. But maybe some special vectors only get stretched, staying on the line they started on. Do such directions exist? If so, they would be the natural axes of $A$: the directions along which its behaviour is simply "multiply by a number."

Definition: eigenvector and eigenvalue

A non-zero vector $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ if $$A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}$$ for some scalar $\lambda$, called the corresponding eigenvalue.

Read the equation aloud, because it is more approachable than it looks: applying the whole matrix $A$ to this particular vector does the same thing as multiplying it by a single number. All the matrix's complexity collapses, along this one direction, into one scalar.

The German prefix eigen means "own" or "characteristic"; these are the matrix's own directions.

Intuition: the natural axes

Picture a rubber sheet being stretched. Most drawn-on arrows change direction as the sheet deforms. But there are special directions along which the stretch is purely outward, with no twisting. Those are the eigenvectors, and how much the stretch multiplies them is the eigenvalue.

The reason this matters: in the coordinate system built from eigenvectors, a complicated matrix becomes a diagonal one. All the mixing between coordinates disappears, and what remains is independent scaling per axis. Since §16 established that diagonal matrices are trivially easy, finding eigenvectors amounts to finding the point of view from which a hard problem becomes an easy one.

What eigenvectors are actually for

The definition is easy to state and easy to forget, because on first meeting it can seem like a curiosity: a few directions that happen to behave unusually. Here is the problem they solve, and once you have seen it the whole section acquires a point.

Ask what happens when a matrix is applied over and over. This is not a contrived question; it is the normal situation. A system that evolves in steps applies the same transformation each step. A recurrent network applies the same weight matrix at every position in a sequence. A deep network with tied weights does the same. PageRank, the algorithm that made Google, is one matrix applied repeatedly until the answer stops moving.

Computing $A^{10}\mathbf{x}$ directly means ten matrix multiplications, and worse, looking at $A^{10}$ tells you nothing you could have predicted from looking at $A$. The entries scramble. There is no pattern to read.

Now do it with an eigenvector. If $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$, then applying $A$ again just multiplies by $\lambda$ again:

$$A^2\mathbf{v} = A(\lambda\mathbf{v}) = \lambda A\mathbf{v} = \lambda^2\mathbf{v}, \qquad\text{and in general}\qquad A^{k}\mathbf{v} = \lambda^{k}\mathbf{v}$$

Ten applications of a complicated matrix have become one number raised to the tenth power. And since every vector can be written as a combination of eigenvectors (when there is a full set), this is not a special case; it is the general one. Decompose your input into eigen-directions, raise each eigenvalue to the power you need, and reassemble. The hard problem became arithmetic.

Intuition: the eigenvalues decide the long run

That formula also tells you the fate of the system without computing anything. Each direction is scaled by $\lambda^k$ at step $k$, so after many steps the direction with the largest $|\lambda|$ dominates everything else: its component grows fastest, or shrinks slowest, and the rest become negligible beside it.

Three regimes, and each is something you will meet by name:

  • Largest $|\lambda| > 1$: the system blows up, and it blows up along that eigenvector. This is exploding gradients in a recurrent network.
  • All $|\lambda| < 1$: everything decays to zero. This is vanishing gradients, and Part VII's chain of shrinking factors is this statement.
  • Largest $\lambda = 1$: the system settles onto that eigenvector and stays. That steady state is what PageRank computes, and what a Markov chain converges to.

So eigenvalues are not a technical device for factoring matrices. They are the answer to "what does this thing do if you keep doing it", which is a question the rest of this book asks constantly.

Reading the eigenvalues

The sign and magnitude of $\lambda$ each say something concrete:

EigenvalueEffect along that direction
$\lambda > 1$stretched outward
$\lambda = 1$unchanged (fixed direction)
$0 < \lambda < 1$compressed toward the origin
$\lambda = 0$collapsed to zero. This direction is annihilated; the matrix is singular
$\lambda < 0$flipped to the opposite side, and scaled by $|\lambda|$

The $\lambda = 0$ row connects back to §11 and deserves emphasis. A zero eigenvalue means some direction is mapped entirely to the origin: information destroyed, no way back. A matrix is invertible if and only if none of its eigenvalues is zero. That is the cleanest characterisation of invertibility we have, and it is a much sharper tool than the definition in §10.

The decomposition itself

Suppose $A$ is $n \times n$ and has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors. Stack them as the columns of a matrix $V$, and put the eigenvalues along a diagonal matrix $\Lambda$. Then:

$$A = V \Lambda V^{-1}$$

Rather than accepting this, read it as a three-step story under the function view, remembering from §5 that the rightmost factor acts first:

  1. $V^{-1}$: translate into eigen-coordinates. Re-express the incoming vector in terms of the eigenvector axes rather than the standard ones.
  2. $\Lambda$: scale each axis independently. In these coordinates the matrix is diagonal, so this is just multiplication by a number per axis. All the work happens here, and all of it is easy.
  3. $V$: translate back into the original coordinate system.

Every eigendecomposition says the same thing: this complicated transformation is a simple scaling, viewed from the correct angle. The two outer factors are just the change of perspective and its undo.

The symmetric case, which is the one we need

For general matrices, eigendecomposition is messy: eigenvalues can be complex, eigenvectors need not be perpendicular, and some matrices have no full set at all. For symmetric matrices, everything is clean:

The spectral theorem (symmetric case)

Every real symmetric matrix can be decomposed as $$A = Q \Lambda Q^\top$$ where $\Lambda$ is diagonal with real eigenvalues, and $Q$ is orthogonal. Its columns are mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors.

Compare with the general form and notice the single change: $V^{-1}$ became $Q^\top$. That is §16's payoff arriving exactly when needed. Because $Q$ is orthogonal, its inverse is free, the decomposition is numerically stable, and the eigenvectors form a proper perpendicular coordinate system.

This is not a niche special case. The matrices that matter most in this book (covariance matrices in §23 and Part V, Hessians in Part III, Gram matrices) are all symmetric. The spectral theorem is the reason those objects are tractable.

Pitfall: eigenvectors are not unique

If $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector then so is $2\mathbf{v}$, and so is $-\mathbf{v}$: scaling both sides of $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$ leaves the equation true. Software resolves the ambiguity by returning unit-length eigenvectors, but the sign stays arbitrary. So if your PCA plot comes out mirrored compared to a colleague's, nothing is wrong; you have the same eigenvector with the opposite sign. Do not go hunting for a bug that is not there.

Relatedly, when two eigenvalues are equal, any combination of their eigenvectors is also an eigenvector, so the individual directions are genuinely not determined.

How you find them

The definition says what an eigenvector is but not how to locate one, and the gap between those is worth closing, because the standard derivation moves fast enough to lose people.

The trouble is that $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$ has two unknowns tangled together: we know neither the direction nor the stretch factor. The move is to get everything onto one side. Rewrite $\lambda\mathbf{v}$ as $\lambda I \mathbf{v}$, which is legal because the identity does nothing, and subtract:

$$(A - \lambda I)\,\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$$

Now read what that says. We need a matrix (namely $A - \lambda I$) that sends some non-zero vector to zero. A matrix that does that is exactly one that destroys information, which by §11 means rank-deficient, which by §10 means not invertible. An invertible matrix cannot send anything but zero to zero, because it could then be undone and you would recover two different inputs from one output.

So the search collapses into a single condition: find the values of $\lambda$ that make $A - \lambda I$ singular. The direction $\mathbf{v}$ has dropped out entirely for the moment; we are hunting one number at a time. And §22 gives a test for singularity that is a plain equation: the determinant is zero. Solving it gives the eigenvalues, and only then, with $\lambda$ in hand, do you go back and solve for the direction.

Two steps, in that order, and the order is the point. Find the stretch factors first by asking which ones make the matrix collapse; find the directions afterwards, when there is only one unknown left.

Pitfall: this is a hand method, not a real algorithm

Everything above is how you compute a $2\times2$ or $3\times3$ example on paper, and it is genuinely useful for that. It is not how software does it. Finding roots of a polynomial is numerically unstable for anything but tiny matrices, and Abel proved in 1824 that no formula in radicals exists for degree five and above; so for a $5\times5$ matrix there is not even an exact expression to evaluate.

Real implementations use iterative methods that never form the polynomial at all, most descending from the QR algorithm of the 1960s. Do the determinant route to understand what an eigenvalue is; call np.linalg.eigh when you want one.

Worked example: eigendecomposition by hand

Take $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, the matrix from Figure 6. It is symmetric, so the spectral theorem applies.

Step 1: find the eigenvalues. We need $\lambda$ with $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$ for some non-zero $\mathbf{v}$; rearranged, that is $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. A non-zero vector maps to zero exactly when $A - \lambda I$ is singular, which (jumping ahead to §22) means its determinant vanishes:

$\det\!\begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = 0$

So $(2 - \lambda)^2 = 1$, giving $2 - \lambda = \pm 1$ and therefore $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 1$. Both real, as promised for a symmetric matrix.

Step 2: find the eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 3$. Solve $(A - 3I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$:

$\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$  gives  $-v_1 + v_2 = 0$, so $v_1 = v_2$.

Any vector along $(1,1)$ works. Normalised: $\mathbf{q}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$.

Step 3: and for $\lambda_2 = 1$. Solve $(A - I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$:

$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ gives $v_1 + v_2 = 0$, so $v_2 = -v_1$, along $(1,-1)$. Normalised: $\mathbf{q}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$.

Step 4: check the theorem's promises. Are they perpendicular? $\mathbf{q}_1^\top\mathbf{q}_2 = \frac12(1 - 1) = 0$. Yes. Unit length? Both are. So $Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ is orthogonal and $\Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, with $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$.

Step 5: sanity check the picture. $A$ stretches by 3 along the $(1,1)$ diagonal and leaves the $(1,-1)$ diagonal alone. Look back at Figure 6: the orange arrows lie along $(1,1)$ and tripled; the purple ones lie along $(1,-1)$ and are unchanged. The algebra and the picture agree.

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2., 1.], [1., 2.]])
w, V = np.linalg.eigh(A)          # eigh: for SYMMETRIC matrices. use eig otherwise.

print(w)                          ### [1. 3.]   ascending order
print(V)                          ### [[-0.707  0.707]
                                  ###  [ 0.707  0.707]]

# the defining property, verified on the second eigenpair
print(A @ V[:, 1])                ### [2.121 2.121]
print(w[1] * V[:, 1])             ### [2.121 2.121]  -- identical

# and the full reconstruction
print(V @ np.diag(w) @ V.T)       ### [[2. 1.]  -- we get A back
                                  ###  [1. 2.]]
Pitfall: use eigh for symmetric matrices

NumPy offers eig for general matrices and eigh for symmetric ones. For a symmetric input, eigh is faster, more accurate, guarantees real output, and returns properly orthogonal eigenvectors. Using eig on a symmetric matrix can hand you complex numbers with tiny imaginary parts from rounding error, which then propagate confusingly through the rest of your code. When you know the matrix is symmetric (and for covariance matrices you always do), say so.

§18 Quadratic forms and positive definiteness

This is the bridge to optimisation: the Hessian is symmetric, and its definiteness is the second derivative test from 0 §19.

One more construction before the SVD, and it is the bridge from this chapter to optimization. Given a symmetric matrix $A$, consider the scalar-valued function

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^\top A \mathbf{x}$$

This is a quadratic form. Check the shapes: $(1 \times n)(n \times n)(n \times 1) \to 1 \times 1$, a single number. It is the natural multi-dimensional generalisation of $ax^2$, and its graph is a bowl, a dome, or a saddle depending on $A$.

The reason to care is that this expression is what a smooth function looks like near a critical point, in any number of dimensions. Part 0 §19 classified a one-variable minimum by the sign of $f''$: positive means bowl, negative means dome. In many variables the second derivative is not a number but a matrix, and "the sign of the second derivative" has to be replaced by something that makes sense for a matrix. The quadratic form is that replacement: it is how you ask whether a matrix curves upward, and the answer can differ from direction to direction.

Which is precisely the new phenomenon. In one dimension a critical point is a minimum, a maximum, or nothing much. In two or more it can curve up along one axis and down along another at the same point, and there is no one-dimensional word for that. That is a saddle, and the whole reason this section exists is to give you the machinery to detect one.

Expanding one, to see it is really just a polynomial

With $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2)$:

$\mathbf{x}^\top A \mathbf{x} = 2x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2$

Every term is degree two, hence "quadratic". The diagonal entries of $A$ give the squared terms; the off-diagonal entries give the cross terms, and they appear doubled because $A_{1,2}$ and $A_{2,1}$ both contribute.

Positive definiteness

The key question about a quadratic form is whether it is always positive.

Definition: the definiteness ladder

A symmetric matrix $A$ is:

Positive definite
$\mathbf{x}^\top A \mathbf{x} > 0$ for every non-zero $\mathbf{x}$. Equivalently: all eigenvalues are strictly positive.
Positive semidefinite
$\mathbf{x}^\top A \mathbf{x} \geq 0$ always. All eigenvalues $\geq 0$; some may be zero.
Negative definite
$\mathbf{x}^\top A \mathbf{x} < 0$ always. All eigenvalues strictly negative.
Indefinite
Positive for some $\mathbf{x}$, negative for others. Eigenvalues of mixed sign.

The eigenvalue characterisation is the useful one, and it follows directly from §17. Rotate into eigen-coordinates using $\mathbf{y} = Q^\top\mathbf{x}$, and the form becomes

$$\mathbf{x}^\top A \mathbf{x} = \sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2$$

a plain weighted sum of squares. Since each $y_i^2 \geq 0$, the sign of the whole expression is controlled entirely by the signs of the $\lambda_i$. Every statement in the definition above becomes obvious in this coordinate system, which is a good example of what eigendecomposition is for.

Why deep learning cares: this is the bridge to Part III

The second derivative of a multivariable function is a symmetric matrix called the Hessian, and its definiteness classifies critical points exactly as the sign of $f''$ does in single-variable calculus:

  • Hessian positive definite → local minimum (a bowl).
  • Hessian negative definite → local maximum (a dome).
  • Hessian indefinite → saddle point.

This is the entire content of the second derivative test, and it is why §18 exists in a deep learning book at all. There is a striking consequence, developed in Part III §18: in high dimensions, a critical point needs every eigenvalue to share a sign to be a true minimum, and that becomes vanishingly unlikely as dimension grows. So the critical points a large network encounters are overwhelmingly saddles rather than the local minima that folklore worries about. That shift in understanding came directly from taking eigenvalues seriously.

Try it

Is $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$ positive definite? What shape is its quadratic form's graph? Find one $\mathbf{x}$ making the form negative.

Answer. The matrix is diagonal, so the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries: $3$ and $-2$. Mixed signs, so it is indefinite (not positive definite) and the graph is a saddle. The form is $3x_1^2 - 2x_2^2$, so any $\mathbf{x}$ with $x_1 = 0$ and $x_2 \neq 0$ makes it negative; $\mathbf{x} = (0, 1)$ gives $-2$. Meanwhile $\mathbf{x} = (1,0)$ gives $+3$. Positive in one direction, negative in another: that is a saddle, seen algebraically.
