§40 Why this is the hardest part

Four difficulties, compounding

The output space is open. There is no right answer to compare against, only a space of acceptable ones: Part XVI §40's problem, with the additional feature that acceptability depends on the asker.

The capability surface is enormous. A model that writes code, summarises, translates and reasons has no single axis, and any benchmark samples a tiny corner of it.

The training corpus is unpublished. Part XIII §13's contamination question cannot be answered from outside, for any model whose data is not disclosed.

The thing you care about is deployment behaviour, which is a distribution of real requests you do not have at evaluation time.

§41 Benchmarks, and their decay

The lifecycle, which is now predictable

A benchmark is released and is hard. It becomes a target. Scores rise, partly from genuine improvement and partly from Part XIV §22's three mechanisms: contamination, saturation and community overfitting. Within a few years it is uninformative and a replacement appears.

This is not misconduct and it is not avoidable by trying harder. It is Goodhart's law (Part XIV §33) operating on a shared public target, and the only structural defences are the ones Part XIV named: held-out sets used once, benchmarks refreshed with new items, and private evaluations that never enter the corpus.

The practical consequence for reading results: a score on a benchmark older than the model is weak evidence, and the age of the benchmark relative to the model's training cutoff is a number worth checking.

§42 Model-as-judge, again

What Part XIV established, and what is specific here

Part XIV §24 gave the method and its biases (position, verbosity, self-preference) and the requirement that its agreement with your own labels be measured on your own task.

Two things specific to evaluating language models. The judge is usually a model from the same family as one of the contestants, so self-preference is not a hypothetical. And §31's verbosity bias means the judge shares the annotators' preference for length, so a model tuned to be verbose scores well with a judge trained on people who liked verbose answers, a correlated error rather than an independent one.

The controls are the obvious ones and are cheap: randomise position, control for length, and use a judge from a different family than either contestant.

§43 What cannot currently be measured well

Four gaps, stated as gaps

Reliability. Benchmarks report average accuracy. Deployments care about the worst case and about consistency across paraphrases of the same request, and almost nothing reports either.

Calibration. Part XIV §5's measurement applied to open-ended output is not solved: there is no agreed way to extract a confidence from a free-form answer, and the model's stated confidence is a learned register (§31) rather than a probability.

Long-horizon behaviour. §36's agents are evaluated on short tasks because long ones are expensive and noisy, so the regime that matters most is measured least.

Harm. Part XXI's subject. Benchmarks for it exist, they are narrow, and the space of misuse is adversarial and therefore not sampled by any fixed set.

§44 Capability against reliability

The gap that matters most in practice

The distinction worth carrying out of this part: can it ever versus does it always. Benchmarks measure the first. Almost every real difficulty is the second.

A model that answers correctly 95% of the time is impressive on a benchmark and difficult to build on, because §36's arithmetic applies: chained twenty deep it succeeds a third of the time, and a system that fails unpredictably one time in twenty needs an error path for every use.

This is why so much of movements D and E is about constraining rather than improving: tools that compute rather than guess, verifiers that check, retrieval that grounds, structured output that cannot be malformed. Each converts a probabilistic behaviour into something with a failure mode you can handle.

And it is why the capability-first framing misleads about deployment. The question is rarely whether the model can do the task. It is what happens the one time in twenty that it does not.

§45 What movements A to F establish

The one thing

Almost nothing in this part is specific to language.

The architecture is Part XII's, the objective Part XVI's, the scaling Part XVII's, the alignment machinery Part XVIII's, the serving Part XIX's. What is genuinely particular to language is the tokenizer, and the tokenizer turns out to explain a family of failures that looked like reasoning failures.

That is the useful way to hold this subject. A language model is not a new kind of object. It is the objects of the preceding nine parts, composed, at a scale where the composition produced behaviours nobody designed: in-context learning most clearly, and it emerged from an architecture chosen for throughput and an objective chosen for tractability.

The second thing, because it is what deployment turns on: capability is not reliability, benchmarks measure the first, and the engineering that makes these systems usable is almost entirely about the second.
