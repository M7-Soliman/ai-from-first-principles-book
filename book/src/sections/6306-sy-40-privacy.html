Part DPrivacy

A model trained on data about people carries information about those people, and the question is how much. Two of the answers are precise (a definition with a composition theorem, and an attack with ground truth), and the gap between what is provable and what is deployed is the subject.

§25 What a model leaks

Four things, in increasing severity

Aggregate properties of the training set: that it was mostly one dialect, or one hospital. Usually unobjectionable and sometimes commercially sensitive.

Membership: whether a particular record was in the training set. In a medical setting that alone can be the disclosure.

Attributes: recovering a field of a record from the rest.

The record itself. Part XIII §33's memorisation, which large models do verbatim and which is retrievable by anyone who can prompt them.

§26 Differential privacy

Go and read

Dwork & Roth, The Algorithmic Foundations of Differential Privacy. Free online. Chapters 2 and 3 are the definition and the composition theorems, which is all most readers need, and reading the definition once removes the temptation to treat the privacy budget as a dial.

A definition, not a technique

A randomised algorithm is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differentially private if, for any two datasets differing in one record and any set of outcomes,

$$\Pr[A(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[A(D') \in S] + \delta$$

Read it as: the output distribution barely changes when one person is added or removed, so nothing an adversary concludes could have depended much on that person. It is a property of the algorithm, holds against any adversary with any side information, and does not depend on what anyone happens to know, which is what makes it different in kind from anonymisation.

Two things that make it usable, and one that limits it

Composition. Running two private algorithms is private, with budgets that add. So a training run of many steps has an accountable total, which is the whole basis of private learning, though "add" is the loose form of accounting; §27 states the one DP-SGD actually uses.

Post-processing. Anything computed from a private output is private. You cannot leak by analysing further.

The unit. The guarantee is about one record. If a person contributes a thousand records, their protection is a thousand times weaker, and defining the unit correctly is where most deployed guarantees are weakest.

§27 Making training private

Go and read

Abadi et al. (2016), Deep learning with differential privacy. Clipping, noise, and the accountant that makes a long training run's budget tractable. Read §3 for the mechanism, then note how much of the paper is the accounting rather than the algorithm.

Clip, then add noise

Bound each example's gradient to a fixed norm, so no single record can move the parameters far; then add Gaussian noise calibrated to that bound. Both steps are necessary: clipping without noise bounds influence but does not hide it, and noise without clipping is calibrated to nothing.

What it costs is accuracy, and what buys it back is data: more records means the noise is averaged over more signal at the same budget. So privacy and scale are complements, which is an unusually convenient fact and the reason private training is most practical exactly where the data is most sensitive and most plentiful.

Pitfall: "budgets add" is the loose bound, not the one this run reports

Basic composition (§26) is valid (it really is an upper bound on the budget of a multi-step algorithm), but it is the loosest valid one, because it treats every step as if its worst case aligned with every other step's. It is also not what the clip-and-noise procedure above actually accounts. Each step touches a random subsample of the data (a fraction $q = $ batch size over dataset size), and subsampling itself buys privacy: a step that would cost $\varepsilon_0$ against the whole dataset costs roughly $q\varepsilon_0$ against a $q$-fraction sample, since most records were never touched by it.

Capturing that amplification needs a finer tool than summing worst cases: Abadi et al.'s moments accountant (equivalently, composing in Rényi differential privacy and converting back to $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ at the end) tracks a tighter quantity than any single step's worst case, and over $T$ steps at sampling rate $q$ and noise multiplier $\sigma$ it gives a budget growing like

$$\varepsilon = O\!\left(\frac{q\sqrt{T\ln(1/\delta)}}{\sigma}\right)$$

Which is proportional to $\sqrt{T}$, not $T$. Over the 103–104 steps a real training run takes, basic composition overstates the true budget by one to two orders of magnitude. That gap is not a rounding error: it is why DP-SGD is practical at production scale rather than a method whose accountable cost only a toy run could afford. Basic composition is the number to reach for on paper; the moments accountant is the number a real system reports.

§29 Federated learning

Keeping the data where it is, and what that does not solve

Train on device, send updates rather than records, aggregate centrally. The data never moves, which addresses a governance problem directly.

What it does not do is make anything private. An update is a function of the data and leaks accordingly: gradients can be inverted to recover inputs in some settings. Federation is a data-movement property; privacy requires differential privacy on top, and secure aggregation so the server sees only the sum.

Its real difficulties are statistical: devices have non-identically-distributed data, most are unavailable at any moment, and the ones that participate are not a random sample, which is Part XIII §7's selection bias arriving in the training loop itself.

§30 Membership inference

Go and read

Shokri et al. (2017), Membership inference attacks against machine learning models. The attack, and its connection to overfitting. Worth reading for the experimental design: it is one of the few security papers where the ground truth is available and used.

Why this is the attack to reason about

It has ground truth. The defender knows who was in the training set, so the attack can be scored exactly rather than estimated, which makes it the standard empirical measure of leakage and the thing to run before claiming a model is safe to release.

It also connects two subjects that look separate: overfitting is a privacy problem, and the gap between train and test accuracy is a leakage statistic as well as a generalisation one.

§31 Memorisation in large models

Go and read

Carlini et al. (2021), Extracting training data from large language models. The leak that needs no attack. Read it beside Part XIII §21 on deduplication, which turns out to be the most effective available mitigation.

Pitfall: the leak that needs no attack

Large language models reproduce training data verbatim when prompted with a prefix of it, and the effect grows with model size and with how often a sequence was duplicated. No inference is required: the model simply emits the record.

Which makes deduplication a privacy control as well as a data-quality one (Part XIII §21's finding, reread). And it makes the standard defences awkward: differential privacy at a meaningful budget is expensive at this scale, so what is deployed is usually filtering, dedup and output-side checks, none of which carries a guarantee.

§32 What movement D establishes

Three things

Differential privacy is a property of the algorithm, holds against any adversary, and composes, which is what makes a whole training run accountable rather than one query.

Privacy and scale are complements. More data makes the same guarantee cheaper, so the trade is most favourable where the data is most plentiful.

Federation moves data, not risk. An update leaks; privacy needs a mechanism on top of the architecture.
