Part AEstimators

§1 What an estimator is

In 60 seconds

An estimator is a function of the data: a recipe that turns a sample into a guess about the world. Because the sample is random, the guess is random too. So an estimator is a random variable, it has a distribution, and every question about whether it is any good is a question about that distribution.

The setup is always the same. There is some quantity $\theta$ you want to know: a population mean, a coin's bias, the weights of the best possible model. You cannot observe it. You have a sample, and you compute something from that sample and call it $\hat{\theta}$.

Definition: estimator

An estimator $\hat{\theta}$ is any function of the observed data. The value it takes on one particular sample is an estimate.

Note how permissive that is. Any function qualifies. "Take the sample mean" is an estimator; so is "take the first observation" and so is "always answer 7". Nothing in the definition says an estimator is good. Sections 2 through 5 build the vocabulary for saying which ones are.

Intuition: the hat means "computed from data"

The notation is worth taking seriously. $\theta$ is a fixed, unknown number, a property of the world. $\hat{\theta}$ is a random variable, a property of your data.

They are different kinds of object, and conflating them causes most of the confusion in §10. When you write $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]$ you are averaging over possible datasets you might have collected, not over anything inside the dataset you have. That is a strange thing to average over the first few times you meet it, and it is the central move of the whole subject.

It is worth confronting the objection that move invites, because it is the right objection and nobody raises it out loud. You have one dataset. You will never have the twenty thousand in Figure 1. So in what sense does the estimator have a distribution at all, when the thing it depends on happened exactly once?

The answer is that the distribution describes the procedure, not your particular run of it. Your sample was one draw from a process that could have produced many. The distribution is over that process (over the samples you might have got, given how you collected the one you did), and it exists whether or not anyone ever draws a second.

The everyday version is a manufacturing tolerance. A bolt supplier who says the diameter is 10 mm ± 0.05 mm is not claiming to have measured your bolt. They are describing the machine that made it. You hold one bolt, you will never see the others, and the tolerance still tells you something true and useful about the one in your hand, namely how far it is likely to be from nominal.

A standard error is that tolerance, for an answer instead of a bolt. It does not say how wrong your estimate is; that is unknowable, because knowing it would mean knowing $\theta$, and if you knew $\theta$ you would not be estimating it. It says how wrong estimates from this procedure typically are, and that is the most any honest method can offer.

Everything in this part is an instance of that move. Confidence intervals in §8 have coverage guarantees about the procedure rather than about your interval. P-values in §11 are probabilities about data the null would have produced, not about the null. The bootstrap in §10 is unusual only in that it simulates the imagined repetitions explicitly instead of reasoning about them, which is why it works when no formula is available, and why it is the most intuitive method here despite arriving last.

§2 Bias

Definition: bias

$$\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta$$ An estimator is unbiased if this is zero, that is, if it is correct on average across all possible samples.

Unbiasedness says the estimator does not systematically overshoot or undershoot. It says nothing at all about how far any individual estimate might be from the truth, which is why it is a much weaker guarantee than it sounds.

Worked: why the sample variance has $n-1$ in it

This is the one piece of statistical trivia everybody half-remembers, and it is worth seeing where it comes from.

The natural estimator of variance divides by $n$: $\;\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac1n\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2$. It is biased low, and the reason is that $\bar{x}$ is itself estimated from the same data. The sample mean sits, by construction, at the point minimising the sum of squared deviations, so deviations measured from it are systematically smaller than deviations from the true mean.

You used up one piece of information estimating $\bar{x}$. Working through the expectation gives $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\sigma}^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$, so dividing by $n-1$ instead of $n$ corrects it exactly.

That is what a "degree of freedom" is: a constraint the data had to satisfy, paid for out of the sample size. NumPy's ddof argument is asking how many you spent.

Pitfall: unbiased is not the same as good

"Always answer 7" has zero variance and is wildly biased. "Use only the first observation" is perfectly unbiased for the mean and nearly useless, because it throws away all but one data point.

Unbiasedness is one property among several, and §4 will show it is frequently worth giving up. Almost every regularized model in this book is deliberately biased, because the bias buys a reduction in variance that more than pays for itself.

§3 Variance and standard error

The second property: how much does the estimate move when the sample changes?

Definition: standard error

The variance of an estimator is $\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})$, the spread of its sampling distribution. Its square root is called the standard error, the standard deviation of the estimator, not of the data.

The distinction in that last clause matters and is routinely lost. The standard deviation of your data describes how spread out the observations are. The standard error describes how spread out your answer is. They are different numbers with different units of meaning, and for the sample mean they are related by

$$\text{SE}(\bar{x}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Make that concrete before deriving it, because the two quantities are so easily confused that a numerical example is worth more than the definition. Suppose adult heights have a standard deviation of 10 cm, and you measure 100 people.

  • The standard deviation is still 10 cm. Individual people genuinely differ by that much, and collecting more data does not make them more alike. This number describes the world.
  • The standard error is $10/\sqrt{100} = 1$ cm. If you repeated the whole study, your average would land within about a centimetre of where it landed this time. This number describes your answer.

Ten centimetres and one centimetre, from the same data, both correct, describing different things. More data leaves the first untouched and shrinks the second, which is the whole reason collecting more data is worth anything.

Where the square root comes from

The $\sqrt{n}$ looks arbitrary and is not. It follows from one fact, and that fact is worth more than the formula:

Variances add; standard deviations do not

For independent quantities, $$\text{Var}(X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n) = \text{Var}(X_1) + \cdots + \text{Var}(X_n)$$ Nothing similar holds for standard deviations, because a square root does not distribute over a sum (Part 0 §3 warned about exactly this).

Now the two lines. Adding $n$ observations each of variance $\sigma^2$ gives a total of variance $n\sigma^2$. The mean divides that total by $n$, and dividing a random quantity by a constant divides its variance by the constant squared:

$$\text{Var}(\bar{x}) = \frac{1}{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{SE}(\bar{x}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

The square root is the last step and nothing more: variance falls like $1/n$, and standard error is its square root. Every appearance of $\sqrt{n}$ in this book traces to that line.

Intuition: errors partly cancel, and cancellation is not linear

Why should adding independent things grow their spread more slowly than adding their sizes? Because independent errors point in random directions and partly cancel.

If ten measurements were all wrong in the same direction, their total error would be ten times one error, and averaging would gain you nothing. Independence means some are high and some are low, so the sum's error is much smaller than ten single errors, and the bookkeeping of how much smaller is exactly the rule that variances add.

The physical version is a drunkard's walk. Take $n$ random steps of length one and you do not end up $n$ away; you end up about $\sqrt{n}$ away, because the steps partly undo one another. Same arithmetic, same square root. Averaging data is that walk, run backwards to cancel error rather than accumulate distance.

Notice what the derivation assumed, because it is where the formula fails. It used independence, and nothing else. If your observations are correlated (repeated measurements of the same subject, examples scraped from the same source, sentences from the same document), the variances no longer simply add, the true standard error is larger than $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, and every interval you build from it is too narrow. This is the most common way a confidence interval lies, and §8 assumes it away without comment.

Why this matters: the $\sqrt{n}$ law is everywhere

You have met this rate twice already without it being named. Part II's Monte Carlo estimate converged at $1/\sqrt{n}$. Part III's minibatch gradient had noise falling as $1/\sqrt{b}$. This is the same law: averaging $n$ independent things reduces noise by $\sqrt{n}$.

Its consequences are practical and unforgiving. Halving your error bars requires quadrupling your test set. Distinguishing two models that differ by 0.2% requires far more evaluation data than most people use. And the reason large-batch training buys so little is that its extra precision costs linearly while paying only as a square root.

§4 Mean squared error, and the trade

Bias and variance are two different ways to be wrong. The natural way to combine them is to ask about total error:

$$\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2\big]$$

and this decomposes exactly, a fact worth deriving once, because §20 is the same derivation applied to a model instead of a parameter.

Worked: the decomposition

Write $\mu = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]$, the estimator's average value. Add and subtract it inside the square:

$\text{MSE} = \mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{\theta} - \mu + \mu - \theta)^2\big]$

Expand the square into three terms:

$= \mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{\theta}-\mu)^2\big] + 2\,\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{\theta}-\mu)\big](\mu-\theta) + (\mu-\theta)^2$

The middle term vanishes, because $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta} - \mu] = \mu - \mu = 0$ by the definition of $\mu$. What survives is

$$\boxed{\;\text{MSE} = \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2\;}$$

Total error is variance plus squared bias. No approximation, no assumptions: an identity.

Intuition: this is why biased estimators are allowed

Because the two terms add, you can trade one against the other. If accepting a little bias buys a large reduction in variance, total error falls, and you are better off, even though your estimator is now systematically wrong.

That trade is not a curiosity. It is the entire justification for regularization. Ridge regression is deliberately biased toward zero and beats the unbiased least squares estimate on total error whenever the problem is ill-conditioned, which, by Part III §7, is most of the time. Shrinking toward a prior, early stopping, weight decay, and parameter sharing are all the same manoeuvre.

§5 Consistency and efficiency

Two more properties, briefly, because you will meet the words.

Consistency and efficiency

An estimator is consistent if it converges to the truth as the sample grows without bound. This is a weaker and more important requirement than unbiasedness: it permits being wrong at any finite $n$, but forbids being wrong forever.

Among unbiased estimators, the one with the smallest variance is called efficient. That floor has a name: the Cramér–Rao bound states that the variance of any unbiased estimator is at least $1/\mathcal{I}(\theta)$, where $\mathcal{I}$ is the Fisher information, a measure of how much the data can, in principle, say about $\theta$. An efficient estimator achieves that bound, so "efficient" means "as good as any unbiased estimator can be".

Consistency is close to a minimum standard: an estimator that does not converge to the right answer with infinite data is difficult to defend. The biased sample variance from §2 is consistent (its bias factor $\frac{n-1}{n}$ tends to 1), which is why the distinction between dividing by $n$ and by $n-1$ stops mattering long before your dataset is interesting.

Why this matters: maximum likelihood, revisited

Part II §26 derived maximum likelihood and showed it is the same procedure as minimising cross-entropy. This section says what kind of estimator it produces.

Under fairly general conditions, the maximum likelihood estimator is consistent and asymptotically efficient: with enough data it converges to the truth, and no unbiased estimator does better. That is a strong endorsement, and it is the real reason the principle is the field's default rather than merely a convenient one.

The qualifications matter, though. It is generally biased at finite sample sizes, the guarantees are asymptotic, and they assume the true distribution is actually in the family you are fitting, which for a neural network it emphatically is not. Knowing what the guarantee promises is also knowing where it stops.

Try it

Two estimators of a population mean. Estimator A is the sample mean: unbiased, variance $\sigma^2/n$. Estimator B always returns 0, whatever the data.

If the true mean is $\theta = 0.1$, $\sigma = 1$ and $n = 4$, which has the lower MSE?

Answer. A: bias 0, variance $1/4$, so $\text{MSE} = 0.25$.

B: variance 0, bias $0 - 0.1 = -0.1$, so $\text{MSE} = 0.01$.

The constant beats the sample mean by a factor of 25: with only four data points and a truth close to zero, the useless estimator wins on total error.

This is not a trick. It is exactly why shrinkage works: when data is scarce and you have a decent guess about where the answer lies, pulling toward that guess reduces total error. Push $n$ to 400 and A's MSE falls to $0.0025$ while B's stays at $0.01$, and the ranking reverses. The right amount of bias depends on how much data you have, which is the entire content of choosing a regularization strength.
