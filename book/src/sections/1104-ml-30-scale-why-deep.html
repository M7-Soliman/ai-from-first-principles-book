Part FMaking it work at scale

§19 Stochastic gradient descent, applied

In 60 seconds

Part III §14 introduced minibatch gradients as a numerical technique. Here is what they buy for learning: the cost of a training step becomes independent of dataset size. That single property is what made learning from enormous datasets possible, and it is a larger factor in the last two decades of progress than any individual model architecture.

The empirical risk of §3 is an average over $m$ examples, so its exact gradient costs a full pass for one step. A minibatch estimate costs $b$ examples regardless of $m$.

The consequence is worth stating carefully. With a fixed compute budget you may either take a few exact steps or a great many approximate ones, and the approximate route almost always wins, because the extra precision would have been discarded at the next step anyway. Convergence per unit of computation, not per iteration, is the quantity that matters.

Why this matters: capacity became affordable

Before minibatch methods, training cost scaled with dataset size, so large datasets were prohibitive and models stayed small to compensate. Once step cost decoupled from dataset size, both could grow, and Part V §20 says growing data is exactly what makes large capacity safe, because variance falls with data while bias does not.

Large models plus large datasets is the entire practical formula of modern machine learning, and it is only available because of this section.

In the framework: the recipe, executable

The four choices above are not an abstraction over what practitioners write. They are literally the four objects a training script constructs, and the correspondence is close enough to be worth seeing side by side:

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
from torch.utils.data import TensorDataset, DataLoader

# 1. DATASET -- the experience
train = DataLoader(TensorDataset(X_train, y_train), batch_size=32, shuffle=True)

# 2. MODEL FAMILY -- what functions are available, i.e. what is assumed
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(20, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 2))

# 3. LOSS -- a negative log-likelihood, per Part II section 26
#    (F.cross_entropy, used below)

# 4. OPTIMISER -- a variant of stochastic gradient descent
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)

for epoch in range(n_epochs):
    model.train()
    for xb, yb in train:
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = F.cross_entropy(model(xb), yb)
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()

    model.eval()
    with torch.no_grad():
        acc = (model(X_val).argmax(1) == y_val).float().mean()

That is a complete, working training script, and every line of it has been derived somewhere in this book. DataLoader with shuffle=True is sampling from the empirical distribution of Part II §17. cross_entropy is Part II §26's maximum likelihood. backward() is the chain rule of Part 0 §17. opt.step() is Part III §16. zero_grad() is there because gradients accumulate, which Part VII §21 explains. Nothing in it is a convention you have to accept on authority.

The two lines that are not about learning at all

model.train() and model.eval() look like decoration and are not. They flip a flag that changes what certain layers do. Dropout is active during training and disabled at evaluation; batch normalisation uses the current batch's statistics while training and stored running averages afterwards.

Forgetting model.eval() is one of the most common causes of a validation score that is mysteriously worse than it should be and mysteriously different on every run, because dropout is still randomly deleting units while you measure. Forgetting model.train() again afterwards silently disables your regularization for the rest of the run. Neither raises an error.

torch.no_grad() is a separate concern: evaluation does not need gradients, so telling the library to stop recording the graph saves the memory it would have spent storing activations for a backward pass that will never happen. On a large model this is the difference between fitting your evaluation batch in memory and not.

What the framework does not give you

Notice what is absent from that script and belongs to §§9–11: there is no test set in it, no interval on the reported accuracy, no record of how many configurations were tried, no seeds. A framework will happily let you tune on the test set for a week. The discipline of this part is entirely yours to supply, and the ease of the code above is precisely why it so often is not.

Part GWhy any of this needed deep learning

The classical methods are elegant, well understood, and hit a wall on the problems this field cares about. These three sections are about that wall, measured, not asserted.

§21 The curse of dimensionality

In 60 seconds

High-dimensional space is mostly empty, and the intuitions built in two or three dimensions fail there. The number of examples needed to cover a space grows exponentially with its dimension, and distances between points lose their meaning. Every method that relies on finding nearby examples degrades.

Intuition: counting cells

Divide each axis into 10 bins. In one dimension that is 10 cells; in two, 100; in three, 1,000. To have even one example per cell in 784 dimensions (a small image) you need $10^{784}$ examples, which exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe by hundreds of orders of magnitude.

So any real dataset in high dimensions is unfathomably sparse. Almost every cell is empty, and any method that answers a query by looking for examples nearby will find nothing nearby.

§22 Local constancy

Most classical methods share one assumption, usually unstated: the function is smooth; nearby inputs have nearby outputs. Nearest neighbours relies on it completely. Kernels with local kernels rely on it. Decision trees rely on a piecewise version.

It is a reasonable prior and it is also a fundamental limitation, because a smoothness prior alone can only interpolate between examples it has seen.

Why this matters: it is the argument for depth

To do better than exponential you need a stronger assumption than smoothness, one that lets a model generalise to regions where it has seen no examples.

Deep learning's assumption is compositional structure: that the data is generated by combining a moderate number of underlying factors, and that a function built by composing simple pieces can capture that. A network that has learned edges, then textures, then parts, can recognise an object arrangement it has never encountered, because it has decomposed the problem rather than memorised the space.

The gain is quantifiable: a $k$-layer network with $O(k)$ neurons can carve input space into $O(2^k)$ distinct regions, whereas a shallow method needs $O(2^k)$ examples to cover those same regions by local interpolation, an exponential saving in labelled data, and the precise reason depth exists. Part VII works this out fully.

That is a strictly stronger prior than smoothness, it is false for arbitrary functions (Section 7 guarantees as much), and it appears to be true of images, audio and language. That empirical fact is the entire justification for the rest of this book.

§23 The manifold hypothesis

Section 21 is alarming, and it overstates the difficulty, because real data does not fill its space.

The manifold hypothesis

Real high-dimensional data concentrates near a low-dimensional manifold: a curved surface of far fewer dimensions embedded in the high-dimensional space.

The evidence for it is easy to appreciate. Generate an image by choosing each of 784 pixels uniformly at random and you get static, essentially never anything resembling a digit. Real images occupy a vanishingly small, highly structured region of pixel space. The same holds for audio that sounds like speech and character strings that read as English.

Intuition: why this rescues the situation

If data fills 784 dimensions, learning is hopeless by §21. If it lies on a manifold of perhaps 10 or 50 intrinsic dimensions, the problem is merely hard.

And it reframes what a model should do. Representation learning is the search for coordinates on that manifold, turning 784 confusing numbers into 50 meaningful ones. PCA does this when the manifold is flat. Autoencoders do it when it curves. Every embedding in modern machine learning is an attempt at the same thing.

Why this matters: the three claims that follow

Taken together, Sections 21 to 23 make the case that carries the rest of the book:

  1. High-dimensional space is too large to cover: so methods that interpolate between nearby examples cannot scale.
  2. Real data lies on a low-dimensional manifold: so the problem is smaller than it appears, if you can find the manifold.
  3. Composition is the prior that finds it: build functions from layers of simpler functions, and learn the features rather than choosing them.

That third claim is what "deep" means, and it is the hypothesis the following parts test.

Try it: the last one

A dataset has 1,000 features and 10,000 examples. A colleague proposes k-nearest neighbours. What is your objection, and what would change your mind?

Answer. With 1,000 dimensions and 10,000 points the space is unfathomably sparse, and by Figure 13 the nearest and farthest training points will be at nearly the same distance. "Nearest" carries almost no information, so the votes it collects are close to arbitrary.

What would change my mind: evidence that the intrinsic dimension is far lower than 1,000, which is entirely possible, since §23 says raw dimension routinely overstates it. Check it: run PCA and look at how many components carry most of the variance. If 15 components explain 95%, the data is effectively 15-dimensional, neighbours are meaningful again, and kNN on those 15 coordinates may work well.

That diagnostic (measure the intrinsic dimension before assuming the ambient one) takes five lines and is the practical residue of this entire part.
