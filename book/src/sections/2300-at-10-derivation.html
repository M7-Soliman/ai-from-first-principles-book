Part AFrom the bottleneck to attention

§1 The problem, restated

In 60 seconds

Part XI §27 measured it: an encoder must compress a whole input into one vector before knowing which output it will be asked for first, and widening the vector sixteenfold bought 0.12. The fix is not a bigger summary. It is to stop summarising in advance and let the decoder go and look.

The encoder–decoder of Part XI §27 is a good architecture with one structural defect, and it is worth stating the defect in the sharpest available form because everything in this part follows from removing it.

The decoder receives a context $C$, one vector, computed by the encoder from the entire input. To produce its first output the decoder consults $C$. To produce its tenth output it consults the same $C$. So $C$ must contain, simultaneously, whatever is needed for every output the decoder will ever produce, and it must have been computed before any of them existed.

Why this is not a capacity problem

The natural response is that $C$ is too small. Part XI's Figure 9 tested it: at an input length of 24, widening the context from 4 to 64, sixteen times, moved accuracy from 0.328 to 0.444.

If capacity were the constraint the curve would rise steeply and flatten near the top. It does not. What limits the architecture is that a fixed summary computed in ignorance of the question cannot be right for every question, and there is no size at which that stops being true.

Which suggests the shape of the fix immediately. Do not compute a summary. Keep everything the encoder produced, and let each decoder step decide for itself what to look at.

§2 Attention, derived

Suppose the encoder gives you not one vector but $n$ of them, one per input position: $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_n$. The decoder, at some step, needs a summary, but a summary chosen for this step.

The most general thing it could take is a weighted average:

$$\mathbf{c} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \mathbf{v}_i, \qquad a_i \geq 0, \qquad \sum_i a_i = 1$$

and the whole design problem is now: where do the weights come from?

Intuition: three demands on the weights, and each pins something down

They must depend on the decoder's current state. Otherwise the summary is the same at every step and you are back to $C$. So something the decoder emits, call it a query, has to enter the calculation.

They must depend on the content at each position. Otherwise the model can only learn fixed positional habits. So each position must offer something to be matched against the query, call it a key.

They must be differentiable. Part XI §26 met the alternative: a hard choice of one position has no gradient, and the whole apparatus of this book is built on gradients. So the selection has to be soft: every position gets a weight, most of them small.

Three requirements, and they have already forced the architecture. A query, a key per position, a score comparing them, a normalisation making the scores into weights, and a value per position to be averaged.

Attention

Given a query $\mathbf{q}$, keys $\mathbf{k}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{k}_n$ and values $\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n$:

$$a_i = \frac{\exp(s(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{k}_i))}{\sum_j \exp(s(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{k}_j))}, \qquad \text{attn}(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_i a_i \mathbf{v}_i$$

The softmax of Part II §20 does the normalising, for the reasons it always does: it turns arbitrary real scores into a distribution, and it is differentiable everywhere.

Nothing here is arbitrary except the score function $s$, and §4 pins that down too.

§3 Queries, keys and values: why three

A reasonable objection: why does each position need two vectors, a key and a value? Why not match against the thing you are going to retrieve?

Why the thing you search by is not the thing you want back

Consider what the two are for. The key is matched against the query: it answers "is this position relevant to what is being asked?". The value is what gets returned: it answers "what should the asker receive?".

Those are different questions and there is no reason the same vector should be good at both. A position holding the word bank might want to be found by queries about rivers and about money, and want to deliver quite different content depending on which. Tying key to value forces relevance and content to be the same representation.

The general form of the argument is worth keeping: separating the address from the contents is what makes a lookup a lookup. Part XI §26's memory cells had the same structure and for the same reason: content-based addressing needs something to address by.

In practice all three come from the same underlying vectors, through three learned linear maps:

$$Q = XW^Q, \qquad K = XW^K, \qquad V = XW^V$$

so the model decides for itself what aspect of each position serves as its address and what serves as its contents. Three matrices, and the fourth ($W^O$, mixing the result back) is §14's.

§4 Scaled dot-product, and where the $\sqrt{d}$ comes from

The score $s(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{k})$ could be anything differentiable. Early work used a small network: additive attention, which is a one-hidden-layer MLP on the concatenated pair. It works. It is also $n$ separate small matrix products, where the alternative is one large one.

The dot product $\mathbf{q}^\top\mathbf{k}$ measures alignment (Part I §6), needs no parameters, and turns the whole score computation into a single matrix multiplication $QK^\top$, which by Part X §17's argument is the operation hardware is built for. That is the entire reason it won.

Used raw it fails, and the failure has an exact cause.

Worked: the variance of a dot product, and the only scale that fixes it

Let $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ have independent components with mean 0 and variance 1. Then

$$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{q}^\top\mathbf{k}\big] = 0, \qquad \text{Var}\big[\mathbf{q}^\top\mathbf{k}\big] = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \text{Var}[q_i k_i] = d$$

because the $d$ terms are independent and each has unit variance. Measured over $d = 4$ to $2048$, the ratio $\text{Var}[\mathbf{q}^\top\mathbf{k}]/d$ came out between 0.86 and 1.12.

So the logits have standard deviation $\sqrt{d}$. At $d = 2048$ that is about 45, and a softmax over logits spread across $\pm 45$ is a hard maximum. Dividing by $\sqrt{d}$ returns them to unit variance, and it is the only scaling that does so independently of $d$.

Why a saturated softmax is fatal rather than merely undesirable

It is not that hard attention is a bad answer. It is what it does to the gradient.

Differentiate Part II §20's definition $a_i = e^{s_i}/\sum_j e^{s_j}$ and the Jacobian falls out directly: quotient rule, using $\partial a_j/\partial s_j = a_j$ for the denominator's own term: $$\frac{\partial a_i}{\partial s_j} = a_i(\delta_{ij} - a_j)$$ When one weight is near 1 and the rest near 0, every one of those terms is near zero. The layer stops learning, and it stops learning at initialisation, before it has any idea which position it ought to be attending to.

So the model is frozen into whatever arbitrary choice its random initialisation made. This is Part VII §7's saturation argument, in a place nobody expects it: not in an activation function but in an attention weight.

Scaled dot-product attention

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V$$

with $Q$ of shape $(n_q, d_k)$, $K$ of shape $(n_k, d_k)$, $V$ of shape $(n_k, d_v)$, and the output $(n_q, d_v)$. The softmax is over the last axis: over keys, so each query's weights sum to one.

In the framework: the whole formula, one call

The scale, the softmax and the weighted sum just derived are one function call, worth meeting now rather than after it has been used silently for the rest of the part. The snippet also passes a mask, which is not yet derived: §21 builds it properly, and it is shown here only because the fused kernel takes it as one argument to the same call rather than as a separate step.

import torch.nn.functional as F

# q, k, v: each (batch, heads, n, d_k)
out = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=True)
# agrees with softmax(q @ k.transpose(-2, -1) / d_k**0.5, masked) @ v to 5e-7

What each piece corresponds to. The $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ scale is applied inside the call, not supplied by the caller; it is this section's derivation, not a hyperparameter. is_causal=True is §21's mask, built in rather than constructed by hand. There is no separate projection step: $Q$, $K$ and $V$ are §3's $W^Q$, $W^K$, $W^V$ applied before this call, not inside it.

Why it is designed that way. One function rather than four composed operations, because the composition is exactly what §29 rewrites. Given $Q$, $K$ and $V$ alone, the library is free to route to whichever tiled, IO-aware kernel is fastest on the hardware it finds itself on; the point of calling the primitive, rather than writing softmax and matmul by hand, is that the substitution happens without the caller's code changing.

What breaks. Passing an explicit attn_mask together with is_causal=True raises: they are two ways of saying the same thing, and the function will not guess which one you meant. And $Q$, $K$ and $V$ must share a dtype; mixing precisions, an easy mistake when only one tensor came from a cached buffer, raises rather than silently upcasting.

§5 Two ways to read it

The formula is short and admits two readings, both useful and neither complete.

Reading one: a soft dictionary

A Python dictionary maps a key to a value by exact match: d[k] returns the value whose key is exactly k, and fails otherwise.

Attention is that operation with the exact match replaced by a similarity, and the single result replaced by a weighted blend of all of them. Query a dictionary of word meanings with something halfway between river and money and you get a mixture. The softmax temperature, set by $\sqrt{d_k}$, controls how nearly exact the match has to be.

This reading is why the mechanism is sometimes called content-based addressing, and why Part XI §26's neural Turing machine turned out to be doing the same thing.

Reading two: kernel smoothing

Part VI §16's nearest-neighbour methods answered a query by looking at nearby training points. Kernel regression generalised that: weight every point by a kernel of its distance to the query, and average.

Attention is kernel regression with an exponential kernel on the inner product, where the "training points" are the other positions in this input and the metric is learned rather than chosen.

This reading is the more useful one for intuition about failure. Kernel methods degrade when the kernel is badly scaled: too wide and everything is averaged into mush, too narrow and only one point contributes. §4's entropy measurement is exactly that failure, and §25's entropy collapse is it happening during training rather than at initialisation.

§6 Cross-attention, and what it fixed

Go and read

Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (2015), Neural machine translation by jointly learning to align and translate. Attention, invented, and worth reading for how narrowly it is framed. It is presented as a repair to a bottleneck in a translation model, and nothing in it suggests that the component is about to replace everything around it. A good corrective to the sense that important ideas arrive announcing themselves.

Put the two together. The decoder produces queries from its own state; the encoder produces keys and values from the input. Every decoder step computes its own weighted average over every encoder position, choosing the weights afresh.

That is cross-attention, and it is the 2014 mechanism in its original form. Against Part XI §28's four limitations:

What cross-attention removed, and what it did not
Part XI §28's limitationStatus
A fixed-size context cannot serve every question Removed. There is no fixed context. Each step builds its own.
The path between distant positions is long Removed between encoder and decoder: one step. Still long within each, which §7 fixes.
Training cannot be parallelised over the sequence Not removed. Both halves are still recurrent.
Stationarity is assumed and never checked Not removed. Still one recurrent transition function.

Two of four, and the two that remained were the ones that mattered most for scale. For three years attention was understood as a component you bolted onto a recurrent model to fix its bottleneck.

Why the next step took three years

The move from here to §7 is one sentence long: apply the same operation with the sequence attending to itself, and then notice that if you do it everywhere you no longer need the recurrence at all.

It is worth sitting with the fact that this took the field three years, because it says something about how ideas actually move. Attention was invented as a repair. Seeing it instead as a replacement required giving up the assumption that a sequence model must process a sequence in order, and that assumption was not written down anywhere, which is precisely what made it hard to discard.

The paper that did it is called Attention is all you need. The title is a joke about exactly this: the component everyone treated as an accessory turned out to be the whole thing.
