Part CSparsity and pruning

Remove weights entirely. The idea is old, the accuracy results are genuinely good, and the speedups mostly do not exist, which is a lesson about the difference between arithmetic and time.

§17 Pruning

The procedure, and the surprising part

Train a network, remove the weights that matter least, fine-tune what remains, repeat. The standard criterion is magnitude (remove the smallest), which is crude and hard to beat.

The surprising part is how much can go. Removing 80–90% of the weights of a well-trained network, with fine-tuning between rounds, typically costs very little accuracy. That is a real statement about how overparameterised these models are, and it is worth taking seriously independently of whether it makes anything faster.

§18 The lottery ticket

Go and read

Frankle & Carbin (2019), The lottery ticket hypothesis. Read the reset step carefully, because it is the whole claim: the survivors go back to their original values, and a fresh random initialisation does not work. Then read the follow-up on rewinding to an early step rather than to initialisation, which is what made the result hold at scale.

The claim, and its careful form

Within a randomly initialised network there exist sparse subnetworks ("winning tickets") that, trained from the original initialisation, match the full network's accuracy in comparable time.

The procedure that finds one: train, prune, reset the survivors to their original values, retrain. The reset is the whole content of the claim: resetting to a new random initialisation does not work, so the specific initial values matter.

Two qualifications that the result is often quoted without. The tickets are found by training the full network first, so this is not a way to train cheaply. And at larger scales the original procedure needed modification: rewinding to an early training step rather than to initialisation.

§19 Structured against unstructured

Two kinds

Unstructured: any weight may be removed. Best accuracy at a given sparsity, because the choice is unconstrained.

Structured: remove whole rows, columns, channels, heads or layers. Worse accuracy at the same nominal sparsity, and it produces a genuinely smaller dense model, one that is faster on any hardware with no special support.

Why the FLOP count is the wrong measure

The figure's flat line is the point. A zero stored in a dense array is a number like any other: it is loaded from memory, multiplied and added. Nothing about it is cheaper.

To exploit it you must change the format, and a sparse format pays for the arithmetic it skips with index storage and irregular memory access. The measured crossover sits somewhere in the high-sparsity regime (on the hardware behind Figure 5, between one half and three-quarters zeroed), and exactly where is a property of the library and the machine, not of the model. It is worse on hardware built for dense throughput because irregular access defeats exactly what that hardware is optimised for.

Then §3 delivers the harder blow: even where a sparse kernel wins on arithmetic, decode is memory-bound, and a sparse format may move more bytes than a dense one, the values plus their indices. Removing FLOPs from an operation that was not FLOP-limited buys nothing.

§20 The pattern that works

2:4 semi-structured sparsity

Require exactly two of every four consecutive weights to be zero. Regular enough that hardware can be built for it (the two survivors of each group carry a two-bit position index apiece, four bits of metadata per group of four, and the access pattern is predictable) and unstructured enough to choose which two.

It delivers close to the theoretical 2× on hardware with support for it, at 50% sparsity, with a modest accuracy cost after fine-tuning. That is far less sparsity than the 90% of §17, and it is the sparsity that actually runs faster.

The general lesson is worth extracting because it recurs: a constraint that makes the pattern predictable is worth more than the freedom it costs. The same trade appears in structured pruning, in grouped-query attention (§4), and in block-sparse attention.

§21 What to prune

Larger units, and what each removes

Attention heads. A substantial fraction can be removed with little loss, evidence about redundancy as much as a technique.

Layers. Whole blocks, particularly in the middle of deep stacks, can often go. Crude and effective, and it produces a smaller dense model.

Width. Remove hidden units from the feed-forward blocks, which is where the parameters are (Part XII §15's two thirds).

All three are structured by construction, which is why they are what deployed systems actually use.

§22 Against quantization

Why quantization won

Both reduce the model. Quantization reduces bytes per weight, which is the binding constraint (§3), on hardware that already supports the format, with no change to the shape of any operation. Unstructured pruning reduces weight count, which is not binding, and needs a format change to realise anything.

So at equal compression, 4-bit quantization is straightforwardly faster and 90% unstructured sparsity is straightforwardly not. That is the whole reason one is ubiquitous and the other is mostly a research topic, and it is a fact about memory systems rather than about either method.

They compose: a 4-bit 2:4-sparse model is smaller than either alone. The accuracy costs add up faster than the compression does, which is worth measuring rather than assuming.

§23 What survives

Three things

Networks are enormously overparameterised, and pruning is the cleanest demonstration. That fact is worth carrying even if you never prune anything.

Structure beats sparsity. The pattern that runs fast is 50% and regular, not 90% and free.

Measure time, not FLOPs. The figure's flat line took ten lines of code to produce and contradicts a great deal of published arithmetic.
