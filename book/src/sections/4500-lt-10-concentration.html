Part AConcentration

All of learning theory rests on one fact: an average of independent quantities is close to its expectation, and the probability of it not being close falls exponentially. This movement states that fact three ways, measures how much each one gives up, and shows why the whole subject then has to do something more elaborate.

§1 The question, stated so it can be answered

Risk, empirical risk, and the gap between them

A hypothesis $h$ has true risk $R(h) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}}[\ell(h(x), y)]$ and empirical risk $\hat{R}_S(h) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \ell(h(x_i), y_i)$ on a sample $S$ of size $n$.

You can compute the second and you care about the first. Learning theory is the study of $R(h) - \hat{R}_S(h)$, and Part VI §4 named this the generalisation gap without saying anything that would let you bound it.

Why this is not simply the law of large numbers

For a fixed hypothesis chosen before seeing the data, the gap is an average of independent terms minus its mean, and §2's bounds settle it immediately.

The difficulty is that you do not use a fixed hypothesis. You choose $h$ because it did well on $S$, which makes $\hat{R}_S(h)$ a biased estimate of $R(h)$, the same structure as Part XIV §20's biased maximum, now the central obstacle of a whole subject rather than a caution about model selection.

Everything in movements B and C is machinery for handling that one problem: how much can the selection have inflated the empirical performance, given how much freedom the selection had.

§2 Three bounds, and what each one uses

Hoeffding, Bernstein, McDiarmid

Hoeffding. For independent $X_i \in [a,b]$ with mean $\mu$,

$$P\left(\left|\bar{X} - \mu\right| \geq \varepsilon\right) \;\leq\; 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-2n\varepsilon^2}{(b-a)^2}\right)$$

Uses only the range. Knows nothing about the distribution inside it.

Bernstein. Adds the variance $\sigma^2$:

$$P\left(\left|\bar{X} - \mu\right| \geq \varepsilon\right) \;\leq\; 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-n\varepsilon^2}{2\sigma^2 + \tfrac{2}{3}\varepsilon}\right)$$

McDiarmid. Generalises Hoeffding from an average to any function of the sample that changes by at most $c_i$ when one point changes. This is the one that does real work later, because the quantity movement C needs to bound, $\sup_h (R(h) - \hat{R}_S(h))$, is not an average of anything.

§3 How much the bounds give up

Reading the two panels together

Hoeffding's curve is identical in both panels. That is the whole content of "uses only the range": the bound for a coin that lands heads half the time is the same as for one that lands heads one time in twenty, and the second is enormously more predictable.

Bernstein pays for using the variance with a worse constant, which is why it loses at $p = 0.5$ where the variance is at its maximum, and wins by four orders of magnitude where the variance is small. Choosing between them is not a matter of one being sharper; it is a question about the problem.

Both are loose by roughly an order of magnitude even where they do best. Every generalisation bound in movements B and C is built on top of this looseness, and then adds more.

Reproduce the figure, then find the $p$ at which the two bounds cross. Then compute what $n$ each one says you need for a 95% confidence interval of width 0.02 at $p = 0.01$, and compare with the exact binomial answer. The ratio is the price of a distribution-free guarantee.

§4 The union bound, and the first real theorem

A finite hypothesis class

If $\mathcal{H}$ is finite, apply Hoeffding to each $h$ and add up the failure probabilities. With probability at least $1 - \delta$, simultaneously for every $h \in \mathcal{H}$:

$$R(h) \;\leq\; \hat{R}_S(h) + \sqrt{\frac{\log|\mathcal{H}| + \log(2/\delta)}{2n}}$$

Which is the first statement in this book that licenses choosing a hypothesis after seeing the data. The price of that licence is $\log|\mathcal{H}|$, the logarithm of how many things you were choosing among.

Why the logarithm is the good news

Doubling the number of candidates adds a constant to $\log|\mathcal{H}|$ rather than doubling the penalty. You can search over an enormous class and pay only logarithmically, which is the reason model search is viable at all.

The bad news arrives immediately: almost no interesting class is finite. A linear classifier has continuum-many members and $\log|\mathcal{H}| = \infty$, making the bound useless. Movement B's entire purpose is to replace the count with something that stays finite.

§5 What "with high probability" is a statement about

Pitfall: the randomness is in the sample, not the hypothesis

A bound holding "with probability $1-\delta$" means: over draws of the training set, at most a $\delta$ fraction produce a sample on which the bound fails. It says nothing about the sample you have.

So a 95% bound is not a promise about your model. It is a property of the procedure that produced it, in the same sense as Part V §9's confidence interval, and the same misinterpretation is available and equally common.

§6 What concentration cannot reach

Three assumptions, and each one is load-bearing

Independence. Correlated samples concentrate more slowly, and Part XIII §25's non-i.i.d. splitting is this failure arriving in practice. Part XXI §27's correlated evaluation suite is the same point again.

Boundedness. Hoeffding needs a range. Unbounded losses need heavier machinery, and Part XXI §18's Goodhart result is a reminder that tails decide outcomes.

A fixed distribution. Everything here assumes train and test come from the same $\mathcal{D}$. Movement F is what remains when they do not.

§7 What movement A establishes

Three things

The gap for a fixed hypothesis is easy and falls exponentially in $n$. The subject is hard only because you choose the hypothesis using the same data you evaluate it on.

Distribution-free bounds pay for their generality. Figure 1 measures the price: about an order of magnitude where they do best, and Hoeffding's indifference to the distribution costs four orders when the variance is small.

A logarithmic penalty in the size of the search is affordable. That is what makes choosing after seeing the data legitimate, and it needs a finite class, which nothing interesting is.
