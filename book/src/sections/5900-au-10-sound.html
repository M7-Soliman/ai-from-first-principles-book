Part ASound, and what a spectrogram commits you to

Audio arrives as one number per sample, tens of thousands of times a second, and almost nobody feeds a model that. The representation everybody uses instead was designed for a different purpose and discards the half of the signal Part XXVII measured as carrying the structure.

§1 The signal, and its rates

Four numbers that decide everything downstream

Sample rate. 8 kHz for telephony, 16 kHz for speech models, 44.1 or 48 kHz for music. Part XXVII §2 is why: the rate must exceed twice the highest frequency you care about, and speech intelligibility survives an 8 kHz cut in a way music does not.

Bit depth. 16 bits gives about 96 dB of dynamic range, which is enough. Quantisation noise is the floor, and §20 is what happens when you go far below it deliberately.

Channels. Stereo carries spatial information a mono model cannot recover, and most speech pipelines discard it in the first line.

Length. A ten-second clip at 16 kHz is 160,000 samples, much longer than a great many language-model contexts, and it is the reason nothing operates on raw samples for long.

§2 Why not the waveform

Three reasons, and the third is the one that matters

Length. A sequence model over 160,000 steps is expensive at best.

Nuisance variation. Two recordings of the same word can have completely different waveforms, Part XXVII §18's point in reverse: shifting the phase of every component changes the samples enormously and the sound not at all.

The structure is in frequency. Pitch, formants, timbre and noise are properties of the spectrum, and a model reading samples must discover the transform before it can use them. Some do, and §23 measures what that costs.

§3 The spectrogram, and its three parameters

Part XXVII §23, applied

Window length, hop, and window function. Part XXVII Figure 5 measured the first: the product of time and frequency resolution is constant, so a 25 ms window resolves phonemes and smears pitch while a 100 ms window does the reverse.

The near-universal choice for speech is a 25 ms window with a 10 ms hop, which is not a law of nature; it is a guess about how fast speech changes, made in the 1970s, and it is worth re-examining whenever the signal is not speech.

§4 The mel scale, measured

What the scale is a claim about

The mel scale spaces bands so they are roughly uniform in perceived pitch, which is approximately logarithmic above about 500 Hz. Pooling the spectrum into those bands is therefore a statement that frequency resolution should degrade with frequency the way human hearing's does.

That is right for speech intended for people and it is an assumption, not a preprocessing step. For machine fault detection, animal vocalisation, sonar or radio it is wrong, and the fourteen-fold pooling in Figure 1 is discarding exactly what you were listening for.

§5 What else is thrown away

Pitfall: the standard pipeline discards phase in its second line

A mel spectrogram is the magnitude of the short-time transform, pooled. The phase is not reduced or compressed; it is deleted.

Part XXVII §18 measured what that means for images: a reconstruction correlates +0.736 with its phase donor and −0.125 with its magnitude donor. The audio consequence is §7's figure, and the practical consequence is that every system producing a mel spectrogram needs a second model to invent the phase back, which is what a vocoder is.

§6 The cepstrum, and why it is still there

A representation that outlived its reason

Take the logarithm of the mel spectrum and transform again: convolution in the time domain becomes addition in the log-spectral domain, so the vocal tract's filter separates from the glottal source. The low coefficients describe the filter, which is what distinguishes vowels, and the rest can be discarded.

Cepstral coefficients dominated speech recognition for thirty years because they were compact and nearly uncorrelated, which mattered when the acoustic model was a mixture of diagonal-covariance Gaussians. Neural models do not need either property, and the field moved back to mel spectrograms, a case where a representation's advantage was a property of the model it fed rather than of the signal.

§7 Getting the waveform back

Go and read

Griffin & Lim (1984), Signal estimation from modified short-time Fourier transform. Four pages, and the algorithm Figure 2 implements: alternate between the current phase estimate and a projection back onto the target magnitude. The paper proves the reconstruction error can only fall or stay flat from one iteration to the next, but proves it in spectrogram distance, not waveform distance, which is exactly the gap the right- and left-hand panels of Figure 2 show. Read it to see that the method's limits were known at publication, not discovered later.

What a vocoder is for

Figure 2 is the argument for every neural vocoder in existence. Classical iterative estimation works; it is used, it is cheap, and it sounds like it: the characteristic metallic quality is the residual the right-hand panel converges to.

A learned vocoder does better because it is not solving Figure 2's problem. It is not estimating the phase that was there; it is generating a phase consistent with the magnitude and with everything it has learned about how speech is put together. Which is a different task with a different failure mode, and §19 is about it.

§8 What movement A establishes

Three things

The standard audio representation is a modelling assumption twice over. The window length is a claim about how fast the signal changes; the mel scale is a claim that resolution should degrade like human hearing's, and Figure 1 measures that as fourteen-fold pooling at the top of the range.

It deletes phase, which Part XXVII measured as carrying the structure.

So the pipeline needs a second model to put phase back. Figure 2 measures classical estimation converging in spectrogram space to a waveform correlation of 0.53, which is what a vocoder exists to beat.
