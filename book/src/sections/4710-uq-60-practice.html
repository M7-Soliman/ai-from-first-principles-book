Part FPractice

What to build, what to measure, what to report, and what an uncertainty estimate is worth to the person receiving it, who is usually not the person who produced it.

§39 Choosing a method

By what you have and what you need

Few points, expensive to acquire, and you will act on the uncertainty: a Gaussian process. Exact, and §13's acquisition function is the payoff.

A trained network and a held-out set: conformal. One calibration pass, a guarantee, and no retraining.

A training budget and a need for out-of-distribution behaviour: an ensemble. Figure 3's bootstrap ensemble scores 0.967; §19's full deep ensemble, retrained from different initialisations, costs the same order and has the best measured behaviour under shift.

A trained network and no budget at all: Laplace, post hoc. Better than nothing, which is what a point estimate gives you.

Combine them. Conformal on top of an ensemble is the strongest common option: the ensemble makes the intervals narrow and adaptive, conformal makes them valid.

§40 The measurement to run

Coverage, on held-out data, before anything else

Compute the fraction of held-out points falling inside your nominal $1-\alpha$ interval. It takes one line and it is the only thing that distinguishes an uncertainty estimate from a number.

Then compute it by group: by segment, by period, by any partition you have. Marginal coverage of 0.90 is consistent with 0.99 on the easy half and 0.81 on the hard one, which is §26 and is where the number stops being reassuring.

Then compute it on the oldest and newest slices separately. A gap between them is Figure 5 beginning, and it is visible before it is expensive.

§41 Under shift

What survives, in order of robustness

Nothing survives concept shift. If $p(y \mid x)$ changed, every method here is calibrated to a world that no longer exists.

Weighted conformal survives covariate shift, if you can estimate the density ratio and positivity holds.

Ensembles degrade most gracefully: their disagreement grows off-distribution, which is the behaviour you want even though the coverage guarantee is gone.

Single-model methods degrade worst, and confidently. A point estimate has no mechanism by which unfamiliarity could raise its uncertainty.

§42 What to report

Five things, and their absence is informative

The nominal level and the measured coverage, side by side. If they differ, that is the headline.

Coverage by group, or a statement that you only have the marginal guarantee.

The interval width, because validity without informativeness is Figure 4's constant predictor.

The assumption (exchangeability with the calibration set, or a kernel, or a posterior approximation) named, so a reader knows what would break it.

Whether the estimate separates the two kinds, since that determines whether more data would help.

§43 Communicating it

The part that is not technical

An interval reaches someone who will act on it, and how it is presented changes what they do. The consistent finding is that people under-use uncertainty when it arrives as a number attached to a confident-sounding prediction, and over-react when it arrives as a warning.

Two things help and are cheap. State the interval before the point estimate rather than after it. And express it in the units of the decision ("between 3 and 19 units, so the safe order is 19" rather than "12 ± 7"), because the recipient is going to do that conversion anyway, and doing it for them is where the uncertainty stops being decoration.

§44 What is unsolved

Four, distinguished by kind

Not yet measured. Whether the ranking of methods in Figure 3 holds on large models and realistic shifts. The published comparisons are mostly on small benchmarks, and the ensembles are usually the ones with the most compute.

Not currently measurable. Whether a model's uncertainty is well calibrated on inputs from a distribution nobody has sampled. This is the case that matters for deployment and the case no held-out set reaches.

Genuinely open. Conditional coverage without assumptions is impossible (a theorem), so the question is which approximations are worth their cost, and it is not settled.

Not empirical. What the costs are. §33's threshold needs a ratio that no dataset contains, and disagreement about it is not resolvable by measurement.

§45 What to carry forward

Five things

An interval makes a checkable promise, so check it. Figure 3 puts four standard methods between 0.790 and 0.993 against a nominal 0.95, and only measurement distinguishes them.

Two kinds of uncertainty, opposite responses. Figure 1's epistemic term falls 213-fold with more data and the aleatoric term does not move at all. A single reported number cannot tell you which you have.

Under-covering and over-covering are not symmetric. Too wide wastes information; too narrow is a decision taken on evidence that does not support it, and it is the failure that reaches production.

A guarantee is available, and it is narrower than you want. Conformal gives exact finite-sample marginal coverage for any model (Figure 4) and gives up conditional coverage and all robustness to shift, which Figure 5 measures falling to 0.496.

The probability is not the decision. A perfectly calibrated model at the default threshold loses 4.7× the optimal cost at a 50:1 ratio, and the fix is a conversation about costs rather than any modelling work.

Sixty seconds

Separate what you cannot know from what you do not know yet: only the second falls with more data. Produce an interval by whatever method you can afford, then measure whether it contains the truth as often as it promised, overall and by group. If you need a guarantee rather than a hope, conformal gives one for any model at the cost of a calibration set, and it holds exactly until the data stops resembling that set, at which point it fails silently. Then remember that none of this is a decision. Someone has to say what a mistake costs, and until they do, your threshold has answered for them.
