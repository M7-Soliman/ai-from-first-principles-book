Part GWhat came after, and what came back

§28 ended at a bottleneck, and Part XII answers it with attention. This movement is what happened next: attention's cost became the binding constraint, and people went back to the recurrence of §1 carrying the one thing it had always lacked: a way to run it in parallel. So the part ends where it began. The interesting question is what the round trip cost, and the answer is specific enough to measure.

§29 Reading this movement in order

Written last, and it depends on what follows it

Everything here postdates Part XII, and several sections assume attention (XII), the cost model of serving (XIX), and the memory hierarchy (XXXII §1). If you are reading straight through, this movement is better after those than before them, and nothing later depends on it.

It is in Part XI rather than in a part of its own because the object under discussion is a recurrence, and everything §1 to §15 established about recurrences (the state as a bottleneck, the gradient through repeated multiplication, the memory the state can hold) applies unchanged. What changed is one algorithmic fact and one design idea, and both are cheap to state once the recurrence is already understood.

§30 The cost, counted properly

The case for recurrence is an inference case

"Attention is quadratic, so it is expensive" skips a step. The quadratic term does not dominate until the sequence exceeds the model dimension, so a model with $d=4096$ run at a 4,096-token context is at the crossover exactly, and below it attention is the cheaper of the two. In training arithmetic the two are much closer than the complexity classes suggest, and which wins depends on a ratio nobody quotes.

Where the argument is unanswerable is generation. Attention must attend to everything already produced, so the cost of the next token grows without bound and the KV cache grows with it, which is Part XIX's whole subject. A fixed-size state makes both constant. That is the trade being bought, and stating it as "linear versus quadratic" hides which half of the workload it applies to.

§31 Removing the softmax

Go and read

Katharopoulos, Vyas, Pappas & Fleuret (2020), Transformers are RNNs: fast autoregressive transformers with linear attention. The reassociation below, and the observation stated in the title. Read it for the derivation and Figure 11 for what the substitution costs: the paper is clear that it is a substitution, and the follow-up literature is often less so.

Why the exponential is what costs

Attention computes $\sum_j \frac{\exp(q_i\!\cdot\!k_j)}{\sum_l \exp(q_i\!\cdot\!k_l)} v_j$. The exponential of a product cannot be factored, so every pair $(i,j)$ must be formed: that is where the $L^2$ comes from.

Replace $\exp(q\cdot k)$ with $\phi(q)\cdot\phi(k)$ for some positive feature map and the sum reassociates:

$$\sum_j \phi(q_i)^{\!\top}\phi(k_j)\,v_j^{\!\top} = \phi(q_i)^{\!\top}\underbrace{\sum_j \phi(k_j)v_j^{\!\top}}_{\text{a matrix, not a list}}$$

The inner sum does not depend on $i$. Accumulate it once, and the whole layer is a recurrence with a matrix-valued state: $S_t = S_{t-1} + \phi(k_t)v_t^{\!\top}$. Linear attention and a linear RNN are the same object written twice.

§32 What that costs

The limitation is the missing exponential, not the resolution

The flat line in the right panel is the result worth carrying. More dimension does not help linear attention point more sharply, at all, and the reason is structural: with a positive feature map every score is a sum of $m$ positive terms sharing a large common component. The discriminative part grows like $\sqrt{m}$ and the common part like $m$, so relative concentration does not improve with dimension: essentially flat, matching Figure 11's dimension sweep, rather than sharpening the way a bigger head would for softmax.

So this is not an approximation that a bigger head closes. Softmax's exponential is what turns a difference in scores into a ratio of weights, and any mechanism that drops it to gain associativity gives up the ability to select, permanently, and by more as the context grows. Every serious system that uses a linear-state layer therefore keeps some attention as well, which is §37.

§33 The parallel scan

Go and read

Blelloch (1990), Prefix sums and their applications. Not a deep-learning paper, a parallel-algorithms technical report, and that is the point: the depth-versus-work trade below is not new to sequence models, it is decades old and imported wholesale. Read §1-2 for the two-pass, work-efficient scan that gives the "Blelloch form" below its name, and notice the algorithm predates the hardware that makes it worth using by thirty years.

The algorithmic fact the 1990s did not have

A linear recurrence $h_t = a_t h_{t-1} + b_t$ composes associatively: applying $(a_1,b_1)$ then $(a_2,b_2)$ is $(a_1a_2,\; a_2b_1+b_2)$, and that composition is associative. Anything associative can be evaluated by a scan in logarithmic depth.

This is why the nonlinearity had to go. §16's gates buy the gradient behaviour by making the recurrence nonlinear, and a nonlinear recurrence has no associative composition, so it must be walked one step at a time. Linearity is the price of parallelism, and gating had to be recovered a different way (§35).

Two algorithms both called "parallel scan"

The measured work ratio grows with a log factor because the implementation is the simple Hillis–Steele form, which does $O(L\log L)$ work. The Blelloch form does $O(L)$ (about twice the sequential work, with no log) at the cost of two passes and more awkward indexing. Both are called "parallel scan" constantly and the difference is exactly a factor of $\log L$ in arithmetic.

Either way the trade has the same shape as Part XXXII's hardware accounting, though the axis there (§1) is bytes moved against FLOPs rather than depth against work: depth is what costs on parallel hardware and work is not. Spending 30× the arithmetic to cut 1,000,000 sequential steps to 20 is an obviously good deal on a machine with thousands of lanes and an obviously bad one on a machine with one.

§34 State space models

Go and read

Gu, Goel & Ré (2022), Efficiently modeling long sequences with structured state spaces (S4). The discretised linear system as a sequence layer, the convolutional view, and the HiPPO initialisation. The part to read is why the initialisation matters, which is §12's vanishing gradient restated as a choice about where the timescales go.

The same recurrence, discretised from a continuous system

Write a linear system in continuous time, $h'(t) = Ah(t) + Bx(t)$, $y = Ch$ (Part XXXI's state space model, used here as a sequence layer). Discretising with step $\Delta$ gives $h_t = \bar{A}h_{t-1} + \bar{B}x_t$ with $\bar{A} = \exp(\Delta A)$.

Three things come free. It is linear, so §33's scan applies. Unrolled, it is a convolution with kernel $(C\bar{B},\, C\bar{A}\bar{B},\, C\bar{A}^2\bar{B},\dots)$, so training can use an FFT instead. And the eigenvalues of $A$ are the timescales, made explicit; §12's vanishing gradient becomes a statement about where you put them, which is what the HiPPO initialisation is: a choice of $A$ whose state approximates a basis of the history rather than decaying arbitrarily.

§35 What a fixed state cannot hold

Go and read

Arora et al. (2024), Zoology: measuring and improving recall in efficient language models. The paper that isolated the gap Figure 13 measures in miniature: subquadratic models match attention on average perplexity and lose badly and specifically on associative recall, with the loss concentrated in an identifiable slice of tokens rather than spread evenly across them. Read §4 for the synthetic recall task this section's figure is a stripped-down version of.

The one thing attention has that a recurrence cannot

§8 said the state is a bottleneck. Figure 13 puts a number on it. A fixed state has a capacity, the capacity is proportional to its size, and past it the information is not degraded but gone. Attention has no such limit because it keeps every key: that is what the KV cache is, and it is the same fact seen from the cost side in Figure 10.

This is why "linear-time model matches transformer on perplexity" and "linear-time model is much worse at retrieving a fact from long context" are both true and not in tension. Perplexity averages over predictions that mostly need recent context; exact retrieval is the measurement that isolates capacity. If your workload is long-context retrieval, Figure 13 is the figure that applies to you and an average loss is not.

§36 Selectivity

Go and read

Gu & Dao (2024), Mamba: linear-time sequence modeling with selective state spaces. Selectivity, and the hardware-aware scan that makes it trainable. Figure 14 is its central motivation at a scale a figure script can reach. Note what is given up: input-dependence destroys time invariance, so the convolution §34 relied on is gone and the scan is all that is left.

What "selective" means, and why it was the missing piece

A classical state space model has a decay fixed at training time, so it applies the same forgetting to every token. It is a linear time-invariant system, and the price of time invariance is that it cannot condition on content: it cannot decide that this token is worth keeping and the next thousand are not.

Making $\Delta$, $B$ and $C$ functions of the input restores that, and Figure 14 measures what it is worth: on a task that is entirely about ignoring the irrelevant, a perfect gate is exact and no fixed decay reaches 0.17. The state is a single number in both arms: the mechanism buys this, not the capacity.

The cost is that the system is no longer time-invariant, so it is no longer a convolution and the FFT route is gone. What remains is §33's scan, which is why the associativity is load-bearing rather than an optimisation: without it, selectivity would put you back at $O(L)$ sequential steps and the whole approach would be untrainable at scale. The engineering that makes this fast, keeping the state in fast memory and never materialising it, is Part XXXII §9's fusion applied to a scan.

§37 What practice settled on

Go and read

Beltagy, Peters & Cohan (2020), Longformer: the long-document transformer. An early instance of the hybrid below: local windowed attention everywhere, plus a handful of tokens given unrestricted global attention by task-specific design. Read §2's dilated-window construction beside the "hybrids" idea that follows (the same two knobs, windowed exactness and sparse global reach, worked out from the attention side years before the recurrent forms reinvented them from the other direction).

Hybrids, and the reason they are hybrids

Figures 11 and 13 are two views of one limitation: a fixed-size state cannot select and cannot store. Figure 10 is the compensating advantage: constant cost per generated token. Neither dominates, so what practice arrived at is mixtures: mostly recurrent layers with a small number of full-attention layers interleaved.

The reasoning is capacity accounting rather than taste. The attention layers supply exact recall and sharp selection where those are needed; the recurrent layers supply cheap generation everywhere else; and the ratio is set by how much of your workload actually needs retrieval. A few attention layers in a deep stack recover most of the retrieval ability at a small fraction of the cache.

Two related families are worth naming since they are the same trade at different points. Sliding-window attention keeps exact attention within a window and drops everything beyond it (a fixed cache, like a recurrence, with exact recall inside the window instead of lossy recall everywhere). Gated linear attention and the modern linear-RNN family put a decay back into the linear-attention state so it forgets rather than saturating, which is §36's idea applied to §31's mechanism. These are not competing lineages; they are the same two knobs.

§38 What to carry forward

Five things

The recurrence came back because inference is not training. Figure 10 puts the training crossover at exactly $L=d$, so the two are comparable across the range most systems run, while the per-token generation gap is unbounded. State the workload before quoting a complexity.

Linearity is the price of parallelism. A linear recurrence composes associatively and scans in $\log_2 L$ depth; Figure 12 measures 1,048,576 steps becoming 20, paid for with 30× the arithmetic. Gating then has to be recovered some other way.

Dropping the softmax gives up selection permanently. Figure 11 measures linear attention near uniform, worsening from 11× to 647× behind softmax with length, and not improving with dimension at all: the limitation is the missing exponential, not the resolution.

A fixed state has a capacity and it is reached. Figure 13 measures usable length exactly proportional to state size. Perplexity will not show you this; exact retrieval will.

Selectivity is a mechanism, not a size. Figure 14 measures a perfect input-dependent gate at 1.000 against 0.166 for the best fixed decay, with one number of state in both arms, and it costs time invariance, which is why the scan rather than the FFT is what makes it work.
