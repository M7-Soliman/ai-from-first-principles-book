Part DThe geometry of embeddings

A representation is a set of points in a high-dimensional space, and the space has properties nobody chose. This movement is about the ones that change what your distances mean.

§24 Similarity, and the three measures

Dot product, cosine, Euclidean

For vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$:

$$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}, \qquad \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|}, \qquad \|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|_2$$

They are not interchangeable. The dot product rewards magnitude: a long vector scores well against everything, so it favours whatever the model is confident or frequent about. Cosine discards magnitude entirely and compares direction. Euclidean distance uses both.

The one identity worth memorising: for normalised vectors, $\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|^2 = 2 - 2\,(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b})$. On the unit sphere, Euclidean ranking and cosine ranking are the same ranking, which is why normalising first makes the choice between them disappear, and why contrastive objectives normalise.

Pitfall: the index and the training objective must agree

A model trained with a cosine objective and queried with dot products is being asked a question it was not trained to answer. The failure is quiet: results are plausible, slightly worse, and biased toward high-norm items, which in text embeddings correlates with document length and frequency.

Establish which similarity the encoder was trained with, and use that one. If you cannot find out, normalise, and then it does not matter.

§25 Anisotropy

The cone

Embeddings from a trained network are usually not spread over the sphere. They occupy a narrow cone: the average cosine similarity between two randomly chosen, unrelated items is far above zero, often 0.6 or higher where random directions in high dimensions would give approximately 0.

The consequence is a loss of resolution. If everything is at similarity 0.7 and related things are at 0.8, then almost all of the available range is being spent on a constant offset, and the differences that matter are compressed into what is left.

Why this happens, and the cheap repair

A large shared component in the representation (a direction almost every item has) produces it. Frequency effects contribute: in language models, rare tokens are pushed into a distinct region, and the common ones cluster.

The repair is to remove what is shared. Centre the embeddings by subtracting the mean, which alone removes most of the offset, and optionally whiten them so the covariance is isotropic. Both are computed on a sample and applied as a fixed affine map, so retrieval costs nothing extra.

Whitening is not free: it amplifies directions of small variance, which may be noise, so it can help retrieval and hurt a downstream classifier. Centring is nearly always safe, and it is the first thing to try when a similarity distribution looks compressed.

§26 Hubness

What hubness is

Let $N_k(i)$ count how many other points have $i$ among their $k$ nearest neighbours. Its mean is $k$ by construction, whatever the dimension. In high dimensions its distribution becomes extremely skewed: a few points are everyone's neighbour, and many are nobody's.

This is a property of high-dimensional geometry, not of any model. The figure uses pure Gaussian noise: there is no structure to find and the effect appears anyway.

Why it happens, and why it matters for anything that retrieves

The right panel is the explanation. In high dimensions the norms concentrate, but not perfectly, and a point slightly nearer the centre of the cloud is slightly nearer to everything. That small advantage is applied across every query, so it compounds into a large one. The correlation between hub-ness and closeness to the centre is +0.88 at $d=10$ and stays strong throughout.

Consequences, all observed in deployed systems:

  • A handful of items are returned for almost every query, regardless of relevance.
  • A large fraction of the corpus is unreachable by nearest-neighbour search at any $k$.
  • Recommendation degenerates toward the same few items, and that looks like popularity bias caused by the data, when a substantial part of it is caused by the geometry.

The mitigations follow the mechanism. Centring (§25) removes the shared component that creates the advantage. Mutual $k$-nearest neighbours, requiring the relation to hold in both directions, discards hub relationships by construction. And rank-based corrections rescale each item's similarities by its own distance distribution, which is what most production systems end up doing.

§27 The linear representation hypothesis

The claim, and its most famous instance

The hypothesis: features are directions. A concept corresponds to a direction in representation space, its presence to a large component along that direction, and combinations of concepts to sums of their directions.

Its famous instance is the word analogy:

$$\text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman} \approx \text{queen}$$

If gender is a direction and royalty is a direction, and the two are approximately independent, then the arithmetic should work. It does, well enough to have shaped a decade of intuition.

Pitfall: the analogy result is weaker than its fame

Two facts about the standard demonstration are worth knowing before relying on it.

The three input words are excluded from the answer. Standard evaluation code removes king, man and woman from the candidate set before taking the nearest neighbour. Without that exclusion the nearest vector to the result is very often king itself: the arithmetic moved a short distance and the answer is dominated by the starting point. The exclusion is defensible and it is rarely mentioned, so the reported accuracy is systematically better than the raw operation.

It works for some relations and not others. Reliable on morphology and geography; substantially worse on relations that are not close to a consistent offset. The aggregate number averages over both.

The hypothesis survives all of this in weakened form: directions do carry meaning, and §34 depends on it. What does not survive is the idea that representation space is a tidy vector space of independent concepts.

§28 Neighbour embeddings, and what they destroy

Representations get looked at through t-SNE or UMAP, which reduce them to two dimensions. These plots are the most widely reproduced images in the field and the most widely over-read.

Why the distortion is the algorithm working correctly

t-SNE minimises a divergence between neighbour probability distributions, with a per-point bandwidth chosen so each point has the same effective number of neighbours: that is what perplexity sets. A point in a tight cluster and a point in a diffuse one are therefore rescaled to comparable local neighbourhoods by construction.

So the flattening in the figure is not a failure. It is the method's definition. The objective contains no term that preserves the ratio of one cluster's size to another's, or one gap to another, and an optimiser will not deliver a property nobody asked for.

What these plots do and do not support

Supported: that points form groups; roughly which points are near which; that a class is split into several groups, or that two classes overlap.

Not supported: that one cluster is tighter than another; that two clusters are further apart than two others; that the space between clusters is empty; anything about the global arrangement. Distance between well-separated clusters is close to arbitrary.

UMAP preserves more global structure than t-SNE and does not preserve enough to make these readings safe. Both are strongly affected by their parameters (perplexity, or the number of neighbours) and it is standard practice to run several settings and confirm the conclusion survives all of them.

Pitfall: a projection is a hypothesis generator, not evidence

The failure mode is reading a claim off the picture and reporting it: "the classes are well separated after fine-tuning", "these two groups are close so the model confuses them", "training tightened the clusters".

Every one of these is checkable in the original space, cheaply: a linear probe for separability (§31), a confusion matrix for confusion, average within-cluster distance for tightness. Use the plot to notice something, then measure the something. Part XIV §11's discipline, applied to a picture.

§29 Retrieval

From embedding to index

The practical destination of most embeddings is nearest-neighbour search over millions of items, where exact search is too slow. Approximate methods trade recall for latency.

The historical route here was semantic hashing: training an autoencoder with a binary code layer so two similar items land in codes a short Hamming distance apart, turning retrieval into a table lookup. IVF, HNSW and product quantization are its modern descendants: same goal, sub-linear search over a learned or approximate index, better recall at the same budget.

IVF partitions the space by clustering and searches only the nearest few partitions; fast, and it misses items near a boundary. HNSW builds a navigable graph with long-range links at the top and short ones at the bottom, and walks it greedily; usually the best recall–latency tradeoff, at a substantial memory cost. Product quantization splits the vector into subvectors and replaces each with a codebook entry, shrinking memory by an order of magnitude at some loss of precision.

The measurement that matters is recall@k against exact search, on your own data. It is cheap to compute on a sample and it is the only thing that tells you what the index is costing you.

Pitfall: two independent sources of loss

A retrieval system that returns bad results has two suspects and they need separating: the embedding may not place the right things near each other, or the index may fail to find the near things.

Separate them by running exact search on a sample. If exact search is also bad, the index is innocent and the encoder is the problem. If exact search is good and the index is not, tune the index. Conflating the two produces months of work on the wrong component, and §26 is the reason the encoder is guilty more often than teams expect.

§30 What movement D establishes

The geometry is not neutral

Four properties, none of them designed, all of them consequential.

Similarity measures disagree, and the one to use is the one the encoder was trained with.

Embeddings occupy a cone, which compresses the range that carries your signal, and centring largely repairs it.

High-dimensional neighbourhoods are lopsided (a few hubs, many orphans) as a property of the geometry rather than of the data.

Neighbour embeddings preserve neighbourhoods and nothing else, so cluster sizes and gaps in those plots carry no information.

The common thread: high-dimensional space does not behave like the two-dimensional pictures everyone reasons with, and the differences are large enough to change conclusions rather than merely qualify them.
