Before you start

Part II taught you to reason forward: given a distribution, what does a sample look like. This part reverses the arrow, and the reversal is where machine learning actually lives.

§0.1 The direction that matters

Everything in Part II ran one way. Given a fair coin, what is the chance of seven heads in ten flips? Given a Gaussian with known mean and variance, how much mass lies beyond two standard deviations?

Nobody ever handed you a fair coin. In practice you have ten flips and a question about the coin, and that is a different problem: harder, and the one every model in this book is solving. Probability reasons from model to data. Statistics reasons from data to model.

A trained model is an estimate. Its parameters are estimates, its reported accuracy is an estimate, and the claim that it beats the previous model is a claim about two estimates. Without the machinery in this part you can compute all of those numbers and say nothing defensible about any of them.

§0.2 Four questions this part answers

Each of these is asked constantly in practice and answered badly.

  1. My model scored 94.2% on the test set. What is the uncertainty on that number? Sections 6–10.
  2. Model A got 94.2%, model B got 93.8%. Is A actually better? Sections 11–16.
  3. I tried two hundred configurations and reported the best. What did that cost me? Sections 14–15.
  4. Why does more capacity reduce one kind of error and increase another? Section 20, derived rather than asserted.
Why this matters

Reported results in this field are frequently indefensible, and not because anyone is dishonest. Point estimates get published with no interval. Differences well inside the noise are described as improvements. Test sets get reused hundreds of times during development and then treated as though they were fresh. Hyperparameter search is multiple hypothesis testing and is almost never accounted for as such.

Every one of those is a mistake this part teaches you to see. That makes it the most professionally consequential part of the book, in the narrow sense that it will change what you are willing to claim.

§0.4 The habit for this part

Earlier parts asked you to write down shapes, to say what is random, and never to trust an unchecked number. This part's is:

Every number you report is a random variable. Say how random.

An accuracy is not a property of a model; it is a property of a model and the particular test set you happened to draw. Run it on a different sample and you get a different number. The size of that wobble is not a detail; frequently it is larger than the difference you are claiming to have found.

Getting into the habit of asking "compared to what noise?" before reporting anything is most of what this part is for.
