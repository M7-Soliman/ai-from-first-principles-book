Part AReading mathematics

§1 How to read a formula

Before any content, a skill nobody teaches explicitly: how to read mathematical notation without freezing.

Most people read a formula the way they read a sentence: left to right, at speed, expecting to understand by the end. That fails, and the failure feels like stupidity when it is actually a technique problem. Mathematics is compressed. A single line can hold a paragraph of meaning, and it has to be decompressed deliberately.

It is worth being precise about what goes wrong, because the diagnosis contains the cure. When you read ordinary English you can afford to move fast, because English is enormously redundant. If you miss a word, the words around it usually tell you what the missing one must have been; if you mishear a syllable, grammar repairs it before you notice. Mathematics has none of that padding. Every symbol is load-bearing. Change one subscript and the statement becomes false, and nothing else in the line will warn you. So the reading strategy that serves you everywhere else (sweep forward, let the gist accumulate) is exactly wrong here, and worse, it fails silently. You finish the line, feel that you followed it, and have absorbed nothing.

The second problem is that a formula is not written in the order it should be read. Prose is built to be consumed front to back. A formula is a structure (closer to a diagram than a sentence), and its most important feature is rarely at the left-hand end. You have to find the shape of it first and then climb down inside, which means your first pass over an unfamiliar formula should not be a reading at all. It should be a survey: what kind of thing is this, before what does it say.

The procedure

Take this, which you will meet in Part II:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \log p(y^{(i)} \mid x^{(i)}; \theta)$$

If that looks like noise, good: that is the honest reaction, and here is how to take it apart.

Step 1: find the equals sign. Everything left of it is a name; everything right is the recipe. So $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ is just a label for whatever the right side computes. Something called $\mathcal{L}$ that depends on something called $\theta$.

Step 2: identify the outermost operation. Ignore the interior entirely. Here it is: negative, times one-over-$m$, times a sum. So the whole thing is the negative average of some quantity. That alone is real progress.

Step 3: unpack one level. What is being averaged? The thing after the $\sum$: $\log p(\dots)$. The log of something.

Step 4: name every symbol out loud, even the ones you know. $m$ is a count, probably of training examples, given the $i=1$ to $m$. The superscript $(i)$ means "belonging to example $i$", not an exponent. $\theta$ is parameters. The bar $\mid$ means "given".

Step 5: say the whole thing in English. "The loss is the negative average, over all $m$ training examples, of the log probability the model assigns to the correct answer."

That sentence is the formula. Nothing was lost. The notation is shorter, not deeper.

Look back at what that actually took. You did no algebra. You computed nothing. You used no fact you did not already possess when you started. All you did was locate the structure (outermost operation first, then one level in, then the individual symbols) and refuse to advance until every level had a name you could say out loud. That is the whole technique, and it survives contact with formulas far uglier than this one, because ugliness in mathematics is almost always depth of nesting rather than genuine difficulty. A frightening expression is usually four unfrightening ones stacked.

The step people skip is the fourth, and it is the one that does the work. Naming symbols you already know feels like a waste of time, so the eye slides over $m$ and $\theta$ and lands on whatever looks unfamiliar. But the unfamiliar part is almost never where the difficulty lives. The difficulty lives in a superscript you assumed was an exponent, or a subscript whose index you never checked, or a semicolon you read as a comma. Saying every symbol aloud is slow precisely because it forces you to look at the parts your eye wants to skip, and those are the parts that were going to cost you an hour later.

One more habit, and it costs nothing: read with a pen. Not to take notes, but to copy the formula out yourself, once, slowly, as you take it apart. Writing it by hand drags your attention through every symbol in sequence, which is exactly what reading it silently permits you to avoid. Nearly everyone who works in a mathematical field does this, and nearly nobody mentions to students that they do, which leaves the impression that professionals simply look at a page and understand it. They do not. They have a pen.

Intuition: notation is compression, not encryption

Mathematical notation exists because writing that English sentence every time would be unbearable. It is an abbreviation system built by people who got tired of writing things out, not a code designed to exclude you.

The corollary matters: anything written in symbols can be said in words. If you cannot say a formula in English, you have not read it yet, and no amount of staring will change that. Decompose instead.

Pitfall: reading at prose speed

A page of mathematics can take an hour, and that is normal rather than a sign of trouble. Professional mathematicians read their own field's papers slowly, with a pen. If you are moving through this document at reading speed, you are moving too fast, and the feeling of "I sort of followed that" is precisely what evaporates when you try to use it.

§2 The symbols, decoded

A reference for everything you will meet. Skim it now; come back when something unfamiliar appears.

Before the tables, a word about why there are Greek letters at all, since the usual suspicion (that they exist to make the subject look harder than it is) is understandable and wrong. Mathematics ran out of alphabet. A single equation in this book may carry an input, a weight, a bias, a learning rate, a count of examples, an index over those examples, a regularisation strength, and a target, and every one of them needs a symbol short enough to write a hundred times. Latin letters were exhausted centuries ago, so the field reached for the next alphabet its readers could be assumed to recognise, and then for boldface, and then for hats and bars and tildes.

What follows from that is more useful than it sounds. Because the letters were allocated by convention rather than logic, the convention is real information. A paper that writes $\alpha$ almost certainly means a step size; $\theta$ almost certainly means the parameters being learned; $\lambda$ almost certainly means either a regularisation strength or an eigenvalue, and the surrounding sentence tells you which within a few words. You are not decoding a cipher. You are recognising vocabulary, and like all vocabulary it becomes invisible with exposure; after a hundred pages you will read $\nabla_\theta L$ the way you read a road sign, without translating.

Greek letters, and what they conventionally mean

History: why the words look like that

Two of the most ordinary words in this book are the same man's name, worn down by a thousand years of transmission.

Around 820, the Persian scholar Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi wrote a treatise whose title contained the phrase al-jabr, roughly "restoration", the operation of moving a subtracted term to the other side of an equation to make it positive. Latin translators kept the word. Al-jabr became algebra.

His own name fared similarly. Latinised as Algoritmi, it came to mean the step-by-step calculating procedures his work described. Al-Khwarizmi became algorithm.

So when you write an algorithm to do algebra, you are saying his name twice.

Pitfall: the same letter means different things

$\sigma$ is standard deviation and the sigmoid function. $\Sigma$ is summation and a covariance matrix. $\lambda$ is regularisation strength and an eigenvalue. There is no fixing this; the conventions grew up in different fields and collided.

Context resolves it, and there is a reliable tell: if it has an argument in parentheses, it is a function. $\sigma(x)$ is the sigmoid; a bare $\sigma$ or $\sigma^2$ is a standard deviation.

The decorations: subscripts, superscripts, hats and bars

More reading errors come from these than from any symbol in the tables, because they are small, they are easy to skim past, and several of them look identical to things they are not.

A subscript almost always means "which one". In $x_3$ the 3 selects the third component of a vector; in $w_{ij}$ the pair selects a single entry of a matrix. Read a subscript as an index into something; the mental translation is x[3], and that translation is exact.

A superscript is where the trouble is, because it does two entirely different jobs. Bare, it is a power: $x^2$ is $x$ squared. Wrapped in parentheses, it is an index: $x^{(i)}$ means the $i$-th training example and has nothing to do with exponentiation. Machine learning uses this second form constantly, because the subscript slot is already taken by the components of each example, so $x^{(i)}_j$ is the $j$-th feature of the $i$-th example, and both numbers are indices while neither is a power. When a superscript appears in this book and it is not obviously an exponent, check for the parentheses.

The accents are simpler and consistent. A hat, $\hat{y}$, marks an estimate: something the model produced, as opposed to the truth. A bar, $\bar{x}$, marks an average. A star or asterisk, $\theta^*$, marks the optimal value, the one you wish you had. So $\hat{y}$ versus $y$ is prediction versus reality, and the entire enterprise of training is the business of shrinking the distance between a symbol with a hat and the same symbol without one.

Pitfall: $\min$ versus $\arg\min$

These return different things and confusing them will genuinely mislead you.

$\min_x f(x)$ is the smallest value $f$ attains. For $f(x) = (x-3)^2$, that is $0$.

$\arg\min_x f(x)$ is the input that achieves it. For the same $f$, that is $3$.

Training a model is always an $\arg\min$: you want the parameters, not the loss value. Nobody cares that the loss got to 0.03; they care which weights did it.

Try it

Say this in plain English, using the five-step procedure from §1:

$$\hat{\theta} = \arg\min_{\theta} \; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y^{(i)} - f(x^{(i)}; \theta)\big)^2$$

Answer. Outermost operation: an $\arg\min$ over $\theta$. So we are choosing parameters. What are we minimising? An average ($\frac1n \sum$) over $n$ examples. Of what? A squared difference between $y^{(i)}$ (the true answer for example $i$) and $f(x^{(i)}; \theta)$ (the model's prediction for that example, using parameters $\theta$).

In English: "choose the parameters that minimise the average squared error between the model's predictions and the true answers." That is linear regression, and the formula says nothing more than that sentence does.
