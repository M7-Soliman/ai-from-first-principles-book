§4 Discrete distributions and the PMF

Definition: probability mass function

For a discrete random variable $X$, the PMF $P(X = x)$ gives the probability of each possible value. It must satisfy two conditions:

$0 \leq P(X = x) \leq 1$ for every $x$,   and   $\sum_x P(X = x) = 1$.

That is the entire definition. Probabilities are never negative, never exceed one, and the total across all outcomes is exactly one: the outcome has to be something.

Think of a PMF as a fixed budget of probability, one unit in total, distributed across the possible outcomes. Giving more to one outcome necessarily takes it from another. That conservation is the source of a great deal of intuition later, including why softmax behaves the way it does in §20.

Notation gets abbreviated constantly. All of these mean the same thing, and you will see all of them:

$$P(X = x) \quad\equiv\quad P(x) \quad\equiv\quad p(x)$$

The shorthand is convenient and occasionally ambiguous: $p(x)$ and $p(y)$ may refer to two entirely different functions, distinguished only by the name of their argument. This is sloppy but universal, and once you expect it, it stops being a problem.

§5 Continuous distributions and the PDF

In 60 seconds

For a continuous variable the probability of any exact value is zero, so we ask about intervals instead, and probability becomes area under a curve. The height of that curve is a density, a probability per unit, and it may exceed 1 without contradiction. Only the total area is capped.

Now the trap. For a continuous variable, the probability of any exact value is zero.

That sounds wrong, so here is why it must be true. Suppose you pick a real number uniformly between 0 and 1. There are infinitely many candidates. If each had some probability $\epsilon > 0$, the total would be $\epsilon \times \infty = \infty$, which violates the requirement that everything sums to 1. The only consistent assignment is zero for each individual point.

So we stop asking about points and start asking about intervals.

Definition: probability density function

For a continuous random variable, the PDF $p(x)$ satisfies

$p(x) \geq 0$ for every $x$,   $\int p(x)\,dx = 1$,   and   $P(a \leq X \leq b) = \int_a^b p(x)\,dx$.

Probability is the area under the curve, never the height of it.

Pitfall: a density is not a probability, and can exceed 1

This is the single most common misconception in the chapter, so it gets its own figure. $p(x) = 2.66$ is a perfectly legal density value. It does not mean "a 266% chance", which would be nonsense; it means the probability is concentrated near $x$, at a rate of 2.66 per unit of $x$.

The units are the giveaway. A probability is a pure number. A density is a probability per unit: per metre, per second, per volt. Densities are rates, and rates can be arbitrarily large as long as they act over a small enough interval.

Intuition: the population density analogy

Population density in Manhattan is roughly 27,000 people per square kilometre. That number exceeding one is not a paradox, because it is a rate. Ask for the population at a single mathematical point and the answer is zero; points have no area. Ask for the population of a neighbourhood and you multiply density by area and get a real count.

Probability density works identically. $p(x)$ is the rate; $p(x)\,dx$ over a region is the probability.

Left out on purpose: the measure-theoretic scaffolding behind this (measure zero, "almost everywhere", the paradoxical constructions like Banach–Tarski that motivate it) buys rigour a model-builder does not need here; it is skipped so this stays about computing, not about set theory.

Change of variables

One consequence of probability being area rather than height is that densities do not simply inherit from one another when you transform the variable. Suppose $X$ has density $p_X(x)$ and you define $Y = g(X)$ for some invertible, differentiable function $g$. What is $p_Y(y)$?

Probability must be conserved: the chance that $X$ falls in a tiny interval $[x, x + dx]$ must equal the chance that $Y$ falls in the corresponding interval $[y, y + dy]$. That is,

$$p_X(x)\,|dx| = p_Y(y)\,|dy|$$

Dividing through by $|dy|$ and using $x = g^{-1}(y)$ gives the change-of-variables formula:

Change of variables: 1-D

If $Y = g(X)$ with $g$ invertible and differentiable, and $x = g^{-1}(y)$, then

$$p_Y(y) = p_X\!\left(g^{-1}(y)\right) \left|\frac{dx}{dy}\right|$$

The term $|dx/dy|$ is the absolute Jacobian: it accounts for how much a small interval in $y$ stretches or compresses relative to the corresponding interval in $x$. A transformation that stretches space by a factor $c$ squashes the density by the same factor, because the same probability is now spread over a wider region.

The multivariate version replaces the scalar derivative with the determinant of the Jacobian matrix of the inverse transform:

$$p_Y(\mathbf{y}) = p_X\!\left(g^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\right) \left|\det J_{g^{-1}}(\mathbf{y})\right|$$

where $J_{g^{-1}}$ is the matrix of partial derivatives $\partial x_i / \partial y_j$. The intuition is identical: the Jacobian determinant measures how much volume a small region in $\mathbf{y}$-space corresponds to in $\mathbf{x}$-space.

Worked: shifting and scaling a Uniform

Let $X \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$, so $p_X(x) = 1$ for $x \in [0, 1]$. Define $Y = 2X + 3$.

What range does $Y$ take? Since $X \in [0, 1]$, $Y \in [3, 5]$.

Apply the formula. The inverse is $x = g^{-1}(y) = (y - 3)/2$, so $|dx/dy| = 1/2$.

$$p_Y(y) = p_X\!\left(\tfrac{y-3}{2}\right) \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} \qquad \text{for } y \in [3, 5]$$

Check the area. $\int_3^5 \tfrac{1}{2}\,dy = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2 = 1$. ✓

The density is halved because the interval $[0, 1]$ was stretched to $[3, 5]$ (twice as wide), so the same probability unit is now spread over twice as much of the $y$-axis. Height times width is conserved: $1 \times 1 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2$.

This formula is load-bearing later. Normalising flows (Part XVI §21) are exactly invertible neural networks whose Jacobian determinant is cheap to compute, allowing them to transform a simple density into a complex one while remaining tractable.

§6 Joint and marginal

Real problems involve several uncertain quantities at once. The joint distribution $P(X = x, Y = y)$ gives the probability that both hold simultaneously; read the comma as "and".

Given a joint distribution, you can always recover the behaviour of one variable alone by summing over everything the other could have been:

$$P(X = x) = \sum_y P(X = x, Y = y)$$

This is marginalisation, and the continuous version replaces the sum with an integral in the obvious way. The name is delightfully literal: with a joint distribution written as a table, you compute these totals and write them in the margins of the page.

Intuition: marginalising is forgetting

Marginalising out $Y$ means "I no longer care about $Y$; account for every value it could take and fold them together." You are deliberately discarding information, and the sum is what keeps the total probability at 1 while you do it.

This operation is more important than it looks. It is how you handle any variable you cannot observe, which is exactly what latent variables are in §18, and what makes mixture models and variational autoencoders work.

§7 Conditional probability

In 60 seconds

Conditioning is two steps: restrict to the outcomes consistent with what you were told, then renormalise so they sum to 1 again. The denominator in the formula is just the size of the slice you kept. And $P(A \mid B)$ is emphatically not $P(B \mid A)$; confusing them is common enough to have a name, the prosecutor's fallacy.

Now the concept that everything else in the chapter is built from.

Definition: conditional probability

$$P(Y = y \mid X = x) = \frac{P(X = x, Y = y)}{P(X = x)}$$ read "the probability of $Y = y$ given $X = x$", defined whenever $P(X = x) > 0$.

Do not memorise the formula. Understand the two-step operation it performs:

  1. Restrict. Throw away every outcome where $X \neq x$. You have been told those did not happen.
  2. Renormalise. What is left no longer sums to 1, so divide by its total to make it a proper distribution again.

The denominator $P(X = x)$ is exactly that total. It is not a mysterious correction factor; it is the size of the slice you kept.

Pitfall: $P(A \mid B)$ is not $P(B \mid A)$

These are wildly different quantities and confusing them is so common it has a name: the prosecutor's fallacy.

$P(\text{has four legs} \mid \text{is a dog})$ is nearly 1. $P(\text{is a dog} \mid \text{has four legs})$ is not; plenty of four-legged things are cats, tables, or horses.

The medical version costs real lives: the probability of testing positive given that you are sick is not the probability of being sick given that you tested positive. Section 22 works through exactly how far apart these can be, and the answer is startling. Bayes' rule in §21 is precisely the machinery for converting one into the other.

Conditioning is not intervening: $P(Y \mid X = x)$ describes $Y$ among the cases where $X$ happened to equal $x$; it says nothing about what happens if you set $X$ to $x$. The two coincide only under assumptions that can fail. That distinction is the whole subject of Part XXII (from §1), and it is easy to blur here because the notation looks identical.
Try it

From the table in Figure 3, the row for $x_2$ is $(0.08, 0.20, 0.07)$. Compute $P(Y = y_2 \mid X = x_2)$.

Answer. The row sums to $0.08 + 0.20 + 0.07 = 0.35$, which is $P(X = x_2)$. So $P(Y = y_2 \mid X = x_2) = 0.20 / 0.35 \approx 0.571$. Note that the joint probability was only $0.20$, but conditioning raised it to $0.571$: being told you are in row $x_2$ eliminated 65% of the possibility space, and the surviving outcomes absorb that freed-up probability.

§8 The chain rule

Rearranging the definition of conditional probability gives

$$P(x, y) = P(y \mid x)\, P(x)$$

and applying that repeatedly factors any joint distribution into a product of conditionals:

$$P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = P(x_1)\, P(x_2 \mid x_1)\, P(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2) \cdots P(x_n \mid x_1, \dots, x_{n-1})$$

This is the chain rule, and it is exact: no assumptions, no approximation. Every joint distribution factors this way.

Why deep learning cares: this is what a language model is

Apply the chain rule to a sentence, treating each word as a variable:

$P(\text{"the cat sat"}) = P(\text{"the"}) \cdot P(\text{"cat"} \mid \text{"the"}) \cdot P(\text{"sat"} \mid \text{"the cat"})$

A large language model is a machine that estimates exactly these conditional factors. Generating text means sampling one word at a time from $P(\text{next word} \mid \text{everything so far})$, which is the chain rule read left to right. The entire architecture of autoregressive generation is this identity plus a way to approximate the factors. Part XVI §8 builds this properly, and Part XX is a full study of the language-model case.

§9 Independence and conditional independence

Definition: independence

$X$ and $Y$ are independent, written $X \perp Y$, if $$P(x, y) = P(x)\,P(y) \quad \text{for all } x, y$$ Equivalently, $P(y \mid x) = P(y)$: knowing $X$ tells you nothing about $Y$.

Independence is a strong condition. It must hold for every pair of values, not merely on average. Two successive fair coin flips are independent; a person's height and weight emphatically are not.

Definition: conditional independence

$X$ and $Y$ are conditionally independent given $Z$, written $X \perp Y \mid Z$, if $$P(x, y \mid z) = P(x \mid z)\,P(y \mid z)$$ Once you know $Z$, learning $X$ tells you nothing further about $Y$.

This one deserves an example, because it is subtler and more useful than plain independence.

Worked example: ice cream and drowning

Ice cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly correlated across the year. They are not independent: a high ice cream day genuinely predicts more drownings.

But condition on temperature. Among days that are all 30°C, ice cream sales tell you essentially nothing extra about drownings. Both were being driven by the heat. So they are conditionally independent given temperature, while being dependent overall.

This is the structure behind every "correlation is not causation" example. A common cause creates a real statistical dependence between two things that do not influence each other, and conditioning on that cause dissolves it.

Pitfall: neither kind of independence implies the other

Conditional independence does not follow from independence, and independence does not follow from conditional independence. The ice cream example shows the first direction. For the second: two independent coin flips become dependent once you condition on their sum: if the total is 1 and you learn the first was heads, you now know the second was tails with certainty. Conditioning can create dependence as easily as it removes it.

Why deep learning cares

Independence assumptions are what make large models computationally possible. A joint distribution over 100 binary variables has $2^{100}$ entries, more than there are atoms in the observable universe. Assume enough independence and it factors into something you can actually store and compute with.

Naive Bayes assumes all features are conditionally independent given the class, which is almost always false and works surprisingly well anyway. Graphical models, covered in Part XXVIII §3 (directed) and §6 (undirected), are precisely a language for stating which independence assumptions you are making. Every such assumption is a trade: less fidelity, more tractability.
