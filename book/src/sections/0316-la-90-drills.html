PracticeDrills

Thirty-six problems. Answers follow each one immediately; cover them, try first, then look. The gap between "I followed that" and "I can do that" is the entire reason this section exists.

A machine-checkable version of the implementation problems lives in drills/problems/ alongside a test file. Green tests mean you are right; feeling right means nothing.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · How long a matmul takes

Two 1024×1024 matrices, multiplied. Write down the number of floating-point operations, and then how long it should take on hardware doing 1011 of them per second.

Answer. 2n3 = 2.1×109 operations, so about 21 ms. If your measured time is far above that you are memory-bound rather than compute-bound, which is a different problem with a different fix, and knowing which before you optimise saves the afternoon. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up: one concept, five minutes each

WarmW1 · Shapes

$A$ is $6 \times 4$, $B$ is $4 \times 9$, $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^9$. What is the shape of $AB\mathbf{v}$?

Answer. $(6\times4)(4\times9)(9) \to \mathbb{R}^6$. Inner dimensions cancel in pairs, the outer one survives.
WarmW2 · Shapes that fail

$A$ is $3 \times 5$ and $B$ is $3 \times 5$. Which of $AB$, $A^\top B$, $AB^\top$ are defined?

Answer. $AB$: needs $5 = 3$, fails. $A^\top B$: $(5\times3)(3\times5) \to 5\times5$, fine. $AB^\top$: $(3\times5)(5\times3) \to 3\times3$, fine. The last two are both defined and are different sizes, a good reminder that transposes are not decorative.
WarmW3 · Matrix-vector by hand

Compute $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$ using the row view, then check with the column view.

Answer. Rows: $1(5)+2(6) = 17$; $3(5)+4(6) = 39$. So $(17, 39)$. Columns: $5(1,3) + 6(2,4) = (5,15) + (12,24) = (17,39)$. ✓
WarmW4 · Dot versus outer

$\mathbf{u} = (1,2)$, $\mathbf{v} = (3,4)$. Compute $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top$.

Answer. $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v} = 3 + 8 = 11$, a scalar. $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix}$, a $2\times2$ matrix of rank 1.
WarmW5 · Norms

For $\mathbf{x} = (3, -4, 0, 12)$: compute $\|\mathbf{x}\|_1$, $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2$, $\|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty$.

Answer. $L^1 = 3+4+0+12 = 19$. $L^2 = \sqrt{9+16+0+144} = \sqrt{169} = 13$. $L^\infty = 12$.
WarmW6 · Stable softmax

Implement $\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_i = e^{z_i} / \sum_j e^{z_j}$ so that it does not overflow for $\mathbf{z} = (1000, 1001, 1002)$.

Answer. Subtract the maximum before exponentiating: the result is unchanged mathematically because the constant cancels between numerator and denominator, but $e^{0}, e^{1}, e^{2}$ are safe where $e^{1002}$ overflows to infinity. z = z - z.max(); e = np.exp(z); return e / e.sum(). This exact trick reappears in Part III.
WarmW7 · Transpose order

Simplify $(ABC)^\top$.

Answer. $C^\top B^\top A^\top$. Apply the two-factor rule twice; the order fully reverses.
WarmW8 · Rank by inspection

What is the rank of $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 12 \end{bmatrix}$?

Answer. Rank 1. Every column is a multiple of $(1,2,3)$, namely $1\times$, $2\times$, $4\times$. One independent direction. (Every row is also a multiple of $(1,2,4)$, which is no coincidence: row rank always equals column rank.)
WarmW9 · Determinant and invertibility

Is $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ invertible?

Answer. $\det = 3(2) - 6(1) = 0$, so no. Column 1 is three times column 2; the matrix flattens the plane onto a line.
WarmW10 · Orthogonality check

Are $(1, 2, -1)$ and $(3, -1, 1)$ orthogonal?

Answer. $1(3) + 2(-1) + (-1)(1) = 3 - 2 - 1 = 0$. Yes.

Tier 2 Core: the things you must be able to do

CoreC1 · Matrix multiply from scratch

Implement matmul(A, B) with explicit loops, no @, no np.dot. Raise a clear error on shape mismatch. Verify against NumPy on random inputs.

Answer. Triple loop: for each output row $i$ and column $j$, accumulate $\sum_k A[i,k] \cdot B[k,j]$. Check A.shape[1] == B.shape[0] first. Test with np.allclose(matmul(A,B), A @ B) rather than ==; floating point sums differ in the last bits depending on order.
CoreC2 · Linear layer, batched

Given $X \in \mathbb{R}^{32 \times 784}$ (a batch of 32), $W \in \mathbb{R}^{784 \times 10}$, $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$, write the forward pass and give the output shape. Why is $XW$ rather than $WX$?

Answer. H = np.maximum(0, X @ W + b), shape $32 \times 10$. Because the batch convention puts examples in rows, so $(32 \times \underline{784})(\underline{784} \times 10)$ chains correctly. $WX$ would need $10 = 32$ and fails. The bias broadcasts down all 32 rows. Note this transposes the convention from §8, where a single example was a column; both appear in the literature, and you must check which one a given text is using.
CoreC3 · Prove the transpose rule

Show $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$ by comparing entries.

Answer. $[(AB)^\top]_{i,j} = (AB)_{j,i} = \sum_k A_{j,k}B_{k,i}$. And $[B^\top A^\top]_{i,j} = \sum_k (B^\top)_{i,k}(A^\top)_{k,j} = \sum_k B_{k,i}A_{j,k}$. The two sums have identical terms (scalars commute), so the matrices are equal.
CoreC4 · Eigen by hand

Find the eigenvalues and unit eigenvectors of $\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

Answer. $\det(A - \lambda I) = (4-\lambda)^2 - 1 = 0$ gives $\lambda = 5, 3$. For $\lambda = 5$: $(A-5I)\mathbf{v} = 0$ gives $-v_1 + v_2 = 0$, so $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$. For $\lambda = 3$: $v_1 + v_2 = 0$, so $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$. Perpendicular, as the spectral theorem promises.
CoreC5 · Definiteness

Classify $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Answer. First: $(2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = 0$ gives $\lambda = 3, 1$. Both are positive, so positive definite (a bowl). Second: $(1-\lambda)^2 - 4 = 0$ gives $\lambda = 3, -1$, mixed signs, so indefinite (a saddle).
CoreC6 · Implement PCA

Write pca(X, k) returning the projected data, the components, and the explained variance ratio. No sklearn. Then verify that your components are mutually orthogonal.

Answer. Follow §23: centre, form the covariance, call eigh, sort descending, slice the top $k$, project. Check orthogonality with np.allclose(comps.T @ comps, np.eye(k)). If that fails, you almost certainly forgot the sort or used eig instead of eigh.
CoreC7 · Least squares two ways

Fit a line to noisy data using (a) np.linalg.pinv and (b) the normal equations $\mathbf{w} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$. Compare answers, then compare on a deliberately ill-conditioned $X$.

Answer. On well-behaved data they agree to many digits. On ill-conditioned data the normal equations degrade noticeably, because forming $X^\top X$ squares the condition number (§13). This is why real libraries use QR or SVD internally, never the normal equations as written.
CoreC8 · Rank and nullspace

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$: find the rank, and find a non-zero $\mathbf{x}$ with $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.

Answer. Both rows are multiples of $(1,2,3)$, so rank 1. We need $x_1 + 2x_2 + 3x_3 = 0$; for instance $\mathbf{x} = (2,-1,0)$, or $(3,0,-1)$. There is a whole two-dimensional space of such vectors (the nullspace) and its dimension plus the rank equals the number of columns, which is the rank–nullity theorem.
CoreC9 · Gradient clipping

Implement clipping by global norm across a list of gradient arrays of different shapes, as a real optimizer must.

Answer. Compute the global norm $\sqrt{\sum_i \|g_i\|_2^2}$ across all arrays, then if it exceeds the threshold scale every array by the same factor. Scaling each array separately is a different and usually wrong operation; it changes the relative direction of the combined update.
CoreC10 · Find the bug

This is meant to project data onto its top 2 components. It runs without error and gives wrong output. Find both bugs.

w, V = np.linalg.eigh(X.T @ X / len(X))
comps = V[:, :2]
Z = X @ comps
Answer. (1) X is never centred; it should be Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0) everywhere. (2) eigh returns ascending eigenvalues, so V[:, :2] takes the two least informative directions. Sort descending first. Both failures are silent, which is what makes them dangerous.
CoreC11 · Cyclic trace

$A$ is $2\times5$ and $B$ is $5\times2$. Verify numerically that $\tr(AB) = \tr(BA)$ even though $AB$ is $2\times2$ and $BA$ is $5\times5$.

Answer. Both traces equal $\sum_{i,j} A_{i,j}B_{j,i}$, the same set of products, grouped differently. The matrices have different sizes and even different ranks, yet identical traces. Generate random $A$, $B$ and check with np.allclose.
CoreC12 · SVD reconstruction

Take a greyscale image as a matrix. Reconstruct it from the top $k$ singular values for $k \in \{1, 5, 20, 50\}$. Plot error against $k$, and against numbers stored.

Answer. Error falls steeply then flattens. Around $k = 20$–$50$ most images are visually near-identical to the original while storing a fraction of the numbers. Compare against the random-matrix run in §19, where the curve barely moves; the difference between the two is exactly the structure real data has.
CoreC13 · Conditioning experiment

Build $A_\epsilon = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1+\epsilon \end{bmatrix}$ for $\epsilon \in \{10^{-1}, \dots, 10^{-10}\}$. Plot the condition number, and the error in solving against a known answer.

Answer. The condition number grows roughly like $1/\epsilon$ and the solution error tracks it. Once $\text{cond} \approx 10^{16}$ you have exhausted double precision entirely and the answer is meaningless. This is §13's warning made quantitative.
CoreC14 · Orthogonal preserves length

Show that if $Q^\top Q = I$ then $\|Q\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2$.

Answer. $\|Q\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = (Q\mathbf{x})^\top(Q\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^\top Q^\top Q\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^\top I \mathbf{x} = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2$. Three steps, and it is the entire reason orthogonal initialization helps deep networks.
CoreC15 · Projection matrix

For a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$, show that $P = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top$ projects onto $\mathbf{u}$'s direction, and that $P^2 = P$.

Answer. $P\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{x})$. This is the direction $\mathbf{u}$ scaled by how much of $\mathbf{x}$ lies along it. And $P^2 = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}^\top = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top = P$ since $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{u} = 1$. Idempotence is exactly right for a projection: shadows do not move when you project them again.

Tier 3 Stretch: reaches past the chapter

StretchS1 · Power iteration

Find the top eigenvector of a symmetric matrix using only matrix-vector products: start random, repeatedly set $\mathbf{v} \leftarrow A\mathbf{v}/\|A\mathbf{v}\|$. Explain why it converges, and when it fails.

Answer. Write the start vector in the eigenbasis. Each multiplication scales component $i$ by $\lambda_i$, so after $t$ steps the ratio between components $1$ and $2$ has grown by $(\lambda_1/\lambda_2)^t$; the largest eigenvalue takes over exponentially. It fails when $|\lambda_1| = |\lambda_2|$ (no gap to exploit), or if the start vector is exactly orthogonal to the top eigenvector, which random initialization makes vanishingly unlikely.
StretchS2 · Gram–Schmidt

Implement Gram–Schmidt to orthonormalise a set of vectors. Then test it on nearly parallel vectors and observe the numerical breakdown.

Answer. Subtract from each vector its projections onto all previously produced ones, then normalise. On nearly parallel input the subtractions cancel almost completely and the result is dominated by rounding error, and the output vectors come out visibly non-orthogonal. Modified Gram–Schmidt, which subtracts projections one at a time against the updated vector, is markedly more stable, and serious code uses Householder reflections instead.
StretchS3 · Derive the normal equations

Minimise $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2$ by expanding and differentiating, and arrive at $A^\top A\mathbf{x} = A^\top\mathbf{b}$.

Answer. Expand to $\mathbf{x}^\top A^\top A\mathbf{x} - 2\mathbf{b}^\top A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{b}$. The gradient is $2A^\top A\mathbf{x} - 2A^\top\mathbf{b}$; set it to zero. Geometrically the condition reads $A^\top(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{0}$: the residual is orthogonal to every column of $A$, which is precisely the perpendicularity in Figure 9. The algebra and the picture are the same statement.
StretchS4 · Eckart–Young empirically

For a random $20\times20$ matrix, compare the truncated SVD against 1,000 random rank-5 matrices of similar scale. Confirm none beats the SVD.

Answer. None will, and none can; the theorem guarantees it. The point of running it is calibration: notice how far off the random attempts are. Optimality here is not a marginal edge.
StretchS5 · Whitening

Implement a transform making data have zero mean and identity covariance. Show it is not unique.

Answer. Centre, then apply $\Lambda^{-1/2}Q^\top$ using the eigendecomposition of the covariance: rotate onto the principal axes, then divide each by its standard deviation. Not unique because any orthogonal $R$ gives another valid whitening $R\Lambda^{-1/2}Q^\top$: once the covariance is the identity, further rotation leaves it the identity. Choosing $R = Q$ gives ZCA whitening, which stays closest to the original data and is standard for images.
StretchS6 · Why deep linear nets are shallow

Show that a network of stacked linear layers with no nonlinearity is equivalent to a single linear layer. What does this imply?

Answer. $W_3W_2W_1\mathbf{x} = (W_3W_2W_1)\mathbf{x} = W\mathbf{x}$ by associativity; the product is one matrix. So depth without nonlinearity buys no expressive power whatsoever. This is the formal reason ReLU and friends exist, and it is worth carrying into Part VI. (Depth does still change the optimization dynamics of a linear net, which is an active research topic: the functions representable are the same, but the ones gradient descent actually finds are not.)
StretchS7 · Condition number and gradient descent

Run gradient descent on $f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac12\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}$ for positive definite $A$ with condition numbers 1, 10, 100. Plot the trajectories.

Answer. At $\text{cond} = 1$ the level sets are circles and descent goes straight to the minimum in one step. As conditioning worsens the level sets stretch into narrow valleys and the path zigzags, with iteration count growing roughly linearly in the condition number. You have just reproduced the central figure of Part III and the entire motivation for momentum, also in Part III.
StretchS8 · Rank of a product

Prove $\rank(AB) \leq \min(\rank(A), \rank(B))$. Why does this matter for LoRA?

Answer. Every column of $AB$ is a combination of columns of $A$, so the column space of $AB$ sits inside that of $A$, giving $\rank(AB) \leq \rank(A)$. Applying the same argument to rows (or to $(AB)^\top$) gives the $B$ bound. Why it matters: LoRA writes a weight update as $\Delta W = BA$ with a small inner dimension $r$, which by this result forces $\rank(\Delta W) \leq r$. The parameter saving is real, and so is the restriction; the bound is exactly what makes the method both cheap and limited.
StretchS9 · PCA on MNIST

Load MNIST, run your own PCA, and answer: how many components retain 95% of the variance? Visualise the top components as images. Reconstruct digits from 10, 50, 150 components.

Answer. Roughly 150 of 784 components reach 95%, an approximately fivefold reduction. The top components look like broad strokes and blobs: the coarse structure shared across digits. Reconstructions at $k=10$ are blurry but often already legible, which is a striking demonstration of how much redundancy raw pixels carry.
StretchS10 · Random projection

Project 1000-dimensional data to 50 dimensions with a random Gaussian matrix, and measure how much pairwise distances change. Compare against PCA.

Answer. Distances are preserved surprisingly well; this is the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, which says a random projection to $O(\log n / \epsilon^2)$ dimensions preserves all pairwise distances within a factor of $1 \pm \epsilon$, with a target dimension that does not depend on the original one at all. PCA preserves variance better because it is fitted to the data; random projection needs no fitting, costs nothing, and comes with a distance guarantee PCA does not provide. Different tools for different jobs.
