BuildThe project

Fourteen to eighteen hours. You build a perception stack on synthetic scenes where every answer is known exactly (detector, segmenter, stereo, tracker) and measure which of its errors a better model could fix and which are geometry.

P.1 What you are building

seescene: a scene generator with exact ground truth, a small anchor-free detector with matching loss and suppression, a dense head, a calibrated camera model with distortion and triangulation, and a tracker with a Kalman filter and exact assignment.

The rule: numpy only, and every scene synthetic. The point is not to reach a benchmark number; it is that you know the true answer, so every error decomposes into a part the model caused and a part the geometry did.

P.2 Stage 1: the metric before the model

  1. Implement IoU, greedy matching and average precision.
  2. Generate scenes and displace the boxes by a known amount. Sweep the IoU threshold.
  3. Construct two detectors whose ordering reverses with the threshold, from the arithmetic.
  4. Rescale every confidence by a monotone function and confirm AP does not move.

Deliverable: the threshold sweep and the reversal. Step four is the one to remember: the metric you were about to optimise cannot see calibration at all.

P.3 Stage 2: a detector

  1. Build a dense head predicting centre and extent per position.
  2. Count your anchors or positions and compute the imbalance ratio.
  3. Train with plain cross-entropy, then with the focal term, and compare the loss contributed by easy negatives in each.
  4. Add suppression, sweep its threshold, and reproduce the crowding trade.
  5. Replace suppression with a matching loss and re-run the crowded case.

Deliverable: the two loss breakdowns and the crowding comparison. Step three is where the one-stage accuracy gap actually closed, and doing it by hand makes that concrete.

P.4 Stage 3: scale

  1. Compute how many feature pixels each object size occupies at each depth.
  2. Detect from the deepest level only and measure recall against object size.
  3. Add a pyramid and repeat.
  4. Write down the assignment of object size to pyramid level, as a table rather than a hyperparameter.

Deliverable: recall against object size for both, and the assignment table.

P.5 Stage 4: dense prediction

  1. Build a segmentation head and compute mean IoU per class and per pixel.
  2. Measure accuracy restricted to boundary pixels.
  3. Upsample with a transposed convolution whose kernel is not a multiple of the stride, and look at the artefact.
  4. Compute the structure tensor for edge, corner and flat patches, and plot the brightness-constancy residual over candidate velocities for each.

Deliverable: the three metrics and the aperture plot. Step four should produce a valley of exact zeros for the edge; if it produces a shallow minimum instead, your patch has texture in it.

P.6 Stage 5: geometry

  1. Write down your predictions first, in a file, before running anything.
  2. Implement projection, back-projection and intrinsics rescaling. Verify the round trip.
  3. Resize an image without rescaling K and measure how far the projection moves.
  4. Apply radial distortion and plot reprojection error against radius.
  5. Build a stereo pair, triangulate, and measure depth error against range for three baselines. Fit the exponent rather than assuming it.
  6. Compute the range at which the error exceeds a tolerance you choose in advance.
  7. Score your predictions.

Deliverable: the distortion-against-radius curve, the depth-error curve with its fitted exponent, and your usable-range number. That last figure is what a sensor suite is designed around and it takes ten minutes to compute.

P.7 Stage 6: tracking

  1. Implement exact assignment and compare against greedy on crossing objects.
  2. Track by last-seen position through an occlusion of increasing length.
  3. Add a constant-velocity Kalman filter and repeat.
  4. Use the filter's covariance as the association gate rather than a constant, and compare.
  5. Find the gap length at which the motion model also fails, and relate it to object spacing.

Deliverable: misidentification against gap length for all three strategies. Step five's number is not a bug: it is the prediction's uncertainty exceeding the spacing, and it tells you when you need appearance.

P.8 Stage 7: the evaluation

  1. Slice every result by range, object size and occlusion.
  2. Split your sequences by sequence and by frame, and compare.
  3. Check the detector's confidence calibration.
  4. Report the rare-event rate with an interval rather than a point.

Deliverable: the sliced table and the two split results. If the frame split is not noticeably better than the sequence split, your scenes are not correlated enough to be video.

P.9 Stretch

  • Bundle adjustment on a small scene, with the sparsity pattern plotted.
  • Outliers added to it, and a robust loss that survives them.
  • A flat-world homography to a top-down view, with an object of height on it.
  • A real calibration from checkerboard photographs, with the residual against radius.
  • Appearance re-identification for the gaps where the motion model failed.
  • An audit of a perception system you actually run.

P.10 Done when

  • Your IoU returns zero rather than a negative number for disjoint boxes.
  • You have watched AP not move when you rescaled every confidence.
  • You have two detectors that change places when the threshold moves.
  • You know your imbalance ratio and have seen the focal term change which examples dominate.
  • You have recall against object size with and without a pyramid.
  • You have a boundary-restricted segmentation number as well as an aggregate.
  • Your aperture plot shows exact zeros along a line for an edge and one point for a corner.
  • Your projection round trip is exact and your resize bug is measured.
  • Your reprojection error is reported against radius, not averaged.
  • You fitted the stereo depth-error exponent and got 2.
  • You have a usable-range number for a tolerance you chose beforehand.
  • Exact assignment beats greedy on your crossing case.
  • You know the gap length at which your motion model fails and why.
  • Every result is sliced, and you have both split results.
  • You wrote predictions down before Stage 5 and scored them afterwards.
Why this project

Perception is the area of this book where it is easiest to spend a year improving a model against an error the model was never going to fix. Synthetic scenes with exact ground truth make the decomposition unavoidable: a distortion error is not a detector problem, a depth error at 100 m is not a network problem, and an edge's tangential motion is not anybody's problem because the information is absent.

Stage 6 step six is the one to keep. A usable-range number, computed from focal length, baseline and disparity precision before anything is built, decides more about a perception system than any architectural choice in it.

The framework, and what the data cannot supply

Dataset, model family, loss, optimiser. Every previous part has been about choosing those well. This part is the first where the binding constraint is frequently none of them.

A single view does not contain scale. A local window does not contain motion along an edge. A stereo pair's depth precision falls as the square of range whatever reads it. An uncalibrated lens misplaces the image corner by more than any detector's precision. These are properties of the measurement, not of the model, and the first question about a perception error is which kind it is. Getting that wrong is how a year goes into improving a network against an error that a fifteen-line calibration or a wider baseline would have removed.
