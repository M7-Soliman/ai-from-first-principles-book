Part DScale, and what it changes

§20 How much data

The question every project starts with, and it has a shape rather than an answer.

Three regimes, and the diagnostic that tells them apart

Data-limited. More data helps and the model is not the constraint. The learning curve, test error against training-set size, is still falling.

Model-limited. More data does nothing; the curve has flattened above the noise floor. You need a different model, not more examples.

At the floor. Both errors sit at the irreducible error of Part V §20. You are done, and no technique in this book will move it.

Part VI §6's learning curves distinguish these for about an hour's work: train on 10%, 25%, 50%, 100% of what you have and plot. It is the cheapest planning instrument in the book and it is routinely skipped in favour of guessing.

Why the curve's shape tells you what to buy

Empirically, test error against dataset size is close to a power law over several orders of magnitude, a straight line on log–log axes. That is not a theorem and it holds well enough to plan with.

The consequence is the one to internalise: on a power law, each fixed improvement costs a fixed multiple of the data. Halving the error might take ten times the corpus. So the question is never "should we get more data" but "what does the slope say the next halving costs, and is that cheaper than the alternatives?".

Fit the slope from the learning curve you already have. It converts an argument into an estimate, and Part XVII does the same thing for compute.

§21 What deduplication does to training

§12 said duplicates reweight the training distribution. At scale the effect is large enough to change conclusions.

Why removing data can improve a model

It sounds wrong: throwing away training examples should hurt. Three reasons it does not.

The distribution was not the one you chose. A document appearing a thousand times contributes a thousand times the gradient. You are training on a corpus reweighted by how often things happen to be mirrored, which is a property of web infrastructure rather than of language.

Duplicates cause memorisation. §33's figure measures the threshold, and it is low: a handful of copies is enough.

Duplicates cause contamination. A benchmark item mirrored across the corpus reaches your training set through a route no decontamination pass anticipated.

The reported effect of aggressive deduplication on large corpora is consistent: equal or better performance from fewer tokens, with substantially less memorisation. It is one of the few interventions that improves several things at once, which is why it is now standard.

§22 Quality against quantity

Given a fixed budget, is it better to train on more data or on better data? The honest answer depends on which regime §20 put you in, and the useful framing is a frontier rather than a choice.

The curation frontier

Filtering trades quantity for quality at a rate set by the filter. Keeping the top 10% of a corpus by some quality score gives you a tenth of the tokens and, you hope, a better tenth.

Whether that trade is worth taking depends on the slope of both curves, and it is measurable: train on the full corpus and on the filtered subset at matched compute, and compare. This is a day's work and it is done far less often than the filter is applied.

Pitfall: quality is defined by the filter, and the filter has opinions

§17's warning, restated where it costs the most. "Quality" in a filtering pipeline means "scores high under our classifier", and the classifier learned "resembles our reference corpus".

So an aggressive quality filter narrows the frame (§2) in exactly the way §3's checklist warns about, while the aggregate metrics improve, because the evaluation sets resemble the reference corpus too. You have improved a number by making the training data more like the test data, which is contamination's respectable cousin.

The check: evaluate on something the filter's reference does not resemble, and see whether the improvement survives.

§23 Repeating data

The unusual regime that large-scale language modelling created: for the first time, teams ran out of data before they ran out of compute.

What repetition is worth

A second pass over the same corpus is not worthless and not equal to fresh data. The reported picture is consistent: the first few repetitions are worth nearly as much as new tokens, and the value decays after that, with returns approaching zero somewhere around four to sixteen passes depending on scale.

Past that, repetition actively hurts: the model begins memorising rather than generalising, which is §33's mechanism.

Why this is now a strategic question rather than a tuning one

Part XVII's scaling laws relate performance to parameters and tokens. They assume tokens are available. Once the supply of high-quality text is a binding constraint, the laws stop describing what you can do and start describing what you cannot.

Three responses exist, and this part covers two of them. Use the data you have more times (this section, with limits). Make more data (§27, with limits). Or change what counts as data: other modalities, other languages, other sources. That last one is where the frame of §2 stops being an abstraction and becomes a procurement decision.

§24 Ordering

Part IX §28 covered curriculum learning and found the honest result: only the stochastic form reliably helps, and most implementations do nothing. Two data-specific points remain.

Ordering matters most at the end. A model's final state is influenced disproportionately by what it saw last, which is why fine-tuning works at all and why the last phase of a pretraining run is often deliberately weighted toward higher-quality data. That is a curriculum, though it is rarely called one.

Shuffling is a correctness requirement, not an optimisation. If your data arrives sorted by class, by source, or by date, then a minibatch is not a sample from your training distribution; it is a sample from one region of it. Part IX §5's unbiasedness argument fails, and the model oscillates. Shuffle once, properly, and verify it.

§25 Splitting, when the data is not i.i.d.

A random split assumes records are independent. §11's group leakage said they often are not. Here is the general procedure.

Split along the axis you will generalise across

Ask: what will be different in deployment? Split on that.

  • New users at deployment → split by user, so no user appears on both sides.
  • New time periods → split by time, training strictly before testing.
  • New sites, hospitals, cameras, sources → split by site.
  • All of them → split by all of them, and accept the smaller test set.

A random split answers "how well does this work on more of the same?", which is almost never the question. It reliably reports a better number than the correct split does, and that gap is a measurement of how much your model depends on the thing you failed to split on.

Worked: the gap between two splits is the informative quantity

Take a medical imaging dataset with several scans per patient from a handful of hospitals.

Random split: 0.94. Some of the same patient's scans are on both sides.

Split by patient: 0.89. The model was partly recognising patients.

Split by hospital: 0.71. Most of what remained was recognising the site: scanner, protocol, population.

The three numbers are all correct measurements of different questions. Only the third estimates deployment at a new hospital, and the drop from 0.94 to 0.71 is the finding: it quantifies exactly how much of the model is shortcut (§8).

Report all three. A single number invites the reader to assume the most favourable interpretation, and the spread is more informative than any one of them.
