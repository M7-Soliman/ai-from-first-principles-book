Part DMeasuring harm

Part XIV built the discipline of measuring whether a model works. This movement is what changes when the question is whether it is safe, and almost everything changes: the quantity of interest is a tail rather than an average, the distribution is chosen by an adversary rather than by you, and a clean result carries far less information than it appears to.

§25 Why this is not ordinary evaluation

Four structural differences

The statistic is a tail, not a mean. Accuracy averages over the distribution. Harm is about the worst outputs, whose frequency is low and whose cost is high, and averages are the wrong summary for both reasons.

The distribution is adversarial. Part XIV's held-out set is drawn from the same process as training. A red-teamer draws from wherever the model is weakest, which is a distribution defined by your failures.

The result is asymmetric. Finding a failure proves the failure exists. Not finding one proves very little, and §27 quantifies exactly how little.

The measurement is a lower bound on capability, not an estimate. Your elicitation may simply have been poor. §31 is this point, and it inverts the usual reading of a negative result.

§26 Red-teaming

What it is, and what it produces

Red-teaming is adversarial search for inputs that produce unacceptable outputs. It produces existence proofs (this input, this output, this is bad) and existence proofs are the only strong result available in this movement.

What it does not produce is a rate. The inputs were chosen adversarially, so the fraction of successful attacks in a red-teaming exercise measures the team's effort and skill, not the system's failure probability under any real distribution.

The three modes, and what each is good for

Human. Expensive, slow, and the only mode that finds failure categories nobody anticipated. Irreplaceable for exactly that reason.

Automated. A model generates candidate attacks against a target. Cheap and enormously scalable, and bounded by the attacker model's own imagination: it finds many instances of the kinds of attack it knows.

Optimisation-based. Gradient or search directly against a differentiable objective, as in Part VIII §20's adversarial examples. Produces the strongest attacks and the least natural ones, which is the right trade when you want an upper bound on robustness.

They find different things and the coverage argument requires all three. Reporting one and concluding the system is robust is the standard error.

Pitfall: patching the instances found

Red-teaming yields specific attacks. The tempting response is to train against exactly those, which reliably fixes them and reliably fails to generalise: you have removed the samples, not the vulnerability, and the next search finds neighbours.

The measurement that tells you which happened is a fresh red-team after the fix, run by people who did not see the first one. If the new attack rate is unchanged, you patched instances. This is Part XIV's train/test discipline, in the one place where violating it is easiest to rationalise.

§27 The statistics of rare failures

What this does to the sentence "we evaluated it and it was fine"

A thorough evaluation might run a thousand adversarial prompts and find nothing. Figure 5 says the failure rate could still be 0.3%. At a million uses that is three thousand failures, and at a hundred million it is three hundred thousand.

The gap between "we found nothing" and "it does not happen" is therefore not rhetorical caution. It is a specific number, it is computable before you run anything, and it is usually several orders of magnitude.

Worked: sizing an evaluation before running it

You need confidence that a harmful-output rate is below 1 in 10,000, at 95%.

By the rule of three, zero failures in $n$ trials bounds the rate at about $3/n$. Setting $3/n = 10^{-4}$ gives $n = 30{,}000$. Exactly: $1 - 0.05^{1/n} \le 10^{-4}$ needs $n \ge 29{,}956$.

So: thirty thousand evaluations, all clean, to support a claim about one in ten thousand. If each needs human review at one minute, that is five hundred hours. This calculation is the one that should happen before the evaluation is designed, because it frequently shows the intended claim is unaffordable, at which point the honest move is to weaken the claim, not to run a smaller study and state it anyway.

Pitfall: the trials are not independent

The arithmetic above assumes independent draws. Evaluation suites are correlated: prompts share templates, topics, and sources. Correlated trials carry less information than independent ones, so the true bound is weaker than Figure 5's, which is already weak.

Treat the figure as the optimistic case. That is an unusual direction for a pitfall box and it is the right one here.

§28 Dangerous capability evaluations

A different question from alignment

These ask what a system can do if someone tries to make it, not what it does by default. Uplift for weapons development, autonomous replication, cyber-offence, large-scale persuasion, and evading oversight are the standard categories.

They are the technical input to §36's release decision and to most regulatory thresholds, which makes their methodology unusually consequential relative to how new it is.

Why the marginal question is the only one that matters

"Can the model produce information about $X$" is nearly always yes for anything in the literature, and nearly always uninformative. The decision-relevant quantity is the uplift: how much does access change what a specific actor could accomplish, compared with a library, a search engine, and the open models that already exist?

That is a difference between two capabilities, so it requires a control group, which makes it a controlled experiment on human performance, not a benchmark. The good studies do this. The headline numbers usually quoted do not, and the two are routinely conflated.

§29 Bias and fairness, as measurement

Go and read

Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016), Inherent trade-offs in the fair determination of risk scores. The impossibility theorem behind this section. Short, entirely elementary, and it converts an interminable argument into a choice that has to be made and defended.

Part XIII covered where dataset bias comes from. The question here is narrower and more technical: given a trained system, what can be measured, and what do the measurements mean when they conflict?

Three criteria that cannot all hold

Demographic parity. The positive rate is equal across groups.

Equalised odds. True positive and false positive rates are equal across groups.

Calibration within groups. A predicted probability of 0.7 means 0.7 in every group.

These are mutually incompatible whenever base rates differ, and the incompatibility is a theorem, not a limitation of current methods. So "make the model fair" has no technical referent until somebody chooses which criterion, and that choice is about what the decision is for.

The consequence, stated plainly

Because the criteria conflict, a fairness result is only interpretable alongside the criterion it used, and a system can be reported as fair and unfair from the same data without anybody lying. Read every such claim by looking first at which measurement was chosen and why.

Part XIV §5's calibration is one of the three, which means the calibration you were told to want has a cost measured in one of the others. That is uncomfortable and it is the actual situation.

All three criteria above are statistical, functions of the joint distribution of prediction, label and group. A fourth kind, counterfactual fairness, instead asks whether the decision would change in a world where only the protected attribute differed, which needs the intervention machinery developed in Part XXII §37.

§30 Evaluation validity

The question behind every number in this movement

Validity is whether the instrument measures the construct it claims to. Part XX §41 showed benchmarks decaying through contamination and overfitting; this is the prior question, and it does not require any decay to bite.

A refusal-rate benchmark measures refusals. Whether that is safety depends on whether the refused set resembles what users actually attempt, whether refusing is the desired behaviour, and whether the model refuses for the reason you think. Each of those is a separate empirical claim, and none is established by the benchmark score.

Pitfall: the construct drifts to fit the instrument

Once a benchmark exists, "safe" quietly becomes "scores well on the benchmark", because that is what can be optimised and reported. This is §18 operating on the research community rather than on a model, and it has the same signature: the measured quantity improves steadily while the thing it stood for does not.

The defence is the one from §19: at least one measurement that does not route through the optimised proxy. Fresh human evaluation on inputs collected after the benchmark was built is the usual form.

§31 Elicitation, and the lower bound problem

Why a negative capability result is weak

If a model fails a task, there are two explanations: it cannot do the task, or you did not ask well. Part XX §22 measured how much prompt phrasing alone moves results, and the answer was large. Add fine-tuning, scaffolding, tools and retries and the gap between a naive attempt and a serious one is larger still.

So every capability evaluation reports a lower bound. "The model cannot do $X$" is never established; only "we did not get it to do $X$ with the effort we spent" is.

Which makes the asymmetry complete

Put §27 and this section together. A positive safety-relevant capability result is strong: it happened, so it is possible. A negative one is weak twice over: your elicitation was a lower bound, and your sample size bounds the rate only loosely.

This is why serious evaluation reports state the elicitation effort explicitly (how many prompts, what scaffolding, whether fine-tuning was attempted) and why a report that omits it cannot be interpreted, whatever its numbers say.

§32 Safety cases

Borrowing from engineering disciplines that had to solve this

A safety case is a structured argument that a system is acceptably safe in a stated context, with the evidence for each step and the assumptions made explicit. Aviation, nuclear power and medical devices all use them, for the same reason that applies here: no single test establishes safety, so the object of review has to be the argument.

Its virtue is that it forces the weak step into the open. A safety case whose argument runs "we red-teamed and found nothing" now has to state the sample size, at which point §27 applies visibly rather than silently.

The shape of one, for a deployed model

Claim. Harm of type $H$ occurs at a rate below $r$ in deployment context $C$.

Evidence. Evaluation with stated sample size and confidence (§27), red-teaming across all three modes with stated effort (§26, §31), the monitoring that will run in deployment (§35).

Assumptions. That the evaluation distribution resembles $C$; that elicitation was adequate; that the monitoring catches what the evaluation missed; that $C$ does not drift.

Defeaters. What observation would falsify the claim, and what happens when one arrives. A case without this section is a summary rather than an argument.

§33 What movement D establishes

Three things

Evidence about harm is fundamentally asymmetric. Finding a failure settles the question; not finding one leaves the rate bounded only by Figure 5's arithmetic, and 1,021 clean tests permit 0.293%.

Negative results are lower bounds twice over: once from sample size, once from elicitation. Both bounds are usually left unstated, and a report that omits either cannot be interpreted.

The unit of review is the argument, not the number. Which is why §32 exists, why it comes from disciplines that had to get this right, and why the assumptions section is the part that does the work.
