BuildThe project

Twelve to sixteen hours. You build a transformer from the formula, verify the one thing that silently ruins it, make generation fast, and then find a circuit inside a model small enough to understand completely.

P.1 What you are building

attention-from-scratch: scaled dot-product attention, multi-head, a working decoder-only model, a KV cache proved exact, and an induction-head hunt with an ablation.

The rule: every optimisation must be shown to compute the same function. The KV cache, the online softmax and the tiled attention are all exact, and a project that cannot demonstrate that has demonstrated nothing.

P.2 Stage 1: the operation

  1. Scaled dot-product attention, from §4's formula, in NumPy.
  2. Verify against a library implementation, masked and unmasked.
  3. Measure $\text{Var}[\mathbf{q}^\top\mathbf{k}]$ against $d$ and confirm it is $d$. Then measure attention entropy with and without the scaling.
  4. Multi-head, and confirm the parameter count does not change with the head count.

Deliverable: Figure 1 from your own code, and the entropy collapse demonstrated at $d = 2048$ rather than asserted.

P.3 Stage 2: position

  1. Verify permutation equivariance numerically, then break it with a learned encoding and with sinusoids.
  2. Implement RoPE. Verify $\mathbf{q}_m\cdot\mathbf{k}_n$ depends only on $m-n$, at absolute positions differing by thousands.
  3. Train the same small model with each of the three and compare, including at a length longer than trained.

Deliverable: Figures 3 and 4, and the extrapolation comparison. The learned encoding should fail past its table; the others should degrade rather than fail.

P.4 Stage 3: the model, and the one test that matters

  1. Assemble the block of §14: pre-norm, residual, multi-head attention, feedforward.
  2. Stack it into a decoder-only model with causal masking.
  3. Verify the mask by consequence. Change the token at position $j$; confirm every output at position $< j$ is bit-identical. Do not inspect the mask; test it.
  4. Train on a task you generate, and confirm the loss cannot fall below the entropy of the data.

Deliverable: the causality test passing, in code, as an assertion that runs every time. Then deliberately introduce the off-by-one and confirm the test catches it and the loss curve does not.

P.5 Stage 4: fast, and provably identical

  1. Implement generation without a cache. Then with one.
  2. Assert the outputs are identical before measuring any speedup.
  3. Measure the cache's memory growth against context length, and time both.
  4. Implement the online softmax of §29 and confirm it matches the one-shot version. Then use it to compute attention in tiles without ever forming the $n\times n$ matrix, and measure the peak memory of both.

Deliverable: a tiled attention that never materialises the score matrix, agreeing with the naive version to rounding, with both memory profiles measured. This is FlashAttention's mathematics; the remaining engineering is which memory the tiles live in.

P.6 Stage 5: find a circuit

The centrepiece, and the part that will change how you read interpretability work.

  1. Train a two-layer, two-head model on a repeated block at a random offset.
  2. Run the control: the same models on a fixed offset. Confirm one layer nearly matches two there, and does not on random offsets.
  3. Locate the previous-token head in layer 1 and the induction head in layer 2. Quantify the induction pattern against uniform attention over the same prefix.
  4. Ablate. Zero the layer-1 head and measure what happens to the layer-2 pattern and to the loss.

Deliverable: the circuit named, measured, and broken. The ablation is what turns a correlation into a causal claim, and skipping it is the most common weakness in this kind of work.

P.7 Stretch

  • In-context learning. Train on many fresh random rules; test on rules never seen; bucket accuracy by demonstrations of that specific query. The conditioning is the exercise.
  • Grouped-query attention. Implement it, measure the cache saving and the quality cost, and find the $g$ where the trade turns.
  • Linear attention. Implement the reassociated form, confirm linear cost, then write down explicitly what its fixed-size state is, and notice you have built a recurrent network.
  • Make it spike. Push the learning rate until training breaks, with the maximum attention logit and per-head entropy logged. Establish that the logit moves first.
  • Lost in the middle. Plant a fact at controlled depth and sweep. Plot retrieval against position.

P.8 Done when

  • Your attention matches a library implementation, masked and unmasked.
  • The $\sqrt{d}$ is derived from a measured variance, not quoted.
  • RoPE's relative property is verified at positions thousands apart.
  • The causality test runs as an assertion, and you have watched it catch a deliberate bug.
  • The KV cache is proved identical before any speedup is reported.
  • Tiled attention agrees with naive attention and never forms the $n\times n$ matrix.
  • An induction head is located, quantified, and ablated, with the fixed-offset control run.
  • Your README names one thing about transformers you believed that the measurements contradicted.
Why this project

Two stages justify it. Stage 3, because the causal mask is the correctness boundary of the entire architecture and its failure mode is a training curve that looks better than a correct one. Everyone should have watched that happen once, deliberately, in code they wrote.

And Stage 5, because it is the smallest complete example of the thing the field most wants and least often achieves: a mechanism identified, predicted from structure, measured, and then broken to prove the claim. Attention maps are easy to plot and hard to trust (§36), and the ablation is where the difference lives.

The one thing to carry forward

Attention buys a constant path length between any two positions, and pays for it with a state that grows without bound.

The quadratic cost, the KV cache, FlashAttention, grouped-query attention, every sparse and linear approximation, and every limit on context length is a consequence of that one trade. Part XI made the opposite choice and paid in path length instead.

Neither is correct. They are two settings of one dial, and attention won because parallel training mattered more than either, which is a fact about hardware rather than about sequences.

Which sets up the next question. If what limits a model is how much data you can push through it, and that limit has just been lifted by orders of magnitude, what happens when you push a great deal? Part XVII is the answer, and it turns out to have laws.
