Part EArchitectures, and what each one fixed

Seven designs, each of which changed one thing. Read as a list of names: they are trivia; read as a sequence of problems and responses: they are the clearest available account of what actually limits a deep network, and three of the answers are things this book has already derived for other reasons.

§20 LeNet to AlexNet

The convolutional network was essentially complete in 1989. What happened in 2012 was not an idea.

History: twenty-three years of waiting

The 1980 Neocognitron already had local receptive fields, parameter sharing across positions, and alternating selective and invariant layers. What it did not have was backpropagation; it was trained by layerwise unsupervised clustering.

In 1988 backpropagation was applied to time-delay neural networks, which in modern terms are one-dimensional convolutional networks over time. In 1989 the same training algorithm was applied to two-dimensional convolution over images, and the modern convolutional network existed. By the late 1990s one such system was reading more than 10% of all cheques in the United States.

Then very little, for a decade. In 2012 AlexNet won ImageNet and the commercial interest in deep learning began.

The difference was not architectural. AlexNet is LeNet, larger, with three changes that this book has already covered: rectifiers instead of saturating units (Part VII §7), dropout (Part VIII §19), and two GPUs. Plus a dataset three orders of magnitude larger than anything LeNet was trained on.

Goodfellow's own assessment of why convolutional networks succeeded when general backpropagation networks were thought to have failed is worth quoting the substance of: it may simply be that they were cheap enough to experiment with, so people tuned them properly; and that the barriers may have been psychological, since practitioners who did not expect neural networks to work did not make a serious effort to make them work. That is an uncomfortable hypothesis and it has not been refuted.

One AlexNet detail is worth keeping because §19 already used it. Its convolutions were grouped (the channels split across two GPUs with limited communication) for reasons of memory rather than modelling. Grouping was rediscovered a decade later as a deliberate design axis, and depthwise separable convolution is its limit. A workaround became a technique, which happens more often than the literature admits.

§21 VGG, and the argument for small kernels

Go and read

Simonyan & Zisserman (2015), Very deep convolutional networks for large-scale image recognition. One idea, stated in a paragraph and then applied with total consistency: only $3\times3$ kernels, stacked. Read §2.3 for the receptive-field argument worked in full. Then Szegedy et al. (2015) on Inception, which asked why you should have to pick a kernel size at all, and which introduced the $1\times1$ reduction that §19 generalises.

VGG's contribution is a single observation, applied uniformly: use only $3\times3$ kernels, and stack them.

Worked: why two $3\times3$ layers beat one $5\times5$

Stack two $3\times3$ convolutions. §10's arithmetic gives a receptive field of $1 + 2\times2 = 5$ pixels, the same as a single $5\times5$ convolution. Now count.

One $5\times5$ layer, $C$ channels in and out: $25C^2$ parameters.
Two $3\times3$ layers: $2 \times 9C^2 = 18C^2$ parameters.

28% fewer, for the same reach. And three $3\times3$ layers reach $7\times7$ with $27C^2$ against $49C^2$: 45% fewer.

The parameter count is the smaller half of the argument. The stack also has two nonlinearities where the single layer had one, so it can express strictly more functions with strictly fewer parameters. That is an unusually clean win, and it is why $3\times3$ became and remains the default kernel size.

Pitfall: the receptive fields are the same and the models are not

The equivalence above is about the theoretical receptive field, and §10 measured the gap between that and the effective one.

A single $5\times5$ kernel weights all 25 positions freely. Two stacked $3\times3$ layers reach the same 25 positions but with a strong bias toward the centre, because the corner pixels are reachable by one path and the middle by many. So the stack is not a reparameterisation of the wide kernel; it is a differently constrained operator that happens to share a support.

Usually that bias is helpful, which is why the substitution works. It is not free, and it is part of why very deep stacks of small kernels have the reach problem §10 measured.

§22 Inception, and the cost of width

VGG chose one kernel size everywhere. Inception asked why you should have to choose, and applied several in parallel ($1\times1$, $3\times3$, $5\times5$ and a pooling path), then concatenated the results along the channel axis, letting the network weight them.

That is expensive, and the fix is the operation §19 already identified. Put a $1\times1$ convolution before each expensive branch to reduce the channel count first. A $5\times5$ convolution from 256 channels to 64 costs $64 \times 256 \times 25 = 409{,}600$ parameters; reduce to 32 channels with a $1\times1$ first and it is $32\times256 + 64\times32\times25 = 59{,}392$: a factor of seven, for an operation with a one-pixel receptive field.

Inception also introduced auxiliary heads, which Part IX §27 already derived: extra classifiers attached to intermediate layers, trained on the same task and discarded afterwards, so lower layers receive a large gradient directly rather than through the whole stack. They were a response to a problem the next section solves properly.

§23 ResNet

In 60 seconds

Deep plain networks were observed to be worse on the training set than shallower ones, which rules out overfitting and points at optimisation. Adding the input of a block to its output fixed it. This book derived the mechanism two parts ago, for a different reason, and the derivation is the same one.

The observation that motivated ResNet is the one to hold on to, and it is easy to state badly. It was not that deeper networks generalised worse. It was that a 56-layer plain network had higher training error than a 20-layer one.

Why that specific number settles the question

Higher test error would have several explanations, overfitting foremost. Higher training error rules almost all of them out.

The deeper network can express everything the shallower one can: set the extra layers to the identity and you have the shallow network exactly. So the function is in the search space, and gradient descent failed to find it. This is Part IX §12's difficulty, stated as an experiment: the model could represent it and the optimiser could not find it.

Part IX §26's FitNets result is the same reasoning applied to thin networks, and it is worth noticing that both of the strongest architectural results of the decade were established by reporting a number almost nobody reports.

The residual block

$$\mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x}$$

Rather than asking the block to compute the map you want, ask it to compute the difference between that map and the identity. If the identity is a good answer, the block only has to learn to output zero, which is what an initialised-near-zero layer already does.

Part IX §27 gave the optimisation reading directly: a skip connection shortens the path from a lower layer's parameters to the output, so a gradient that would have been multiplied by twenty Jacobians has a route where it is multiplied by two. Part IX §10's drift has that much less distance to act over. Nothing new is needed here; the mechanism was derived when we asked how to make models optimisable, before there was any reason to be discussing images.

Pitfall: three separate lessons in one figure, and the popular account has one

Plain networks really do degrade, and completely. Not "generalise worse": a 32-layer plain network without normalisation sits at chance on its own training set.

Residual connections alone are not enough. The right-hand arm of the left panel diverges past depth 24. Each block adds to the signal, so the variance of the residual stream grows with depth and eventually overflows. This is exactly why the ResNet block has normalisation inside it, and it is a fact you can only see by trying the combination nobody ships.

Normalisation alone gets you most of the way. With batch norm, the plain network trains fine to depth 32. At this scale it is normalisation doing the heavy lifting, and the residual connection's contribution is that it keeps improving with depth where the plain network plateaus. The original degradation appears at 56 layers on a much harder task than fits in a figure here, and the honest summary is that the two mechanisms overlap substantially rather than that one of them is the answer.

§24 Normalisation, and where it goes in an image

Part IX §23 derived batch normalisation and its two modes. Two things are specific to convolutional networks.

The statistics are shared across space. A feature map's mean and standard deviation are computed over the batch and over every spatial location, giving one pair of numbers per channel rather than per channel per pixel. Part IX §23 stated this as a rule; §8 of this part is the reason. The kernel is shared across positions, so a feature detected at the top left is the same feature as one detected at the bottom right, and normalising them separately would break the equivariance the architecture was built to have.

The normalisation family, by what they average over

An activation tensor has axes $(N, C, H, W)$. Every normalisation scheme is a choice of which axes to compute statistics over.

SchemeAverages overDepends on batch size?
Batch norm$N, H, W$: one pair per channelYes
Layer norm$C, H, W$: one pair per exampleNo
Instance norm$H, W$: one pair per example per channelNo
Group norm$H, W$ and a group of channelsNo

Group norm interpolates: one group is layer norm, one channel per group is instance norm.

Why anything other than batch norm exists

Batch norm estimates its statistics from the batch, so by Part V §3 the error in those estimates falls like $1/\sqrt{N}$. At $N=32$ that is fine. At $N=2$ (which is what you get when the images are large, as in segmentation and detection) the normalisation is substantially noise, and it degrades.

It also creates a dependence between examples that should be independent, which is awkward for anything sequential and outright wrong for some generative models.

Every alternative in the table removes the batch axis from the average, which removes the problem. The cost is that they lose the mild regularising effect of batch norm's noise, and on large-batch classification batch norm is still usually best. This is why vision architectures kept batch norm and transformers use layer norm; different batch regimes, not different beliefs.

§25 DenseNet, and scaling three ways at once

ResNet adds a block's input to its output. DenseNet concatenates instead, and does it with every earlier layer in a block rather than just the previous one. Each layer sees all preceding feature maps, which strengthens the gradient path further and encourages feature reuse; a layer need not re-derive something an earlier layer already computed. It is more parameter-efficient than ResNet and less memory-efficient, because nothing can be discarded.

EfficientNet asks a different question. A network has three size knobs (depth, width and input resolution), and the usual practice was to turn one. The observation is that they are coupled: a higher-resolution input needs more depth to reach across it (§10) and more width to represent the extra detail. So scale all three together by a fixed ratio, determined once by a small search and then applied at every size.

Why compound scaling is the shape of a later argument

Two things about EfficientNet's method matter more than the architecture it produced.

It treats the scaling rule as the object of study. Not "here is a better model" but "here is how to build a family of models at any budget". That reframing (from architecture to scaling law) is what Part XVII is about, and this is its first appearance.

The search was done once, at small scale, and extrapolated. That is only legitimate if the optimal ratio does not depend on scale, which was an assumption, and which was later found to be approximately but not exactly true. Assumptions of that form recur throughout the scaling literature and are the most common place its conclusions turn out to need revising.

§26 ConvNeXt, and the experiment that should have been done first

Go and read

Liu et al. (2022), A ConvNet for the 2020s. The method is the contribution. A ResNet is modernised one change at a time, each measured, until it matches transformers, and the reader can see exactly how much came from the training recipe and how much from architecture. Read it as a model of how an ablation should be reported, and then reread the papers whose claims it deflates.

By 2020 vision transformers were outperforming convolutional networks and the conclusion was widely drawn that attention was the reason. ConvNeXt tested that conclusion, and the method is the interesting part.

Start with a standard ResNet. Change nothing about the convolution. Adopt, one at a time, each of the training practices that had arrived with transformers: the AdamW optimiser of Part IX §18, a long cosine schedule with warmup, heavy augmentation, stochastic depth, label smoothing; and then each of the architectural details that were not really about attention: fewer and wider blocks, larger kernels, layer norm rather than batch norm, GELU rather than ReLU, an inverted bottleneck.

The result was a purely convolutional network matching transformers on the benchmarks where they had been said to win. A large share of the reported gap was the training recipe.

Why this is the most useful result in the section

Part XIV will make this a discipline; here it is a warning.

Two systems were compared, differing in architecture and optimiser and schedule and augmentation and regularisation, and a single aggregate number was attributed to the first of those. Part IX §29's ladder exists because that reasoning is wrong for a single training run; ConvNeXt shows it is equally wrong for a whole subfield, for several years.

The honest 2026 position is narrower than either side claimed. Convolution and attention have different inductive biases; convolution's is better when data is limited, attention's scales better when data is not; the architectures have converged considerably, with convolutional networks adopting transformer training recipes and transformers adopting convolutional hierarchy and locality. Most of what was attributed to the architecture was not the architecture, and §32 returns to what genuinely was.

Try it

You replace a network's $5\times5$ convolutions with pairs of $3\times3$ convolutions, as §21 recommends, and accuracy falls. Give three explanations consistent with that, and the measurement that distinguishes them.

Answer. One: the effective receptive field shrank. §21's equivalence is about the theoretical field; §10 showed the effective field of a stack is centre-weighted and smaller. If your task needs the corners of that $5\times5$ region, you have lost them. Measure: the gradient-based receptive field of Figure 4, before and after.

Two: the network got deeper and the optimisation got harder. You added a layer for every substitution. If the model has no residual connections or normalisation, §23's degradation is a live explanation. Measure: the training loss. If it rose, this is optimisation, not capacity, which is exactly ResNet's own diagnostic.

Three: the regularisation total changed. Fewer parameters is less capacity, and Part VIII §22's warning applies: if the model was already near the underfitting boundary, removing 28% of its parameters pushes it over. Measure: the train–test gap. If it narrowed while both errors rose, you have over-regularised.

The three are distinguished by two numbers you already log, and note that only the first is about convolution at all. That is the general shape of §26's lesson.
