Part ECausality and machine learning

The previous movements are the statistics of causation. This one is what changes when the models are the ones this book has been building: what a policy is worth before you deploy it, how effects vary across units, why a predictor that uses a cause survives a change of environment when one that uses an effect does not, and what Part XXI's "causal" claims are and are not.

§32 Evaluating a policy you have not run

Off-policy evaluation

You have logs from a system that acted under policy $\pi_0$ and a proposed $\pi_1$. What would $\pi_1$ have earned?

If the logging policy's action probabilities were recorded, this is inverse-probability weighting with the propensity known rather than estimated:

$$\hat{V}(\pi_1) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \frac{\pi_1(a_i \mid x_i)}{\pi_0(a_i \mid x_i)} \, r_i$$

Knowing the propensity exactly is a large advantage over movement C's situation, and it is free: it requires only that the system logs the probability it acted with. Systems that log the chosen action but not its probability throw that away, and it cannot be reconstructed afterwards.

The two failures, and both are positivity

Support. If $\pi_1$ would take actions $\pi_0$ never took, no reweighting recovers their value. Figure 3's overlap problem, in the place it does the most commercial damage.

Variance. Where the ratio is large the estimate is dominated by a handful of logged events. The standard remedies, clipping and self-normalising, are Figure 4's right panel: they trade the variance for a bias.

This is also the honest frame for Part XVIII §40's offline reinforcement learning. The conservatism those methods impose is not a heuristic; it is the recognition that value estimates outside the logged support are unidentified.

§33 The average conceals the decision

Why the average effect is often the wrong target

A treatment with an average effect of zero can help half the population and harm the other half. If you can tell which is which, the policy of treating only the first group has a large positive value, and the trial that reported zero would have led you to abandon it.

The quantity that supports a decision is the conditional average treatment effect, $\tau(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y_1 - Y_0 \mid X = x]$, and estimating it is a machine-learning problem, with the twist that the target is never observed for any individual, because no unit is both treated and untreated.

Meta-learners: turning it into supervised problems

S-learner. One model on $(X, T)$; predict both ways and difference. Simple, and it can shrink the treatment signal to nothing when the model has many covariates and regularises $T$ away.

T-learner. Separate models per arm, differenced. No shrinkage problem; wasteful when the arms share structure, and noisy in the smaller arm.

X-learner. Fit per arm, impute each unit's missing potential outcome, regress the imputed effect on covariates, combine with propensity weights. Better when the arms are unbalanced.

Causal forests. Trees split to maximise heterogeneity in the effect rather than in the outcome, with honest sample splitting so the same data does not choose the split and estimate within it.

Every one of them still needs movement C's assumptions. A flexible model of $\tau(x)$ fitted on confounded data estimates a confounded function of $x$ very precisely.

Pitfall: you cannot validate it the usual way

Part VI's whole apparatus rests on holding out labels and comparing. Here the label, $Y_1 - Y_0$ for one unit, does not exist in any dataset, ever. There is no held-out set that scores a CATE model directly.

What can be done: score the policy the model implies, using §32's off-policy machinery, or compare groups ranked by predicted effect and check that the realised effect ordering matches. Both are weaker than a held-out loss, and treating a low error on some proxy target as validation is the standard mistake in this area.

Go and read

Athey & Imbens (2016), Recursive partitioning for heterogeneous causal effects. Trees that split on effect heterogeneity rather than outcome variation, with honest sample splitting. Read §3 for why the same data cannot both choose a split and estimate within it: the problem Part XIV §20 measures in another setting.

§34 Invariance, and why it transfers

Why the causal model is flat and the others are not

The mechanism producing $Y$ from its causes is the same in every environment: that is what §8's modularity assumption says. A predictor built only on causes is therefore estimating something stable, and its error does not move.

A predictor using an effect of $Y$ is exploiting the reverse relationship, and nothing holds that fixed. When the environment changes it, the model does not degrade gracefully; it degrades in proportion to how much it was relying on the shortcut, which is exactly how much it was winning by before.

This is the causal account of what Part VI called spurious correlation and Part XIII §8 called shortcuts. It also says why the problem is invisible in ordinary validation: a random split leaves the spurious relationship intact in both halves.

What to do about it

Split by environment, not at random. If you have data from several sites, periods or populations, hold out a whole one. It is the only split that can detect this.

Penalise instability. Invariant risk minimisation and its relatives search for predictors whose optimal coefficients are the same across environments. The idea is right; the measured performance of the current methods against a well-tuned baseline is contested, and should be checked rather than assumed.

Accept the cost. Figure 6's causal model is four times worse on training-like data. Invariance is bought with accuracy in the environment you have, and if that environment is the only one you will ever face, it is not worth buying.

Go and read

Peters, Janzing & Schölkopf (2017), Elements of Causal Inference. Where causality meets machine learning: invariance, transfer, and causal discovery, with the identifiability results stated properly. The chapter on invariant prediction is the theory behind Figure 6.

§35 Causal discovery

Learning the graph, and the limits

Given only observational data, how much of the causal structure is recoverable?

Constraint-based methods (PC, FCI) test conditional independences and keep the graphs consistent with them. Score-based methods search graph space against a penalised likelihood. Both return a Markov equivalence class: a set of graphs no observational data can distinguish, because they imply identical independences.

So the honest output is not a graph but a family of them, and the direction of many edges remains undetermined. Extra assumptions (additive noise, non-Gaussianity, known temporal order) can orient more edges, and each is an assumption rather than a finding.

Pitfall: discovery does not remove the need for knowledge

The appeal is obvious: skip the uncomfortable graph-drawing of §9 and learn it. The results do not support that. Discovery is sensitive to sample size, to faithfulness violations, and above all to unmeasured confounders, which by construction it cannot see.

Where it earns its place is as a check on a graph you drew: run it, and where the data contradicts your assumed independences, find out why. That is the same use as a residual plot, and it is a good one.

§36 What Part XXI's "causal" means, and what it does not

Two uses of one word, and the relationship between them

Part XXI §9–§13 intervenes on a network's internals (patching an activation, ablating a direction, adding a steering vector) and calls the resulting claims causal. They are. The network is a structural causal model: every activation is a deterministic function of its parents, the graph is the architecture, and it is written down exactly.

That makes it the easiest causal inference problem in this book. There is no unmeasured confounding, because you have every variable. Positivity is whatever you choose to set. The intervention is exact rather than approximated. Part XXI's figures do not need any of movement C or D.

What those claims are about is the computation, not the world. Figure 3 of Part XXI establishes that ablating a direction does not change the network's output. It establishes nothing about whether the property that direction encodes affects anything outside the model. The two questions share a formalism and answer to different evidence.

The check worth running on any interpretability claim

Ask what the units are. If the intervention is on activations and the outcome is a model output, the claim is about the mechanism, and the graph is known. If either side reaches into the world (a user, an outcome, a decision), every assumption in movement C is back, and the fact that a network's internals are transparent buys you nothing there.

§37 Fairness, with the causal versions available

Part XXI §29 gave three observational fairness criteria and the theorem that they cannot all hold. With this part's machinery, criteria that refer to mechanism rather than correlation can be stated, and the reason they are not simply better is worth having.

Three causal criteria

Total effect. The effect of the protected attribute on the decision, along all paths. Simple, and usually not what anyone means.

Path-specific effects. Some paths are held legitimate (through qualifications, say) and others are not. The criterion is that the effect along the illegitimate paths is zero. This matches how the question is actually argued about.

Counterfactual fairness. The decision would be unchanged had the individual's protected attribute been different, holding their noise terms fixed. The strongest statement, and it lives on rung three.

Why they have not displaced the observational criteria

Each requires a causal graph over attributes including the protected one, and those graphs are contested: what counts as a legitimate path is the disagreement itself, not something the data settles. The observational criteria of Part XXI §29 require no graph, which is why they are used, and they are cruder for exactly the same reason.

The useful contribution here is not a better metric but a clearer statement of the disagreement: an argument about fairness is usually an argument about which paths are legitimate, and saying so moves it forward more than another impossibility theorem does.

§38 What a randomised system gets for free

The cheapest thing in this part

A system that already randomises (an A/B test, an epsilon-greedy recommender, a bandit) is generating unconfounded data continuously, and Part XVIII's exploration machinery is a causal design that nobody labelled as one.

Two requirements to keep the benefit, both of them logging decisions rather than modelling ones. Log the probability, not just the action: §32 needs it and it cannot be reconstructed. Log the version of the policy that acted, because a propensity from the wrong policy is worse than none.

Systems that do this can answer counterfactual questions about their own past for the cost of a query. Systems that do not have thrown away an asset that was free at the time and is unrecoverable afterwards.

§39 What movement E establishes

Three things

The stability of a predictor is a causal property. Figure 6's causal model is four times worse in the training environment and the only one still working when the environment changes. Ordinary validation cannot see the difference, because a random split preserves the shortcut.

Individual effects cannot be validated the way predictions can. The label does not exist for any unit. Everything available is a proxy, and treating a proxy loss as validation is the standard failure in this area.

Interpretability's causal claims are the easy case and a different claim. A network is an SCM you wrote down, so intervening on it needs none of movements C or D, and establishes something about the computation, not about the world.
