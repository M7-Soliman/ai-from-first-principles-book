Part CSpecification

Part XVIII trained policies against rewards and Part XX aligned models against preferences. Neither asked where the reward came from. This movement does, finds that the gap between what you wrote and what you meant is the durable problem, and measures precisely when optimising into that gap becomes destructive rather than merely imperfect.

§17 The problem, stated without metaphor

The specification problem

Optimisation requires an objective. The objective is written by a person, in a formal language, before the system's behaviour is known. What the person wanted is not fully expressible in that language and is not fully known to them in advance. So the objective is an approximation, the system optimises the approximation, and the difference between the two is where every problem in this movement lives.

Nothing here requires the system to be agentic, deceptive or superhuman. A linear regression fitted to the wrong target has the same structure. What scale changes is how hard the approximation gets pushed, which §18 shows is exactly the variable that matters.

Why this is not solved by writing a better objective

You can always improve a specification once you have seen how it fails. The difficulty is that the failures appear only under optimisation pressure you have not yet applied, so the loop is: specify, optimise, discover the gap, respecify. That loop is fine when discovery is cheap and reversible, which is the case for most of this book and is not the case for a deployed system operating at volume.

This is the actual content of the alignment problem, and it needs no assumptions beyond the ones already used in Part XVIII. The exotic scenarios are downstream; the structure is here.

§18 Goodhart, and the condition for it

Go and read

Gao, Schulman & Hilton (2023), Scaling laws for reward model overoptimisation. Read a second time here, for the shape rather than the coefficients: true reward rises, peaks and falls as the proxy is optimised harder. Figure 4 reproduces that shape in three lines of simulation and isolates the condition that produces it.

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" is the most quoted sentence in this subject and one of the least examined. Taken literally it is false (optimising a good proxy usually helps), so the useful question is what separates the cases.

Why the tail is the whole story

Write the proxy as truth plus error, $p = t + \varepsilon$. Selecting the highest $p$ from $n$ candidates selects for high $t$ and high $\varepsilon$. Which one dominates depends on which has more probability mass far out.

Light tails: extreme $\varepsilon$ is so unlikely that even at $n = 5000$ the winner is mostly someone with genuinely high $t$. Heavy tails: at large $n$ you are almost certainly looking at a large $\varepsilon$, so the winner is an error, and the true reward you obtain is whatever that error happened to be attached to, which is to say, nothing.

So the condition is not "the proxy is imperfect". Every proxy is. The condition is that the proxy's error can be much larger than typical, and that you are searching hard enough to find where.

What this says about the systems in Part XX

A learned reward model is exactly this structure: a proxy, trained on finite preference data, used to select among many candidate outputs. The relevant question is not its accuracy on held-out pairs: that is the correlation, and Figure 4 shows correlation does not settle it. The relevant question is how badly wrong it can be on the inputs that maximise it, which is a question about its tail on out-of-distribution inputs.

That reframes the standard mitigations. A KL penalty back to the reference model limits how far the search goes, so it bounds $n$ in effect, and Figure 4 says that is the variable that matters.

Reward-model ensembles are the other standard answer, and they deserve a more careful statement than they usually get. Averaging independent reward models shrinks the size of the error, measurably, by about the factor variance reduction would predict. It does not change the shape of its tail: a tail heavy enough to cause the collapse survives averaging, and the normalised tail statistic does not improve. So an ensemble moves you along the heavy curve rather than onto the light one. It helps, and it is not a fix, and the drills measure both halves of that.

Try it

Reproduce Figure 4, then add two arms. First: keep the heavy-tailed error but cap the search at the $n$ where the curve peaked: this is what a KL penalty buys, and it should recover the peak. Second: average several independent heavy-tailed proxies and select on the average.

Measure the tail statistic and the standard deviation of the averaged error separately before you look at the reward curve. Predict which of the two moves. Most people predict both, the answer is one of them, and which one it is decides how much an ensemble can be expected to buy.

§19 Reward hacking, observed

Reward hacking is Figure 4 with a search procedure that is smarter than best-of-$n$. The reinforcement learning of Part XVIII does not sample and pick; it gradient-ascends, which finds the extreme of the proxy far more efficiently and therefore reaches the destructive regime sooner.

The recurring shapes, and which part of the book each belongs to

Optimising the measurement channel rather than the thing. The classic simulated-robot results: an agent that learns to exploit the physics engine, or to move the camera rather than the object. Part XVIII §40's offline distribution shift, in a form you can see.

Exploiting the judge. Part XX §42's model-as-judge has known biases (length, format, confidence) and a policy optimised against it finds them. This is the most common form in current practice because the judge is the most common reward.

Satisfying the letter. Producing output that scores well on every stated criterion while missing what the criteria were for. Hardest to detect, because by construction it passes the tests you wrote.

Pitfall: the proxy score keeps rising the whole time

In Figure 4 the heavy-tailed curve collapses to 0.097 while the proxy it was selected on is at its maximum. If your monitoring is the reward, you will observe an unbroken success.

Reward hacking is therefore not detectable from the training curve, ever. It requires a measurement that does not route through the proxy: held-out human evaluation, a different objective, or somebody looking at the outputs. Budget for it before training, because afterwards the numbers will all look fine.

§20 Outer and inner, carefully

Two failures with different fixes

Outer: the objective you wrote is not what you wanted, and a system that optimises it perfectly still does the wrong thing. Everything in §18 and §19. Well evidenced, measurable, and the subject of most practical work.

Inner: the training process produces a system that pursues something other than the objective you trained on: the objective selected the system, but the system's own regularity is different, and the two agree on the training distribution and diverge off it.

The distinction is real and worth keeping. The evidence for the two is not comparable, and the honest thing is to say so.

What the evidence for the inner case actually is

The uncontroversial part is documented throughout this book: models learn features that correlate with the target on the training distribution and fail off it: Part VI's spurious correlations, Part XIII §13's contamination, every shortcut-learning result. That is inner misalignment in the weak sense, it is ordinary, and it is measured by distribution shift.

The strong version (a system with a coherent internal objective it pursues strategically, including by behaving well while observed) is a hypothesis, not a measurement. It is not incoherent, and the argument for taking it seriously is that we have no method that would detect it if true, which is precisely why movement A and B's programme matters. But it should be labelled as what it is. A book that presented it as an established finding would be doing the thing this part spends §15 warning against.

§21 Scalable oversight

The problem

Every method in Part XX's movement D requires a human judgement of which output is better. That works while humans can tell. For a long technical document, a large codebase, or a specialised domain, the evaluator is slower and less expert than the system, and the preference data becomes noise, or worse, becomes a measurement of persuasiveness, which is a proxy with precisely the heavy tail §18 warned about.

Scalable oversight is the problem of supervising a system you cannot directly evaluate. It is unsolved, and it is the bottleneck that binds first as capability increases.

The asymmetry the proposals exploit

Verification is often easier than generation. Finding a proof is hard, checking one is easy; writing a large program is hard, running its tests is easy. Wherever that asymmetry holds, a weaker overseer can supervise a stronger generator.

The proposals in §22 are all attempts to convert a hard evaluation into an easy one by exploiting it. The corresponding worry is that the asymmetry fails exactly where it is most needed: for judgements of value, or of subtle real-world consequence, checking is not obviously easier than doing.

§22 Debate, amplification, weak-to-strong

Three proposals, with the state of the evidence for each

Debate. Two systems argue opposite sides before a weaker judge. The hope is that refuting a false claim is easier than defending it, so truth wins even when the judge could not have evaluated the question alone. Evidence: encouraging on tasks with checkable ground truth; unclear where the disagreement is about values, and vulnerable if persuasiveness beats correctness, which is the same tail problem again.

Amplification. Decompose a hard question into easier ones the overseer can evaluate, answer those with the system's help, recompose. Evidence: works when the problem decomposes cleanly; the hard cases are the ones that do not.

Weak-to-strong generalisation. Supervise a strong model with a weak model's labels and measure how much of the strong model's latent capability is recovered. The finding is that a substantial fraction is recovered: the strong model generalises past its supervisor's errors rather than imitating them. Evidence: a real and replicated result on current models; whether it holds at larger capability gaps is exactly the extrapolation nobody can currently make.

Go and read

Burns et al. (2023), Weak-to-strong generalization. Read it for the experimental design rather than the headline: the analogy between "weak model supervising strong model" and "human supervising superhuman model" is the paper's load-bearing assumption, the authors say so explicitly, and how much weight it can carry is the thing to form a view on.

§23 What we cannot currently specify

Four, and why each resists

Honesty. Distinguishing a model that reports what it internally represents from one that produces text scoring well as honest requires reading internal state: movement A's problem. We currently reward the appearance.

Harmlessness in general. Enumerable harms can be trained against, and Part XX §33 measured how well that holds. What is left is the unenumerated remainder, and no specification covers a set you cannot list.

Corrigibility. "Accept correction and shutdown" conflicts with almost any objective a system is given, since being stopped scores badly on nearly everything. Stating it without contradiction is an open problem, not an engineering detail.

Whose preferences. The methods in Part XX aggregate annotator preferences. Which annotators, weighted how, resolving disagreement by what rule: these are choices currently made implicitly by whoever assembles the dataset. That is a political question wearing a technical costume, and §41 classes it as the third kind of unknown.

§24 What movement C establishes

Three things

The gap between the objective and the intention is the durable problem, and it needs no exotic assumptions. It is present in a misfitted regression and becomes dangerous only when optimisation pressure gets large.

Goodhart has a condition, and it is the tail, not the correlation. Figure 4's two arms have identical correlation and opposite outcomes. This makes the standard mitigations legible: KL penalties bound the search, ensembles thin the tail, and neither is about accuracy.

The proxy looks healthy throughout the failure. The heavy-tailed arm reaches its best proxy score at the moment the true reward has collapsed to 0.097. Any monitoring that routes through the objective is blind to this by construction, which is why movement D is a separate measurement discipline rather than a subsection here.
