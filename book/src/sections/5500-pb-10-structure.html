Part AWhat a joint distribution factorises into

A probabilistic model is a joint distribution over everything you care about. Written out in full it is unusable, and every method in this part exists because of one fact: real joint distributions factorise, and the factorisation is a set of claims about independence you can state, draw, and check.

§1 The object, and why it is intractable

Where the difficulty comes from

A joint distribution over $n$ binary variables has $2^n - 1$ free parameters. At $n = 40$ that is a table larger than any machine will hold, and you would need more data than exists to estimate one entry of it.

So the question is never "what is the joint" but what structure does it have. Every model in this book is an answer: a claim that the joint is a product of small factors, and that most of the $2^n$ degrees of freedom were never there.

§2 Factorisation is the whole idea

The chain rule, and then an assumption

The chain rule is free and unhelpful: any joint equals $\prod_i p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})$, and the last factor is as big as the original problem.

It becomes useful when you assert that each factor depends on only a few of its predecessors:

$$p(x_1, \dots, x_n) \;=\; \prod_{i} p\!\left(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}(i)\right)$$

Now the parameter count is exponential in the largest parent set rather than in $n$. That is the entire economics of the subject, and everything that follows is either exploiting the structure or paying for its absence.

§3 Directed models

A graph is a factorisation

Draw a node per variable and an arrow from each parent to each child. The graph must be acyclic. The joint is the product above, and the graph and the factorisation are the same object written two ways.

What an arrow does and does not mean. It licenses a dependence; it does not assert one. And it is not by itself a causal claim: Part XXII spent a whole part on the difference, and the graphs look identical. What makes a directed model causal is the additional commitment that intervening on a node deletes its incoming arrows, which is a statement about the world and not about the factorisation.

§4 Conditional independence, read off the graph

Go and read

Pearl (1988), Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems. Where graphical models and d-separation come from. Chapter 3 is the one to read, and the collider argument in it is the argument Part XXII needs, stated more clearly here than in most causal-inference texts.

Three shapes, and one of them is the surprising one

Chain $X \to Y \to Z$. $X$ and $Z$ are dependent, and conditioning on $Y$ makes them independent. Information flows along the path and $Y$ blocks it.

Fork $X \leftarrow Y \to Z$. Same: dependent, and conditioning on the common cause separates them. This is Part XXII's confounder.

Collider $X \to Y \leftarrow Z$. Here it inverts. $X$ and $Z$ are independent, and conditioning on $Y$ makes them dependent. Learning the effect ties its causes together: if the alarm rang and it was not a burglar, it was an earthquake.

d-separation chains these rules along every path. Two sets of variables are independent given a third exactly when every path between them is blocked, and the check is graph-theoretic: no distributions, no integrals, just paths.

§5 Measured

Pitfall: the collider is why "control for more things" is wrong

The instinct that conditioning on more variables can only help is exactly false, and Figure 1 is the measurement. Conditioning on a collider, or on anything downstream of one, manufactures a correlation between variables that had none.

This is Part XXII §4's finding arriving from the other direction: there it was a threat to a causal estimate, here it is a property of the factorisation. And note the third bar: the damage does not require conditioning on the collider itself. Selecting your sample on any consequence of two independent causes is enough, which is how it happens in practice, silently, through the choice of who ends up in the dataset.

§6 Undirected models, and what they buy

When there is no natural direction

For pixels in an image or atoms in a lattice there is no parent and no child, only mutual influence. An undirected model writes the joint as a product of non-negative factors over cliques, divided by a normalising constant:

$$p(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z} \prod_{c} \psi_c(\mathbf{x}_c), \qquad Z = \sum_{\mathbf{x}} \prod_c \psi_c(\mathbf{x}_c)$$

Independence is simpler to read than in the directed case: two nodes are independent given a set that separates them in the graph, with no collider exception. What you pay for that is $Z$: a sum over every configuration, which is Part XVI §2's normalising constant and the reason energy-based models are hard.

Neither language contains the other

A collider's independence structure cannot be drawn undirected. A four-cycle's cannot be drawn directed without adding an edge. So the two formalisms overlap and neither is more general, which is worth knowing before spending an afternoon converting one to the other.

§7 Plates, and the models you already know

Most of this book, in this notation

A plate is a box drawn around a subgraph with a count, meaning "repeat this". It is how a model with a million observations fits on a page, and it makes the shared structure visible.

Naive Bayes is a class variable with a plate of conditionally independent features. A mixture model is a latent assignment per observation and shared component parameters. An HMM is a chain of latent states each emitting an observation. A VAE is a latent vector per observation with a shared decoder (Part XVI §18, drawn).

Writing a model this way is not decoration. It tells you immediately which variables are independent given which others, and therefore which of the next movement's algorithms apply.

§8 What movement A establishes

Three things

A model is a factorisation, and a graph is a picture of one. The parameter count goes from exponential in the number of variables to exponential in the largest parent set, and that gap is why the subject exists.

Independence is readable from the structure without touching a distribution, by d-separation.

Conditioning creates dependence as well as removing it. Figure 1 measures a collider turning +0.004 into −0.758, and a descendant of one doing the same thing at −0.423, which is why "adjust for everything you measured" is not a safe default.
