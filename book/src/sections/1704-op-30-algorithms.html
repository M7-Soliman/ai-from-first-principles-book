Part CThe algorithms, completed

Part III derived SGD, momentum, the adaptive methods and the schedules. This part supplies what was left out: the numbers you actually set them to, the physical argument that explains momentum's form, the variant that improves it, and the initialisation rules that decide whether any of it works.

§13 SGD, with the schedule spelled out

In 60 seconds

Part III §14 gave the Robbins–Monro conditions and §17 gave the shapes of the common schedules. Here are the actual settings, the reason the best initial rate is higher than the one that looks best after a hundred steps, and an argument that a faster-converging optimiser would not help you even if you had one.

Go and read

Bottou & Bousquet (2008), The tradeoffs of large scale learning. The argument this section closes with, in full: once you account for the fact that generalisation error cannot improve faster than $O(1/k)$, an optimiser that converges faster than that is not buying you anything you wanted. Short, and it permanently changes how you read a convergence-rate claim.

Recall why a schedule is needed at all. The minibatch gradient carries noise that does not vanish as you approach a minimum: even sitting exactly at the optimum, a random sample of examples has a nonzero average gradient. Batch gradient descent can therefore use a fixed step size and SGD cannot: a fixed step leaves you rattling in a neighbourhood whose radius is set by the noise and the step.

The classical sufficient conditions for convergence, from Part III §14's history:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\epsilon_k = \infty \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\epsilon_k^2 < \infty$$

The first says the steps must still be able to carry you any finite distance; the second says the accumulated noise must be finite. Together they force the rate to decay, but not too fast.

The linear schedule, with numbers

Decay linearly to iteration $\tau$, then hold constant:

$$\epsilon_k = (1-\alpha)\,\epsilon_0 + \alpha\,\epsilon_\tau, \qquad \alpha = \frac{k}{\tau}$$

Three parameters, and two of them have conventional answers:

  • $\tau$: the number of iterations in a few hundred passes over the training set.
  • $\epsilon_\tau$: roughly 1% of $\epsilon_0$.
  • $\epsilon_0$: the one that matters, and the one you have to find.

Setting $\epsilon_0$ is done by watching learning curves, and it is more art than science; be sceptical of anyone who tells you otherwise, including this book. What can be said usefully is what the two failure modes look like.

Too large and the curve shows violent oscillation, with the cost frequently increasing by a lot. Gentle oscillation is fine and expected, particularly with a stochastic cost such as one involving dropout; do not tune it away. Too small and learning is slow; worse, if $\epsilon_0$ is too small the run can become stuck at a high cost and stay there.

Pitfall: the rate that looks best after 100 steps is not the best rate

This is the most useful counterintuitive fact about learning rate selection, and the usual tuning procedure gets it backwards.

The standard sweep runs each candidate rate for a few hundred iterations and picks the one with the lowest loss. But the rate that wins that comparison is reliably lower than the rate that gives the best total training time and the best final cost.

The reason is §6. A large rate spends its early iterations moving fast and looking ragged, while a smaller rate produces a tidier early curve and then runs out of headroom later. What you want is the rate that is higher than the early winner, but not so high that it destabilises, so use the first hundred iterations to find the instability threshold, not the optimum, and set $\epsilon_0$ below the threshold rather than at the early optimum.

This is also the argument for warmup. Starting small and increasing over the first few hundred steps lets you run at a rate that would have been unstable from a cold start, once the parameters have moved somewhere the curvature tolerates it.

What the convergence rates are, and why they do not matter much

The standard measure is the excess error $J(\boldsymbol{\theta}) - \min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} J(\boldsymbol{\theta})$. On a convex problem, SGD achieves $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ after $k$ iterations, and $O(1/k)$ if the problem is strongly convex. Neither can be improved without extra assumptions. Batch gradient descent does better in theory.

And yet stochastic methods win overwhelmingly in practice, for a reason worth stating carefully, because it is not simply "the constants are better".

Why a faster optimiser would not help you

There is a bound on the other side. The Cramér–Rao bound (Part V §5's efficiency limit) says that generalisation error cannot decrease faster than $O(1/k)$ in the number of examples processed.

Put those together. If your optimiser converges faster than $O(1/k)$ in optimisation error, the extra speed cannot be buying generalisation, because generalisation is not allowed to improve that fast. The extra speed is being spent on fitting the training set more precisely than the training set deserves, which is a definition of overfitting.

So the case for pursuing faster-converging optimisers for machine learning is weak, and this is an unusual situation: a place where the theory says the obvious improvement is not worth having. It also explains why the asymptotic analysis is the wrong lens. Almost every method in this part earns its keep in the early iterations, in constants that asymptotic notation discards.

The property that actually matters is the one that has nothing to do with convergence rates: the cost per update does not grow with the size of the training set. That is what makes training on a hundred million examples possible at all, and on a large enough dataset SGD can reach a fixed tolerance on test error before it has finished one pass.

§14 Momentum, and why the form is forced

Part III §15 derived momentum's terminal velocity $\|\mathbf{g}\|/(1-\beta)$ and its reading as an average over the last $1/(1-\beta)$ gradients. What it did not do is justify the form of the update, which looks like one arbitrary choice among many. It is not, and the argument is one of the more satisfying in the subject.

Treat the parameters as the position of a particle. Newton's second law says force is proportional to acceleration, a second-order equation in position; introduce velocity and it becomes two first-order equations,

$$\mathbf{v}(t) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\theta}(t), \qquad \mathbf{f}(t) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbf{v}(t)$$

and momentum is simply Euler's method applied to that pair: take small finite steps in the direction each derivative indicates. The first force is $-\nabla J$, pushing the particle downhill. A hockey puck on ice, gathering speed down a slope and coasting until it starts to climb.

But a puck on frictionless ice never stops. It slides down one side of the valley, straight up the other, and oscillates forever. Something must remove energy.

Worked: why viscous drag, and not the alternatives

The second force is $-\mathbf{v}(t)$: drag proportional to velocity, as though moving through syrup. That is the $\beta\mathbf{v}$ term. Why that power of $\mathbf{v}$ and not another? Physics offers exactly three candidates, and two of them fail for opposite reasons.

Turbulent drag, $\propto \|\mathbf{v}\|^2$, the drag on a body moving through air. It is too weak. As velocity falls the force falls quadratically, so it becomes negligible exactly when you need it. A particle with a nonzero initial velocity and only turbulent drag never comes to rest at all: its distance from the start grows like $O(\log t)$, forever.

Dry friction, a constant magnitude (a block sliding on a surface). It is too strong. A constant opposing force does not care how gently the objective slopes, so wherever the gradient is small but nonzero, friction cancels it and the particle stops short. You would converge to a point that is not a minimum, chosen by the friction coefficient rather than by the objective.

Viscous drag, $\propto \|\mathbf{v}\|$, is the only one between them. It is weak enough that any nonzero gradient can still produce motion, and strong enough that motion stops when the gradient no longer justifies it.

So the form of the momentum update is not a convenient choice among several. It is the unique integer power of velocity that both permits convergence and guarantees it, and the two neighbouring powers fail in the two available ways.

§15 Nesterov momentum

One line changes, and it is worth understanding because the change is smaller than the usual descriptions suggest.

Nesterov momentum

$$\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \alpha\mathbf{v} - \epsilon\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \Big[\tfrac1m\textstyle\sum_i L\big(f(\mathbf{x}^{(i)};\,\boldsymbol{\theta} + \alpha\mathbf{v}),\,y^{(i)}\big)\Big], \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} + \mathbf{v}$$

The only difference from standard momentum is where the gradient is evaluated: after the current velocity has been applied, rather than before.

The interpretation that makes it obvious: you already know you are about to move by $\alpha\mathbf{v}$, whatever the gradient says. So evaluate the gradient where you are going rather than where you are. If the surface has started to curve upward at the landing point, you find out before committing rather than after, and the correction is applied one step earlier.

The theory is precise and its limits should be stated with it. In the convex batch case Nesterov improves the excess error from $O(1/k)$ to $O(1/k^2)$, which is a genuine acceleration and the reason the method is celebrated. In the stochastic case (the one you are in) it does not improve the convergence rate at all.

That does not make it useless, and Figure 6 is not a trick: the damping is real and costs nothing, which is why it is a checkbox on most optimisers. But the honest summary is that Nesterov momentum is a modest practical improvement whose famous theoretical guarantee applies to a setting deep learning is not in.

§16 Initialisation: what the scale has to achieve

Part VII §11 derived the $1/\sqrt{n_{\text{in}}}$ rule from the forward pass, and the factor of two that ReLU adds. That derivation looked at half the problem. Here is the other half, and it changes the answer.

Consider a layer with $m$ inputs and $n$ outputs, weights of variance $s^2$.

  • Forward: each output sums $m$ terms, so the activation variance is multiplied by $m s^2$. Preserving it requires $s^2 = 1/m$.
  • Backward: each input's gradient sums $n$ terms, so the gradient variance is multiplied by $n s^2$. Preserving that requires $s^2 = 1/n$.

Unless $m = n$, you cannot have both. This is not a subtlety to be resolved by a cleverer derivation; it is a genuine conflict between two things you want, and every initialisation scheme is a position on it.

Glorot's normalised initialisation

$$W_{i,j} \sim U\!\left(-\sqrt{\frac{6}{m+n}},\; \sqrt{\frac{6}{m+n}}\right) \qquad\text{giving}\qquad s^2 = \frac{2}{m+n}$$

The harmonic-mean compromise: forward variance is multiplied by $2m/(m+n)$ and backward by $2n/(m+n)$, so both are wrong, and both are wrong by less than either single-sided rule is wrong in its bad direction.

The derivation assumes the network is a chain of matrix multiplications with no nonlinearities, which real networks obviously violate. It works anyway, which is a recurring pattern: strategies designed for the linear model transfer surprisingly well.

Beyond the variance rules

Three refinements are worth knowing, each addressing something the variance argument misses.

Orthogonal initialisation. Initialise each weight matrix to a random orthogonal matrix, scaled by a gain $g$ chosen for the nonlinearity in use. Under the linear model this guarantees that the number of iterations to convergence is independent of depth, a much stronger property than variance preservation, which only controls the scale on average. Setting the gain correctly turns out to be enough on its own: feedforward networks of a thousand layers have been trained with ordinary random weights and a correct gain, and no orthogonality at all.

Sparse initialisation. Every rule of the form $s = 1/\sqrt{m}$ has a side effect: as layers get wide, every individual weight becomes tiny. Sparse initialisation instead gives each unit exactly $k$ nonzero incoming weights, of ordinary size. The total input to a unit is then independent of $m$ without the individual magnitudes shrinking, and the units are far more diverse at the start.

Its cost is a strong implicit prior: the weights chosen to be large are large by accident, and gradient descent takes a long time to shrink a weight that should not have been large. That makes it a poor fit for architectures whose filters must be coordinated with one another, maxout units being the standard example.

Why bigger is not simply better, in three tensions

Larger initial weights are good for several reasons at once. They break symmetry more decisively, so fewer units end up redundant. They preserve signal through the linear part of each layer in both directions. And they keep you away from the flat region near zero.

They are also bad for several reasons at once. They can explode in the forward or backward pass. In recurrent networks they produce chaos, a sensitivity to input so extreme that a deterministic forward pass looks random. And they push activation functions into saturation, where the gradient is gone entirely, which is Part VII §19's failure.

There is a third consideration that is not about optimisation at all. Because SGD makes small incremental changes and tends to halt near where it started, initialising at $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$ acts like a Gaussian prior centred on $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$, Part VIII §14's early-stopping-equals-weight-decay result, read as a statement about initialisation. Choosing $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$ near zero is then a substantive claim: that units are more likely not to interact than to interact, and should interact only where the data insists. Initialising large asserts the opposite, and specifies which interactions you expect.

So the optimisation view wants larger weights and the regularisation view wants smaller ones, and they are answering different questions. This is why the theoretically optimal scale routinely fails to be the best scale in practice: the criterion may be wrong, the property may not survive the first few updates, or it may speed optimisation while hurting generalisation.

§17 Initialisation: biases, and a protocol that beats searching

Weights get all the attention. Biases are easier, and there are four cases where the default of zero is wrong in a way that costs you real performance.

When a bias should not start at zero

Output biases, to match the marginal. If the initial weights are small, the output is determined by the bias alone. So set the bias to the inverse of the output nonlinearity applied to the training set's marginal statistics: for a classifier with class frequencies $\mathbf{c}$, solve $\text{softmax}(\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{c}$. The model then begins by predicting the base rates rather than a uniform distribution, and does not waste its early steps learning what a single pass over the labels would have told it.

ReLU biases, to avoid starting dead. Setting a ReLU's bias to $0.1$ rather than $0$ keeps the unit off the flat side at initialisation. Do not combine this with schemes that assume the bias contributes nothing, such as random-walk initialisation.

Gate biases, so the gate starts open. Where a unit $h \in [0,1]$ multiplies another unit's output $u$, a gate that starts closed means $u$ receives no gradient and never gets a chance to learn. Initialise $b_h$ so $h \approx 1$. This is why the standard advice is to set an LSTM's forget-gate bias to $1$.

Variance and precision parameters may safely start at $1$; or set the biases to produce the correct marginal mean and the variance parameters to the training set's marginal variance.

The output-bias rule is the one most often skipped and the easiest to justify. On a problem with 99% negatives, a zero bias starts the model predicting 50/50, and the first phase of training is spent undoing that. It is free information and it takes one line.

A protocol that is cheaper than hyperparameter search

When you can afford it, treating each layer's initial scale as a hyperparameter and searching over it is the reliable answer. There is a much cheaper procedure that usually gets close, and it needs no validation set and no trained model:

  1. Push a single minibatch through the freshly initialised network.
  2. Look at the range or standard deviation of the activations at each layer.
  3. Find the first layer where they have become unacceptably small, and increase that layer's weights.
  4. Repeat until the activations are reasonable all the way through.
  5. If learning is still slow, do the same for the gradients in the backward pass.

The reason this is so much cheaper is structural. Hyperparameter search gets its feedback from a trained model on a validation set, one full training run per candidate. This gets its feedback from the initial model on one batch. It is seconds against hours, and it can be automated.

It is also the diagnostic to run first when a deep network will not train at all, before touching the optimiser. Part VII §11's figure is this measurement made once; the protocol is that measurement turned into a loop.

In the framework: every rule above is a one-liner, and the defaults are not what you derived

Having derived the schemes, the mapping is direct. What matters is that PyTorch's default for a linear layer is none of them.

import torch.nn as nn

lin = nn.Linear(512, 128)

nn.init.xavier_uniform_(lin.weight)                        # Glorot, section 16
nn.init.kaiming_normal_(lin.weight, nonlinearity="relu")   # He, Part VII section 11
nn.init.orthogonal_(lin.weight, gain=nn.init.calculate_gain("relu"))
nn.init.zeros_(lin.bias)

What each piece corresponds to. xavier_uniform_ draws from $U(-a, a)$ with $a = \text{gain}\sqrt{6/(m+n)}$, the bound in §16's box, with gain the $g$ of the orthogonal discussion. kaiming_normal_ uses $\mathcal{N}(0, 2/m)$, the factor of two being ReLU's. calculate_gain returns the $g$ derived for each nonlinearity: $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$ for ReLU, $5/3 \approx 1.667$ for tanh, and exactly $1$ for sigmoid and for no nonlinearity at all. That tanh wants a gain above one is worth a moment's thought: tanh compresses its input toward zero, so the weights must be enlarged to compensate, and the plain $1$ of the linear case is the exception rather than the rule.

Why it is designed that way, and the trap. A fresh nn.Linear is not initialised by any of these. Its default is $U(-1/\sqrt{m},\, 1/\sqrt{m})$, which has variance $1/(3m)$, a third of the fan-in rule and a sixth of He. It is a historical default that predates both papers and it survives for backward compatibility.

For a shallow network the difference is invisible. For a deep ReLU stack it is a factor of six per layer in variance, compounding, and it is a routine cause of a deep network that trains to chance and no further. If you build a deep network from bare nn.Linear layers and never touch the initialisation, you have chosen a scheme, just not one anybody would recommend.

The output-bias rule, which no framework will do for you:

counts = torch.bincount(train_labels, minlength=n_classes).float()
with torch.no_grad():
    head.bias.copy_(torch.log(counts / counts.sum()))   # softmax(b) = c

Since $\text{softmax}(\log \mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{c}$ for any $\mathbf{c}$ summing to one, the log of the class frequencies is the exact solution to §17's equation. On an imbalanced problem this alone removes the early phase where the loss falls quickly and the model has learned nothing but the base rates.

Keras spells the same operations tf.keras.initializers.GlorotUniform() and HeNormal(), and its Dense layer defaults to Glorot, a better default than PyTorch's, and a difference worth knowing when porting a model that trains in one and not the other.

§18 Adaptive methods: choosing, and the honest answer

Part III §16 derived AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam as a line of descent, each fixing the previous one's flaw. Two things remain to be said: where the idea came from, and which one to use.

The origin is older than it looks. Delta-bar-delta, from 1988, adapts a separate learning rate per parameter by a rule that needs no algebra to state: if a parameter's partial derivative keeps the same sign, that direction is consistent, so increase its rate; if the sign flips, you are oscillating across something, so decrease it. It only works full-batch; with minibatch noise the sign flips constantly for reasons that have nothing to do with the surface, and the modern methods can be read as the same intuition made robust to sampling noise by squaring instead of counting sign changes.

Which optimiser should you use? There is no consensus, and that is the answer

The most careful large comparison across many tasks found that the adaptive family performs robustly, and that no single algorithm is best. The methods in routine use (SGD, SGD with momentum, RMSProp, RMSProp with momentum, AdaDelta, Adam) are all defensible, and the choice appears to depend mostly on the user's familiarity with the algorithm, because familiarity is what makes its hyperparameters tunable.

That is an uncomfortable conclusion and it is the correct one. It also has a practical reading that is more useful than a ranking: the returns to knowing one optimiser well exceed the returns to picking the right one. Someone who understands what Adam's $\beta_2$ does will beat someone who switched to the current favourite and left the defaults alone.

Part III §16's caveat stands alongside this: well-tuned SGD with momentum still generalises better than Adam on some problems, particularly in vision, even while Adam reaches a lower training loss faster. Lower training loss is not the objective, per §1.
