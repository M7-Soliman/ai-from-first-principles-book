Part DSecond order, properly

Part III §12 gave four reasons nobody uses Newton's method at scale and moved on. That was the right call at the time and it left three well-known methods unexamined. They are worth having: one of them is the standard tool for the largest problems that are solved this way, and understanding why they lose here sharpens the reasons the first-order methods win.

§19 Newton's method, damped

The update from Part III §11 is $\boldsymbol{\theta}^* = \boldsymbol{\theta}_0 - H^{-1}\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)$, and it is exact for a quadratic with positive definite $H$. §8 gave the objection that matters here: in deep learning $H$ is not positive definite, most critical points are saddles, and at a saddle the negative eigenvalues make $H^{-1}$ point some components of the update uphill.

The standard repair is to add a constant along the diagonal.

The damped, or regularized, Newton update

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}^* = \boldsymbol{\theta}_0 - \big[H(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) + \alpha I\big]^{-1}\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)$$

Adding $\alpha$ to every eigenvalue. Choose $\alpha$ larger than the most negative eigenvalue and every direction becomes a descent direction again. This is the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm, from 1944 and 1963.

Why this expression should look familiar

$[H + \alpha I]^{-1}$ is exactly the operator Part VIII §4 derived for ridge regression, where it produced the shrinkage factor $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i + \alpha)$ on each eigendirection.

The same algebra is doing two apparently unrelated jobs. In Part VIII the objective was statistical: shrink the directions the data does not constrain, so the fit does not chase noise. Here it is numerical: lift the negative and near-zero curvatures, so the step is well defined and points downhill.

The connection is not a coincidence. Both are the same complaint about the same matrix (that some of its eigenvalues are too small to trust) and the fix is the same in both cases because "too small to trust" and "too small to invert" are the same condition. It is worth noticing that the identical two-line change is called regularisation in one chapter and damping in another, and that neither field usually mentions the other.

The repair works while the negative eigenvalues are near zero. It fails, informatively, when they are not.

Watch what happens as $\alpha$ grows. The matrix $H + \alpha I$ becomes increasingly dominated by $\alpha I$, so its inverse tends to $\frac{1}{\alpha}I$, and the update tends to

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \;\longrightarrow\; \boldsymbol{\theta}_0 - \frac{1}{\alpha}\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)$$

which is plain gradient descent with learning rate $1/\alpha$. So damping does not merely fix Newton's method; it interpolates between Newton's method and gradient descent, with $\alpha$ as the dial.

And that gives the failure in one sentence. Where the curvature is strongly negative, the $\alpha$ required to make the step valid is large, large enough that the update is essentially gradient descent with a small learning rate. Newton's method, corrected enough to be safe here, takes smaller steps than a well-tuned gradient method, at $O(k^3)$ per iteration and with the whole Hessian recomputed each time.

Together with §5's measurement (that a second-order step amplifies gradient noise tenfold, so you need batches two orders of magnitude larger) the case is closed. Three independent objections, any one of which would be enough.

§20 Conjugate gradients

In 60 seconds

Steepest descent with an exact line search zigzags, and the zigzag is not bad luck; it is forced by the line search. Each new direction is necessarily orthogonal to the last, which is precisely the property that undoes the previous step's progress. Conjugate gradients picks directions that do not undo each other, and reaches the exact minimum of a quadratic in $n$ dimensions in at most $n$ steps, using only gradients.

Start with the diagnosis, which is the interesting part. Part III §13 introduced line search: from the current point, move along a direction and go to the lowest point on that line. Do that repeatedly along the gradient and you get the familiar zigzag. Here is why it is unavoidable.

Worked: the line search creates the zigzag

Let $\mathbf{d}_{t-1}$ be the previous search direction. At the point where the line search stopped, you are at the minimum along that line, so the directional derivative there is zero:

$$\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \cdot \mathbf{d}_{t-1} = 0$$

The next steepest-descent direction is $\mathbf{d}_t = -\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta})$. So $\mathbf{d}_t \cdot \mathbf{d}_{t-1} = 0$: the new direction is exactly orthogonal to the old one. Every time.

Now the damage. Moving along $\mathbf{d}_t$ changes the objective, and there is nothing keeping you at the minimum with respect to $\mathbf{d}_{t-1}$ while you do it. So you leave it, and a later iteration has to come back and re-minimise along $\mathbf{d}_{t-1}$ again. By following the gradient at the end of each line search you are systematically undoing work you already did.

The fix is to ask for a different relationship between consecutive directions. Orthogonality is the wrong requirement; what you want is that moving along $\mathbf{d}_t$ does not spoil the minimisation along $\mathbf{d}_{t-1}$. That condition has a name.

Conjugacy

Two directions are conjugate with respect to $H$ when

$$\mathbf{d}_t^\top H\,\mathbf{d}_{t-1} = 0$$

Orthogonality is this with $H = I$. Conjugacy is orthogonality in the geometry the Hessian defines, which is the geometry the objective actually has.

Build the new direction by adding back a measured amount of the old one:

$$\mathbf{d}_t = -\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t) + \beta_t \mathbf{d}_{t-1}$$

and the remarkable fact is that the right $\beta_t$ can be computed from gradients alone, with no access to $H$ whatever. Two standard choices:

$$\beta_t^{\text{FR}} = \frac{\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)^\top \nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)} {\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})^\top \nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})} \qquad \beta_t^{\text{PR}} = \frac{\big(\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t) - \nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})\big)^\top \nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)} {\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})^\top \nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})}$$

Fletcher–Reeves on the left, Polak–Ribière on the right.

That $\beta_t$ needs only gradient inner products is what makes the method viable. The naive route to conjugacy runs through the eigenvectors of $H$, which would cost more than Newton's method and defeat the purpose entirely.

The payoff: on a quadratic surface, conjugate directions guarantee that the gradient along previous directions does not grow, so you stay at the minimum along everything you have already searched. Each line search therefore finishes a dimension permanently, and in $k$ dimensions the method needs at most $k$ line searches.

Pitfall: "terminates in $n$ steps" is a theorem about exact arithmetic

The right panel is the caveat that textbooks state and implementations rediscover. Conjugacy is maintained by a recurrence, and in floating point the recurrence drifts. At $\kappa = 1000$ the directions have lost enough conjugacy by iteration 12 that the guarantee is simply gone.

This is Part III §6's distinction doing real work: the algorithm is exact, the implementation is not, and the gap widens with the condition number. It is why serious CG codes reorthogonalise or restart, and why "$n$ iterations" should be read as an optimistic bound rather than a schedule.

On non-quadratic objectives (that is, on anything you would train) the conjugate directions no longer keep you at previous minima, so nonlinear conjugate gradients periodically resets, throwing away $\beta$ and starting again from the plain gradient, typically every few iterations. It works reasonably on neural networks, and it works better if you run a few SGD iterations first to get somewhere sensible. Minibatch versions exist and have been used successfully, which is not obvious given §5's warning: CG uses gradient differences, and differences of noisy quantities are noisier still.

§21 BFGS and its limited-memory form

Go and read

Martens (2010), Deep learning via Hessian-free optimization. The most serious attempt to make second-order methods work at scale, and it works by never forming $H$: Hessian-vector products for the curvature, conjugate gradients for the solve, §§20 and 21 assembled into a real optimiser. It is also where the sparse initialisation of §16 comes from. It did not displace SGD, and the reasons are §5's batch-size measurement and §8's saddles, which makes reading it a good way to understand why those two results matter.

Conjugate gradients avoids $H^{-1}$ by never forming it. The quasi-Newton family takes the other route: build an approximation to $H^{-1}$ directly, and improve it as you go. Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno is the prominent member.

Keep a matrix $M_t \approx H^{-1}$, refined by low-rank updates from the gradients you have already computed. Each iteration takes $\boldsymbol{\rho}_t = M_t \mathbf{g}_t$ as its direction and a line search for the distance.

Why BFGS can afford a sloppier line search than CG

This is the practical difference between the two and it is easy to miss.

Conjugate gradients depends on the line search finding a point very close to the true minimum along the line, as the conjugacy argument assumes. A sloppy line search breaks the property the method is built on.

BFGS does not. Its second-order information lives in $M_t$, which is accumulated from gradient differences regardless of where along each line you stopped. So it can spend far less effort per line search, and since line searches are where the function evaluations go, that is a real saving.

The obstacle is memory. $M$ is $n \times n$, so BFGS costs $O(n^2)$ storage, which for a model with millions of parameters is not merely expensive but impossible: a million parameters would need a $10^{12}$-entry matrix.

L-BFGS removes it with one assumption: rather than carrying $M^{(t-1)}$ forward, assume it is the identity at the start of each step and apply the updates from there. With exact line searches the resulting directions are mutually conjugate (so it recovers CG's property) and unlike CG it stays well-behaved when the line search is only approximate, which is BFGS's advantage retained. The storage-free version can be extended by keeping a handful of the update vectors, at $O(n)$ per step rather than $O(n^2)$.

In the framework: L-BFGS has a different API, and the reason is §20

Every optimiser in Part III §17 followed the same five statements. L-BFGS does not, and the difference is not arbitrary: it falls directly out of the line search.

opt = torch.optim.LBFGS(model.parameters(), lr=1.0, max_iter=20,
                        history_size=100,
                        line_search_fn="strong_wolfe")   # default is None

def closure():
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss = F.mse_loss(model(x), y)
    loss.backward()
    return loss

opt.step(closure)          # <- step() takes a function, not nothing

What each piece corresponds to. history_size is the number of update vectors kept, the $O(n)$-per-step extension at the end of §21; set it to zero and you have the storage-free version. max_iter is how many inner iterations one step() performs, because a single L-BFGS step already contains a loop. Fitting a three-parameter regression, one step() called the closure eight times and took the loss from 2.54 to $7.1\times10^{-3}$, a whole optimisation, inside one call.

Note the last argument. PyTorch's default is line_search_fn=None, which means no line search at all: it takes a fixed step of size lr and relies on heuristics. Given that §20 and §21 are entirely about what the line search buys, running L-BFGS without one and then concluding it does not work well is a common and avoidable mistake.

Why it is designed that way. A line search must evaluate the objective at several trial points along the direction before deciding how far to go. Every other optimiser here uses the gradient autograd already deposited and needs nothing else, hence opt.step() with no arguments. L-BFGS has to re-run the forward and backward pass itself, at points you have not visited, so you must hand it something it can call. That is the closure. The unusual API is the line search made visible.

What breaks. Three things, in the order people hit them.

Forgetting zero_grad() inside the closure. It runs many times per step(), so gradients accumulate across trial points and the search direction is nonsense, Part III §17's accumulation trap, with the frequency multiplied.

Using it with minibatches. The line search assumes the objective is the same function each time it is evaluated; with a different batch per call it is not, and the search is minimising a function that changes underneath it. L-BFGS is a full-batch method, and the closure must see the same data on every call within a step.

Expecting it on a large model. $O(n)$ per step with history_size=100 means a hundred copies of the parameter vector. That is fine for the problems L-BFGS is right for (small full-batch fits, physics-informed networks, style transfer, calibrating a handful of parameters) and not for anything with millions of weights, which is the whole content of §21's memory objection.
