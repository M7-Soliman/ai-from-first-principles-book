Part EMaking it fast

§13 measured the cost. This movement is what was done about it, and the useful lesson is that the technique which mattered most changed no asymptotics whatever, while a large literature with excellent asymptotics was not adopted.

§27 Two different problems

Before any technique, a distinction that most treatments skip and that determines which technique applies.

Training and inference are not the same computation
TrainingGeneration
SequenceAll $n$ positions at onceOne new token at a time
Dominant costArithmeticMemory bandwidth
Matrix shapesLarge, denseTall and thin, often a vector
BottleneckFLOPs, then activation memoryReading the weights and the cache

Generating one token requires reading every parameter of the model to do a small amount of arithmetic with each. The hardware is starved of data, not of compute: the exact reverse of training.

Why this distinction organises the rest of the movement

Almost every efficiency technique targets one side and not the other, and knowing which is how you avoid applying the wrong one.

Training-side: FlashAttention (§29), mixed precision, activation checkpointing, sparse attention (§31).

Inference-side: KV caching (§28), grouped-query attention (§30), quantization (§34), speculative decoding.

Both: mixture of experts (§33), which reduces active parameters per token.

And it explains the recurring surprise of §19's pitfall: a change that cuts FLOPs by nine can leave latency untouched, because latency was never about FLOPs.

§28 The KV cache

Generate token $t$, append it, generate token $t+1$. Done naively, the second step recomputes the keys and values for every previous position, which have not changed.

Caching

Keep $K$ and $V$ for every position generated so far. At each new step compute only the new position's key and value, append them, and attend.

Cost per token drops from $O(n^2 d)$ to $O(nd)$, so generating $n$ tokens goes from $O(n^3)$ to $O(n^2)$. There is no approximation: the arithmetic is identical.

Why the cache became the binding constraint

The cache is $2 \times n_{\text{layers}} \times n \times d$ values, and it grows linearly with every token generated, per concurrent request.

So a long-context deployment allocates most of its memory to a data structure that is not the model. This is why serving systems are built around cache management rather than around arithmetic: paged allocation to avoid fragmentation, sharing prefixes between requests that begin identically, and evicting or compressing old entries.

It is also why §30 exists. If the cache is the constraint, then the way to serve longer contexts is to make each cached entry smaller, and that is an architectural change, decided when the model is designed rather than when it is deployed.

§29 FlashAttention

In 60 seconds

The $n \times n$ score matrix is written to memory and read back several times. Never materialise it: process the sequence in tiles that fit in fast on-chip memory, and keep a running softmax. Same arithmetic, same output to the bit, and memory drops from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n)$.

Go and read

Dao et al. (2022), FlashAttention: fast and memory-efficient exact attention with IO-awareness. The word exact in the title is the whole argument. Read §2 for the memory hierarchy and §3 for the tiling, and notice what is absent: no approximation, no asymptotic improvement in arithmetic, and it succeeded where a decade of cleverer algorithms did not. The resource that mattered was never the one being counted.

This is the technique that mattered, and it is instructive precisely because it is not an approximation and not an asymptotic improvement in compute.

Why the bottleneck was never the arithmetic

An accelerator has a small amount of very fast on-chip memory and a large amount of much slower high-bandwidth memory, with the gap in bandwidth being an order of magnitude or more. This is an arithmetic-intensity argument, FLOPs performed per byte moved, applied to a single kernel rather than a whole forward pass; Part XIX §3 develops it properly, with the roofline it implies, once inference has a chapter of its own.

A standard attention implementation computes $S = QK^\top$ and writes $n^2$ values to slow memory. Then reads them back to softmax. Writes them again. Reads them again to multiply by $V$. The arithmetic is $O(n^2d)$; the memory traffic is $O(n^2)$, and at realistic sizes the traffic is what you wait for.

FlashAttention never writes the matrix. It loads a tile of $Q$ and a tile of $K, V$, does the arithmetic on-chip, updates a running output and running softmax statistics, and moves on. The score matrix exists only in fast memory, one tile at a time.

The trick that makes it work: online softmax

A softmax appears to need all its inputs: you cannot normalise before you have seen every term. The standard stable form subtracts the maximum (Part III §2), which also seems to need everything.

It does not. Keep a running maximum $m$ and a running sum $\ell$. On a new tile with maximum $m'$, rescale what you have by $e^{m - m_{\text{new}}}$ and add the new terms scaled by $e^{m' - m_{\text{new}}}$. The same correction applies to the accumulated output.

Exact, numerically stable, and one pass. The whole technique rests on this.

Why this is the most instructive optimisation in the part

It computes the same function (bitwise, up to floating-point summation order) and it made long context practical where a decade of cleverer algorithms did not.

The lesson generalises past attention: the asymptotic cost model that says $O(n^2 d)$ arithmetic is the thing to reduce was measuring the wrong resource. Once the traffic was addressed, the quadratic arithmetic turned out to be affordable at lengths nobody had thought reachable.

Which puts §§31–32's approximations in an uncomfortable position, and §32 says so plainly.

§30 Multi-query and grouped-query attention

§28 established the cache as the constraint. This is the architectural response.

Sharing keys and values across heads

Multi-head: $h$ query heads, $h$ key heads, $h$ value heads. Cache size proportional to $h$.

Multi-query: $h$ query heads, one key head and one value head, shared by all. Cache size divided by $h$.

Grouped-query: $h$ query heads sharing $g$ key–value heads, with $1 < g < h$. Cache divided by $h/g$.

The queries, where each head looks, stay independent. Only what they retrieve is shared.

Multi-query cuts the cache by the head count and costs a little quality. Grouped-query, with $g$ around 8, recovers nearly all of it while keeping most of the saving, and is the current default in large models. It is an unusually clean example of a design decided by a deployment constraint reaching back into the architecture.

§31 Sparse and local attention

If the $n \times n$ matrix is the problem, compute only part of it. Every scheme is a choice of which entries to keep.

The standard patterns
  • Local or sliding window. Each position attends to the $w$ nearest. Cost $O(nw)$. Stacked, the receptive field grows with depth, which is Part X §10 exactly, including the $\sqrt{L}$ effective-field problem.
  • Strided. Attend to every $k$-th position, so a few layers reach everything.
  • Global tokens. A handful of positions attend to all and are attended by all, giving every pair a two-hop path.
  • Combinations. Most practical schemes are local plus a few global tokens.
Pitfall: sparse attention gives back what §8 bought

The reason to use attention at all was §8: a path length of 1 between any two positions. Local attention has a path length of $O(n/w)$ and needs depth to close it.

So a sliding-window transformer is, structurally, a convolutional network with learned weights, and it inherits Part X's limitations, including the gap between theoretical and effective receptive field. That is a real trade and it is often worth making; it is not the free lunch the asymptotics suggest.

The honest position: sparse patterns are used in practice, usually as some layers rather than all, with full attention retained in the rest so at least one $O(1)$ path exists.

§32 Linear attention

A more ambitious line removes the quadratic term entirely, and the mechanism is one this book has already derived.

Worked: where the quadratic term actually comes from

Attention computes $\text{softmax}(QK^\top)V$. The parenthesisation is forced: the softmax sits between $QK^\top$ and $V$, so the $n \times n$ matrix must be formed.

Replace the softmax with a kernel $\phi(\mathbf{q})^\top\phi(\mathbf{k})$ and the normalisation with a sum. Now there is no nonlinearity in the middle, so you may reassociate:

$$\big(\phi(Q)\,\phi(K)^\top\big)V \;=\; \phi(Q)\,\big(\phi(K)^\top V\big)$$

The right-hand form computes a $d \times d$ matrix first. Cost $O(nd^2)$: linear in $n$. The quadratic term was never intrinsic to attention; it came from the softmax standing in the way.

Why this is Part VI §15's kernel trick, backwards, and why it lost anyway

Part VI §15's kernel trick avoided computing an explicit feature map by working with inner products. This does the opposite: it replaces the implicit kernel with an explicit feature map in order to reassociate. Same identity, run in the other direction.

And there is a second reading worth having. $\phi(K)^\top V$ is a fixed-size matrix summarising everything seen so far, updated additively as the sequence proceeds. That is a recurrent state, so linear attention is a recurrent network, and Part XI §28's bottleneck is exactly what it reintroduces.

Which is why, despite excellent asymptotics, these methods have not displaced softmax attention. They trade the property that made attention work (an unbounded, query-dependent view of the whole past) for a fixed-size summary. §40 returns to this, because the modern state space models are the same trade made much more carefully.

§33 Mixture of experts

Go and read

Shazeer et al. (2017), Outrageously large neural networks: the sparsely-gated mixture-of-experts layer. Conditional computation, with the load-balancing problem confronted rather than mentioned. Note where the experts are placed: the feedforward layer, because §15 says that is where the parameters are. Then Shazeer (2019), Fast transformer decoding, two pages of multi-query attention and an unusually clean case of a serving constraint reaching back into the architecture.

§15 established that two thirds of the parameters are in the feedforward layer. This is what follows if you take that seriously.

Conditional computation

Replace the single feedforward layer with $E$ of them, experts, and a small router that, per token, selects $k$ of them (typically $k = 1$ or $2$). Only the selected experts run.

Total parameters scale with $E$; compute per token scales with $k$. A model with 8× the parameters can cost the same per token as one without.

Why this decouples two things that had been welded together

Part XVII's scaling laws relate performance to parameters and to compute. Dense models tie them: every parameter participates in every token, so more parameters means proportionally more compute.

MoE breaks the tie. Capacity and cost become separate dials, which matters enormously when serving cost is the binding constraint rather than training cost.

The costs are real and they are not FLOPs. All experts must be in memory even though few run, so the memory footprint is the dense one. Routing must be balanced or a few experts receive everything, which needs an auxiliary loss and remains fiddly. And routing is discrete, which is Part XI §26's problem: the top-$k$ selection has no gradient, and the standard fix, weighting the outputs by the router's probabilities, is a soft relaxation of exactly the kind attention itself is.

§34 Quantization

Part III §1 established the precision–range trade and Part IV met bfloat16. Inference pushes further: 8 bits, 4 bits, sometimes fewer.

Two regimes

Post-training quantization. Take a trained model and round its weights. Cheap, and the quality loss becomes serious below 8 bits without care.

Quantization-aware training. Simulate the rounding during training so the model adapts. Better results, at the cost of retraining.

Weights and activations are separate decisions. Weight-only quantization is easier and often sufficient, because §27 established that generation is bandwidth-bound: halving the bytes read per parameter nearly halves the time, whatever the arithmetic precision.

Pitfall: outlier features, and why attention is the hard part

Quantization assumes values are spread reasonably within their range. Transformer activations violate this badly: a small number of feature dimensions carry values orders of magnitude larger than the rest, and they appear reliably in the same dimensions across inputs.

Quantise naively and the range is set by the outliers, so everything else collapses into a few levels. The standard remedies keep the outlier dimensions in higher precision, or migrate the scale from activations into weights before quantising.

The attention logits are the other sensitive point, for §4's reason: they feed a softmax, and a softmax is sensitive to differences that quantisation error can swamp. Most schemes keep the softmax and the accumulation in higher precision even when everything around them is in four bits: Part III §2's stability argument, still binding at the far end of the stack.
