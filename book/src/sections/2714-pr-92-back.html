BuildThe project

Eight to twelve hours. Someone hands you four broken projects and tells you nothing. You diagnose each from the instruments alone, in order, recording what you ruled out and what it cost.

P.1 What you are building

diagnose: a reusable instrument panel, and four sabotaged training runs to point it at.

The rule: for each broken run, write down your hypothesis before the next measurement. A diagnosis you reconstruct afterwards is not a diagnosis, and the discipline this project teaches is entirely in the ordering.

P.2 Stage 1: the panel

Build each as a function you can point at any run:

  1. Evaluation check. Score the model on training data through the test-time path. Separates a bad model from a broken measurement.
  2. Tiny-fit. Ten examples, regularization off, assert zero training error.
  3. Gradient check. Central difference with a swept $\epsilon$, the random-projection trick for vector functions, and a complex-step version where the operation is analytic.
  4. Train/test table. §10's four cases, reported as a verdict rather than two numbers.
  5. Sorted mistakes. Training examples ranked by the probability of the correct answer.
  6. Instruments. Per-layer gradient norms, dead-unit fraction, and the update-to-parameter ratio.
  7. Calibration. Reliability diagram, expected calibration error, and a fitted temperature.

Deliverable: seven functions with a common interface, each documenting what it does not detect.

P.3 Stage 2: four sabotaged runs

Have someone else (or a script you then forget the seed of) introduce exactly one of these into each of four copies:

  • A bug: labels shifted by one, a detached gradient, a wrong reduction axis.
  • Underfitting: capacity or learning rate far too low.
  • Overfitting: capacity far too high, regularization off, small training set.
  • A data defect: a subset corrupted, or a shortcut feature added (Part XIII §8).

Deliverable: for each run, an ordered log (hypothesis, measurement, what it ruled out, next hypothesis) ending in the diagnosis and the fix. Record how many rungs of §34 you needed. The bug and the data defect should be reachable in under fifteen minutes; if either took an hour, the log will show which instrument you skipped.

P.4 Stage 3: the search, honestly

  1. Run a random search over at least 50 configurations, keeping every validation score.
  2. Report the best validation score, then evaluate the winner on a test set used nowhere in the selection.
  3. Plot the best-so-far validation score against the number of configurations tried.
  4. Repeat the whole search with a different seed and compare the winners.

Deliverable: the gap between the best validation score and the honest test score, stated as a number. That gap is §20's inflation, measured on your own experiment rather than simulated, and step four tells you whether the "best" configuration is even stable.

P.5 Stage 4: grid, random, halving

  1. Implement all three at matched total budget.
  2. Compare on a problem where one hyperparameter dominates, sweeping the number of hyperparameters.
  3. Find the budget at which halving stops being identical to random.

Deliverable: Figures 5 and 6 from your own code, including the region where halving does not win. Reporting only the favourable region is the failure mode this stage exists to inoculate against.

P.6 Stage 5: is the difference real

  1. Take two configurations whose validation scores differ by a little.
  2. Train each with ten seeds. Report means and spreads.
  3. State whether the difference survives, using Part V §16.
  4. Then construct two configurations that are genuinely identical and confirm your procedure declares no difference.

Deliverable: the smallest difference your experimental setup could detect, given your seed variance and the number of runs you can afford. That number governs every comparison you will make afterwards, and almost nobody knows theirs.

P.7 Stretch

  • Complex-step everywhere. Take a network of analytic operations and gradient-check the whole thing at $\epsilon = 10^{-100}$. Then add a ReLU and watch it break, and explain why.
  • Both ablations on a three-component system, with and without re-tuning.
  • A judge, validated. Grade outputs with a model, then measure its agreement with your own labels and test it for position and length bias.
  • Monitoring. Build §27's monitors, inject a shifted input distribution and a changed upstream encoding, and measure detection latency for each.
  • A feedback loop, simulated, with and without a randomised holdout.

P.8 Done when

  • Seven instruments exist, each documenting what it cannot see.
  • All four sabotaged runs are diagnosed, with an ordered log for each.
  • The gap between best-validation and honest-test is a number you measured.
  • You know the budget at which halving starts to beat random, on your problem.
  • You know the smallest difference your setup can detect.
  • Your gradient check runs at $\epsilon$ small enough that a finite difference would fail.
  • Your README names one run where you reached for a fix before you had a diagnosis.
Why this project

Stage 2 is the reason. Diagnosing a fault you did not plant, with instruments you built, in an order you committed to in advance, is the only way to find out whether you actually work this way or merely agree with it.

And Stage 5's number (the smallest difference you could detect) changes how you read everything afterwards. Most people have never computed it, which is why most reported improvements are of unknown size relative to the noise of the experiment that produced them.

The one thing to carry forward

Diagnose before treating.

Every remedy in this book treats a specific condition, and applied to the wrong one it moves you backwards while looking like progress. Regularization treats variance and worsens bias. More data treats variance and does nothing for underfitting. A bigger model treats underfitting and worsens overfitting.

The instruments that identify the condition are cheap: two error numbers, ten examples, a sorted list of mistakes, five seeds. The discipline is remembering to look before reaching, and the ladder of §34 is that discipline written down so it does not depend on remembering.
