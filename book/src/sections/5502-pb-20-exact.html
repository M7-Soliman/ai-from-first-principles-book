Part BExact inference, and the models that permit it

For a small class of structures the posterior can be computed exactly, in polynomial time, with no approximation anywhere. That class is worth knowing precisely: partly because those models are still deployed, and mostly because everything in the next two movements is what you do when your model falls outside it.

§9 The query, and the three kinds

What "inference" is asking for

Marginal. $p(x_i \mid \mathbf{e})$: the distribution of one variable given the evidence. This is what a filter, a classifier and a posterior all want.

Most probable configuration. $\arg\max_{\mathbf{x}} p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{e})$: the single best joint assignment, which is not the assignment of each variable's most probable value and can have probability zero under it.

Evidence. $p(\mathbf{e})$: the normalising constant, needed for model comparison and for learning.

The first and third have the same algorithm with different bookkeeping; the second replaces every sum with a max.

§10 Variable elimination

Distribute the sum

The naive marginal sums the joint over every configuration of everything else. The observation that saves it is that sums distribute over products: a factor not involving the variable being summed can be pulled outside the sum.

$$\sum_{x_3} \psi_1(x_1,x_2)\,\psi_2(x_2,x_3) \;=\; \psi_1(x_1,x_2) \sum_{x_3} \psi_2(x_2,x_3)$$

Eliminate variables one at a time, each time multiplying together the factors that mention it and summing it out, leaving a new factor. The cost is set by the largest intermediate factor created, and the order of elimination decides how large that gets.

§11 Treewidth, and the wall

The one number that decides whether exact inference is possible

The size of the largest intermediate factor, minimised over elimination orders, is one more than the graph's treewidth: treewidth is defined as that size minus one, so that a tree's chain-like elimination, which never creates a factor over more than two variables, comes out to 1 rather than 2. Exact inference costs exponential time in the treewidth, and nothing better is available: finding the optimal order is itself hard, and the exponential dependence cannot be removed.

Treewidth is 1 for a tree, small for a chain, and grows with the side length of a grid, which is why exact inference is routine on a sequence and impossible on an image. A model's tractability is a property of its graph, decided before any data arrives, and it is worth computing before choosing an algorithm.

§12 Sequences: the forward–backward algorithm

Go and read

Rabiner (1989), A tutorial on hidden Markov models. Still the clearest exposition of forward–backward. Read it for the scaling that stops the recursion underflowing: most modern treatments omit it and every implementer rediscovers it.

A hidden Markov model

A chain of latent states, each depending only on its predecessor, each emitting one observation:

$$p(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}) \;=\; p(z_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T} p(z_t \mid z_{t-1}) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid z_t)$$

Variable elimination on a chain has a name. The forward pass accumulates $\alpha_t(z) = p(z_t = z, x_{1:t})$, the backward pass accumulates $\beta_t(z) = p(x_{t+1:T} \mid z_t = z)$, and their product is the posterior over each state. Cost $O(TK^2)$ against $K^T$ for enumerating paths.

§13 Linear-Gaussian: the Kalman filter

The continuous case that stays closed

Replace the HMM's discrete states with continuous ones, linear transitions and Gaussian noise:

$$\mathbf{z}_t = F\mathbf{z}_{t-1} + \mathbf{w}_t, \qquad \mathbf{x}_t = H\mathbf{z}_t + \mathbf{v}_t$$

Gaussians are closed under linear maps and under conditioning, so every posterior in the recursion is Gaussian and the whole filter is bookkeeping on a mean and a covariance. The predict step pushes both through $F$ and adds process noise; the update step conditions on the observation.

The Kalman gain is the weight given to the new observation against the prediction, and it is not a tuning parameter: it is what the arithmetic of conditioning produces. Figure 2's right panel is the check: the filter is the posterior, to floating point.

Three things to know before using one

Smoothing beats filtering when you can wait. The filter conditions on the past; the backward pass adds the future, and the resulting estimate is strictly better.

Nonlinear variants are approximations and stop being exact. The extended filter linearises and the unscented filter propagates sigma points; both can diverge, and neither carries the guarantee this section is about.

Particle filters handle arbitrary dynamics by sampling, at the cost of movement D's variance problem, and they degenerate in high dimensions for the reason §26 gives.

§14 Learning with hidden variables

Why maximum likelihood stops being easy

With everything observed, maximum likelihood for these models is counting. With latent variables the likelihood contains a sum inside a logarithm ($\log \sum_z p(x, z \mid \theta)$), which does not decompose, and the closed-form solution disappears.

The observation that rescues it: if you knew the latent values, the problem would be easy again. So alternate between inferring them and re-fitting as though they were observed. That is the next section, and its guarantee is better than the description suggests.

§15 EM, and what it guarantees

Go and read

Dempster, Laird & Rubin (1977), Maximum likelihood from incomplete data via the EM algorithm. The monotonicity proof, which is short and is the whole guarantee. Read it beside §18's bound and notice that they are the same inequality.

Expectation–maximisation

E step: compute the posterior over latents under the current parameters. M step: maximise the expected complete-data log-likelihood under that posterior.

The guarantee is that each iteration cannot decrease the observed-data likelihood. It comes from the same inequality as movement C's: the E step makes a lower bound on the likelihood tight at the current parameters, and the M step raises the bound, so the likelihood is raised at least as much. EM is coordinate ascent on the objective of §18, which is why the two movements are the same subject.

Why the failure rate rises with separation

The intuition says overlapping components are harder, and for estimation that is right: it is why the two hollow points exist. For optimisation it is backwards, and the mechanism is worth holding on to.

Well-separated components create genuinely distinct basins. A restart that happens to put both means inside one cluster cannot escape, because moving a mean across the gap means crossing territory where the likelihood is worse. When the components overlap the surface is nearly unimodal and there is nowhere to get stuck.

The practical reading: random restarts are not optional, and the temptation to skip them on an easy-looking, well-separated problem is exactly backwards.
