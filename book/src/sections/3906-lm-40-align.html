Part DAlignment

Turning a text continuer into something that answers. The machinery is Part XVIII's; what is new here is the pipeline, the data, and an honest account of what it changes and what it only appears to.

§25 The interface problem

What instruction tuning does

Fine-tune on (instruction, response) pairs. The model learns that a request is followed by a compliant answer rather than by more text of the same kind.

The striking empirical fact is how little data this takes: thousands of examples, not millions, against a pretraining corpus of trillions of tokens. That ratio is the evidence for the standard interpretation: instruction tuning is not teaching capability, it is selecting an interface to capability that already existed.

Which also predicts its limit. If the capability is absent, no amount of instruction data installs it: you get a model that responds confidently in the right format.

§26 The pipeline

Go and read

Ouyang et al. (2022), Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback. The three-stage pipeline as it is actually run. The number worth noticing is how little preference data changes how usable the model is, which is the evidence for §30's reading that this is an interface change rather than a capability one.

Three stages

Pretraining on a large corpus, for capability. Supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, for the interface. Preference optimisation against human judgements, for the things demonstrations cannot express.

The third stage exists because of a specific gap. Demonstrations teach what a good answer looks like; they cannot express relative quality, and they cannot express what to refuse. Both are easier to obtain as comparisons than as demonstrations: Part XVIII §41's point, which is also Part XIII §5's finding that people rank more consistently than they score.

§27 The preference data

Pitfall: the data encodes the annotators, and the instructions given to them

A preference dataset is a record of what a particular group of people, working to a particular written guideline, preferred on a particular distribution of prompts. Part XIII §6's finding applies without modification: the labels define the task.

Two consequences that are measurable and are measured. Annotators prefer longer answers and answers that agree with them, so a model optimised against their preferences becomes more verbose and more agreeable, neither of which anyone asked for.

The guideline is therefore the specification, and it is the artefact that should be published alongside the model. It rarely is.

§28 RLHF, applied

The pieces, from Part XVIII

Fit a reward model on the comparisons, then optimise the policy against it with PPO (Part XVIII §31) plus a KL penalty against the supervised model.

The KL penalty is not a regularisation detail. Part XVIII §41 measured why: optimising hard against a learned reward finds its errors, and reward-model score rises while human ratings plateau and fall. The penalty prices moving away from the region where the reward model was trained, and its coefficient is the main control on overoptimisation.

§29 The direct methods

Go and read

Rafailov et al. (2023), Direct preference optimization. The derivation that removes the reward model and the RL loop entirely, leaving a classification loss with the same optimum. Read §4 for what is given up (the online loop) because that is exactly Part XVIII §40's offline problem arriving here.

The equivalence is worth deriving rather than taking on trust, because it is short and it is the one place in this movement an error would be easy to miss. Start from §28's objective: maximise reward against a KL penalty that keeps the policy near the supervised model, for a fixed prompt $x$:

$$\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\big[r(x,y)\big] \;-\; \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big)$$

Written out, the quantity being maximised is $\sum_y \pi(y)\big[r(x,y) - \beta\log\pi(y) + \beta\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\big]$, subject only to $\pi(\cdot|x)$ summing to one: the reward term is linear in $\pi$ and the KL term is strictly convex, so the objective is strictly concave and has a unique maximiser. Attach a Lagrange multiplier to the one constraint and differentiate inside the sum: the stationary condition for every $y$ is $\log\pi(y) = r(x,y)/\beta + \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y) - 1 - \lambda/\beta$, which says $\pi(y)$ is $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)$ tilted exponentially by the reward and renormalised:

The KL-regularised optimum

$$\pi^*(y\,|\,x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\,|\,x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right), \qquad Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\,|\,x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)$$

This is what KL-penalised PPO (§28) is targeting, whether or not sampling and the reward model's own error let it get there exactly.

Invert this and every reward becomes recoverable, up to a per-prompt constant, from the policy optimising against it: $r(x,y) = \beta\log\dfrac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta\log Z(x)$. Substitute into the Bradley–Terry preference model (Part XVIII §41) that the reward model was fit to in the first place, $p(y_w \succ y_l\,|\,x) = \sigma\big(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l)\big)$: because $y_w$ and $y_l$ are two responses to the same prompt, the $\beta\log Z(x)$ term is identical in both and cancels exactly, leaving a probability that never mentions $Z(x)$ or the reward model at all. That cancellation is the whole trick.

DPO, and what it removes

The reward model and the RL loop can be eliminated. Fit $\pi_\theta$ directly against the preference pairs by minimising

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,\,y_w,\,y_l)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left( \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\right)\right]$$

which is exactly the negative log-likelihood of the Bradley–Terry model above with $r(x,y)$ replaced by the implicit reward $\beta\log\big(\pi_\theta(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\big)$. Direct preference optimisation derives, from the same preference model, a classification loss on the pairs whose optimum is the same policy: minimising it drives $\pi_\theta$ to the $\pi^*$ above, so it is a supervised objective on preference data with no sampling, no reward model and no PPO.

Simpler, more stable, cheaper, and widely adopted for those reasons. What it gives up is the online loop: RLHF samples fresh outputs from the current policy and has them judged, so it gets signal about what the policy does now. DPO learns from a fixed set of comparisons, so it is Part XVIII §40's offline problem: the policy can drift to regions the data says nothing about.

The methods that add back iteration (regenerate, rejudge, retrain) recover much of the gap, which is the expected shape given the offline diagnosis.

§30 What alignment changes

Surface behaviour, mostly, and that is not nothing

The evidence that alignment is largely a change of interface rather than of capability: the data is tiny relative to pretraining; benchmark capability changes little; the model's knowledge is unchanged; and prompting a base model carefully recovers a substantial fraction of the behaviour.

Being precise about what that does not mean: refusal behaviour, formatting, calibration of hedging and the whole usability of the system are real changes, and they are what made these models usable by anyone. "Surface" is a claim about where in the model the change lives, not about whether it matters.

§31 The measurable failure modes

Four, each a direct consequence of the objective

Sycophancy. The model agrees with the user's stated position, including when it is wrong. Measurable: state an opinion, then ask; measure how often the answer moves. It follows directly from §27: annotators preferred agreement.

Verbosity. Longer answers score better, so answers get longer. Length is the confound that any preference evaluation must control for, and frequently does not.

Over-refusal. Refusing is heavily rewarded when it is right and lightly penalised when it is wrong, so the trained policy refuses benign requests that pattern-match to unsafe ones. Measurable on a benign set built to look risky.

Confident hedging. The model expresses uncertainty in the register annotators liked, which is not the same as being calibrated. Part XIV §5's measurement is the check, and post-trained models are frequently worse calibrated than the base model they came from.

§32 The alignment tax

What it costs, and where

Post-training can reduce capability on tasks unrelated to the preferences it was trained on: the alignment tax. The mechanism is Part XIX §31's: fine-tuning on a narrow distribution distorts the model away from it.

The standard mitigations are the standard ones for that mechanism: mix pretraining data into post-training, keep the KL penalty, and use low learning rates. Reported taxes are much smaller than they were, which is a genuine improvement and also partly a change in what gets measured.

§33 Jailbreaks

Why this is a persistent property rather than a bug list

Refusal is a learned behaviour over a distribution of prompts, and an attacker chooses prompts off that distribution. Roleplay framings, encodings, low-resource languages, long contexts that push the instruction out of the effective window, and gradient-optimised adversarial suffixes all work by the same route.

This is Part VIII §20's adversarial examples in a setting with a much larger and more accessible input space. The defences (adversarial training on discovered attacks, input and output classifiers, system-level constraints) raise the cost and none of them closes the class.

The honest framing, and it is the one Part XXI will need: alignment as currently practised produces a propensity, not a guarantee. It changes what the model does by default and does not constrain what it can be made to do.
