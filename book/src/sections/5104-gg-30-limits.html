Part CWhat message passing cannot do

The family of movement B has an exact upper bound on what it can distinguish, and it is not a loose one: it is a classical graph-isomorphism heuristic from 1968. This movement establishes the bound, demonstrates it on two graphs of six nodes, and covers the second structural failure, which is about distance rather than expressivity.

§16 The colour-refinement algorithm

Go and read

Xu et al. (2019), How powerful are graph neural networks? The Weisfeiler–Leman bound proved below, and the reason sum aggregation is an expressivity choice rather than a preference. The proof is short and the consequence is the only exact capability limit in this book.

One-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman

Give every node the same colour. Repeatedly, replace each node's colour with a hash of its own colour together with the multiset of its neighbours' colours. Stop when the partition stops changing. Two graphs whose final colour multisets differ are certainly not isomorphic; if the multisets agree, the test is inconclusive.

Compare that with §8's loop. Node state, neighbourhood multiset, update: it is the same procedure with a learned hash. That correspondence is exact, and it is the source of the bound.

The theorem

A message-passing network is at most as powerful as 1-WL at distinguishing graphs: if colour refinement cannot separate two graphs, no message-passing network can, at any depth, with any weights.

And it is tight: with an injective aggregator, the network is exactly as powerful. Sum is injective on multisets and mean and max are not, which is why §12's choice is an expressivity question rather than a preference.

§17 The bound, demonstrated

Why zero and not merely small

Every node in both graphs starts identical and has an identical neighbourhood multiset at every round, so the two computations are the same computation. The difference is zero for the same reason $x - x$ is zero, not because the numbers happened to agree.

This is worth dwelling on because it is a rare thing in this book: a capability limit that is provable and exact, rather than a measured tendency. Part XXIII found the classical bounds vacuous; this one bites.

§18 What the limit costs in practice

Three things a message-passing network cannot count

Triangles, and cycles generally. Which matters for molecules, where a ring is a chemical fact, and for social graphs, where a closed triangle is a real relationship.

Regular substructures. Any two $k$-regular graphs on the same number of nodes are indistinguishable without features.

Distinguishing nodes that are structurally identical. In a symmetric graph two nodes in equivalent positions get equal representations, always, which is correct if you wanted equivariance and wrong if you wanted to tell them apart.

§19 Three ways past it

And what each gives up

Better features. Precompute what the loop cannot: cycle counts, distances to landmarks, spectral coordinates. Cheap, effective, and it fixes the specific gap you anticipated rather than the family's limit.

Random node identifiers. Give each node a random vector, which breaks every tie. Now the network is more expressive and no longer invariant (the same graph gives different answers), so invariance has to be recovered by averaging over draws, which costs samples.

Higher-order message passing. Pass messages between pairs or triples of nodes rather than nodes, climbing the WL hierarchy. Strictly more expressive, and the cost goes from linear in edges to polynomial in nodes.

The pattern is the one from Part XXIII §21: every step up in power is bought with an assumption given up or a cost taken on. There is no free expressivity.

§20 Over-squashing

Go and read

Alon & Yahav (2021), On the bottleneck of graph neural networks and its practical implications. Over-squashing named and measured. Read it beside §13's over-smoothing, because the two failures pull in opposite directions and a fix for one usually worsens the other.

Over-squashing

A node's receptive field grows exponentially with depth while its representation stays a fixed-width vector. Everything in that growing neighbourhood must be compressed into the same number of dimensions, so a distant node's contribution is diluted in proportion to how many other nodes are competing.

It is the opposite constraint from over-smoothing and they bite together: too few layers and a distant node is unreachable; too many and every node says the same thing. Between them they are the reason long-range dependence on graphs is hard, and both are properties of the architecture rather than of the optimiser.

§21 Bottlenecks, and rewiring

The graph's own shape is the problem

Figure 4's second graph is the standard construction: two trees joined at the root, so every message between the halves crosses a single edge. That edge has to carry an exponential number of node pairs' worth of information, and it cannot.

The graph-theoretic quantity is negative curvature (edges whose removal disconnects many pairs), and the standard repairs rewire: add edges to relieve the bottleneck, either by a curvature-guided rule or by connecting every node to a small number of random others.

Every rewiring changes the graph, which was the input. That is a real cost, not a technicality: a model that has been given extra edges is answering a question about a different graph, and whether that is acceptable depends on whether the edges meant something.

§22 Graph transformers

The limit case of rewiring

If bottlenecks are the problem, remove them entirely: let every node attend to every other, and supply the structure as a positional encoding instead of as a restriction. That is a graph transformer, and it is exactly Part XII's architecture with the adjacency moved from the mask into the features.

It escapes both failures (no diffusion and no bottleneck) and pays Part XII §13's quadratic cost, which on a graph with a hundred thousand nodes is prohibitive. It also gives up the sparsity prior: the model must now learn from data that non-adjacent nodes are usually irrelevant, which message passing asserted for free.

The encodings that work are spectral: eigenvectors of the Laplacian, which are the graph's analogue of the sinusoids of Part XII §10, and are permutation-equivariant by the argument of §3.

§23 Which of these you actually have

A diagnostic, before reaching for a fix

The two failures are distinguishable and the repairs are opposite, so identify which you have.

Over-smoothing: measure the mean pairwise distance between node representations as depth grows. If it falls toward zero, that is your problem, and the answer is fewer layers or residual connections.

Over-squashing: check whether the task needs information from far away, and whether your graph has a bottleneck. If accuracy is poor with enough depth to reach, adding depth will not help and rewiring might.

The WL bound: check whether the pairs your model confuses are structurally indistinguishable. If they are, no amount of training helps and the answer is features.

§24 What movement C establishes

Three things

The family has an exact expressive ceiling, equal to a 1968 isomorphism heuristic. Figure 3 measures two non-isomorphic graphs receiving identical representations to exactly zero, at every depth.

There is a second, independent failure about distance. Figure 4 measures a source's contribution falling by a factor of 6,561 across a path and considerably faster through a bottleneck.

The two pull in opposite directions, which is why long-range dependence on graphs is genuinely hard, and why the diagnostic in §23 comes before the repair.
