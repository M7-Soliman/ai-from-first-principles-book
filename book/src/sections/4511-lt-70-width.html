Part GWidth

Movements A to F took the sample size to infinity and asked what happens. This one takes the width to infinity instead, and the answer is stranger and more useful: the network becomes exactly solvable, and the solvable version cannot do the thing that makes networks work. What survives the disappointment is the reason a hyperparameter tuned on a small model transfers to a large one, a result Part XVII §34 has been using since it was written, without the theory that says why.

§46 A different limit

Which quantity goes to infinity

Every bound in this part so far has been an $n\to\infty$ statement: as the sample grows, the empirical risk concentrates on the true risk, at a rate set by how much freedom the hypothesis class had. §23 to §30 showed that argument going numerically vacuous exactly where modern models live.

Take $m\to\infty$ instead: the width of a hidden layer, with the sample fixed. Two things happen that have no analogue in the sample limit. The network's initial function becomes a Gaussian process, exactly. And its training dynamics become linear in the parameters, so gradient descent on a neural network becomes kernel regression, which is a closed form from Part I.

This is not a bound. It is an identity in a limit, which makes it a completely different kind of object from anything in movements A to F, and it fails in a completely different way.

§47 The neural tangent kernel

Linearise around the initialisation

Write $f(x;\theta)$ for the network. First-order Taylor expansion in the parameters around $\theta_0$:

$$f(x;\theta) \approx f(x;\theta_0) + \nabla_\theta f(x;\theta_0)^{\!\top}(\theta-\theta_0)$$

which is a linear model in the features $\phi(x)=\nabla_\theta f(x;\theta_0)$. Its kernel is

$$\Theta(x,x') = \nabla_\theta f(x;\theta_0)^{\!\top}\nabla_\theta f(x';\theta_0)$$

the neural tangent kernel. Two facts make it more than an approximation. As $m\to\infty$ with the right parameterisation, $\Theta$ converges to a deterministic limit: it stops depending on the random initialisation. And it stays constant during training, so the linearisation is not just accurate at $\theta_0$ but along the whole trajectory.

Grant those two and gradient descent on the network has a closed-form solution: it is gradient descent on a linear model, which converges to the kernel regression solution, whose test predictions you can write down. Every question about generalisation becomes a question about the spectrum of a fixed matrix, territory Part I already covers. That is an enormous amount of structure to get for free, and it is why the result mattered.

§48 How constant is constant

A limit statement is not a statement about your network

"The NTK is constant during training" is true and is about $m=\infty$. At width 2,048, wider than the hidden dimension of many deployed models, the kernel moves by 4.8%, and at width 16 it moves by more than its own size, which is not a perturbation at all.

Two things follow. Predictions derived from the constant-kernel assumption should be treated as a leading-order term with a 1/√$m$-ish correction, not as a description. And the direction of the correction matters: the kernel does not drift randomly, it moves toward the data, which is the next section's subject and the entire reason finite networks beat their own infinite-width limits.

§49 Lazy and rich

The regime is a property of the scale, not the architecture

Multiply a network's output by a constant $\alpha$. Nothing about the architecture changes, but the function now moves $\alpha$ times as far for the same parameter change, so reaching a given loss requires the parameters to move $1/\alpha$ as far. Large $\alpha$ gives the lazy regime: parameters barely move, the linearisation is exact, and the network is kernel regression. Small $\alpha$ gives the rich regime, where the features themselves change.

Width does the same thing implicitly, which is the connection: the standard infinite-width limits are lazy limits, and that is why they are solvable.

What "does not learn features" means, as a number

The alignment column is the measurement worth having. The lazy network fits the training data exactly as well as the rich one and does it without ever finding the direction the data depends on. It is not learning a worse representation; it is not learning a representation. Kernel regression with a fixed kernel is precisely a method whose features were chosen before it saw the data, and the infinite-width limit says a wide network is that method.

So the NTK cannot be the explanation of why deep learning works. It is an exactly solvable model of a network that has had the interesting behaviour removed, which is still worth a great deal, in the way that the harmonic oscillator is worth a great deal, and is worth nothing at all if you forget which one you are holding.

Laziness costs compute too, which is not usually mentioned. Reaching the same training loss took 17× more steps at the lazy end of Figure 9 than at the rich end. The regime that generalises worse is also the slower one to train.

§50 Three parameterisations

Where the width shows up decides what the limit is

Standard. Initialise every layer with variance 1/fan-in, put no width factor in the forward pass, use one learning rate everywhere. What almost everybody does.

NTK. Initialise at unit variance and put $1/\sqrt{m}$ explicitly in the forward pass. Constructed so the kernel has a finite limit, and it does, at the price of §49: the features stop moving.

μP (maximal update). Three changes together. The output layer is initialised at $1/m$, so the network starts at the zero function. The output layer's learning rate falls like $1/m$. And the input layer's learning rate grows like $m$. The combination is chosen so that activations, and their updates, are $\Theta(1)$ in width, the largest updates that do not blow up, which is what "maximal" names.

Why all three parts of μP are needed

The third one is the easy one to omit and it is the one that matters. With the output layer small and slow and nothing compensating on the input side, the features move like $1/m$, which makes μP lazier than NTK parameterisation, the precise opposite of the property it exists to have. The scheme is not a list of tricks with an independently useful subset; it is one solution to one system of constraints, and two thirds of it is worse than none.

§51 The coordinate check

The diagnostic, in full

Take one optimiser step at three widths. Record the mean absolute change in each layer's activations, and in the output. Plot against width on log axes and look for flat lines.

A rising line means the update size depends on the width, so no single learning rate is correct across scales and any hyperparameter you tuned is tied to the width you tuned it at. A falling line in the feature panel means that layer is going lazy and will stop contributing as you scale.

It costs one step, not a training run, and it catches the failure before a sweep does. This is the whole of what §52 rests on: the transfer property is a consequence of the flat lines, not an additional empirical fact.

§52 Why hyperparameters transfer

What Part XVII §34 was relying on

Part XVII §34 tunes hyperparameters on a small model and uses them on a large one. Figure 11 is why that is allowed and why it is not automatic. Under standard parameterisation the answer you get from the small sweep is wrong by the width ratio, and it is wrong in the expensive direction, since the learning rate that was optimal at small scale is far too large at large scale. Every hour of small-scale tuning is then actively misleading.

Under μP the optimum sits still, so the sweep transfers. The honest bound from this measurement: residual drift is below a factor of 1.33 over an 8× width range, which is the resolution of the grid rather than zero. Transfer is a very good approximation, not an identity, and a claim of exactness should be treated as untested rather than true.

This is the movement's practical payoff. It is also an unusually clean example of theory paying for itself: the limit that turned out not to explain generalisation turned out to explain something that saves a great deal of money.

§53 Depth, and what a random network does to a signal

Two inputs, many layers

Take two inputs and push them through a randomly initialised network. Track the correlation between their representations layer by layer. It converges to a fixed point, and which fixed point depends on the initialisation variance $\sigma_w^2$.

Ordered (small $\sigma_w^2$): correlation → 1. Every input produces the same representation and the network cannot tell them apart. Chaotic (large): correlation → a fixed point well below 1, so nearby inputs are driven apart and the network forgets that similar things are similar. Between them is a critical value where the approach is slowest.

The phase diagram is not symmetric in what it costs

The third panel was meant to confirm that trainability tracks the phase diagram. It confirms half of it. The ordered phase degrades exactly as predicted: at depth 64 it reaches a training loss of 0.094 against 0.100 for predicting the mean, which is to say it does not train at all. The chaotic phase trains perfectly at every depth tested, despite a gradient scale of 10¹².

The asymmetry has a reason, and it is more useful than the tidy picture would have been. The ordered phase destroys information; the chaotic phase only destroys scale. When correlations collapse to 1, every input produces the same representation and what is gone is gone; no learning rate recovers it. An exploding gradient is a large number, and a large number can be divided by, which is what tuning the learning rate does and what gradient clipping and normalisation do more robustly.

Stated with its scope: this is one easy task at width 64 and depth up to 64. The claim supported is that exploding gradients were survivable here with a tuned rate, not that chaotic initialisation is harmless.

§54 What initialisation is choosing

Not a number, a depth scale

Initialisation is usually taught as a variance formula to be memorised: 2/fan-in for ReLU, 1/fan-in for tanh. Figure 12 says what the formula is for: it places the network at the boundary between two regimes, and the quantity it controls is how many layers information about the input survives.

That reframing explains several things at once. Why the right constant depends on the nonlinearity: the fixed-point structure does. Why very deep plain networks were untrainable before anyone had a residual connection or a normalisation layer, and why both fixes work: they change the propagation so that criticality is automatic rather than something to be tuned onto. And why scaling residual branches by $1/\sqrt{L}$ is not arbitrary: it keeps the variance from growing with depth, which is the same calculation done on a different architecture.

The through-line for this whole movement: the useful theory of deep networks is about scales (of activations, updates, gradients, and correlations) and about keeping them $\Theta(1)$ as something is taken to infinity. It is much less ambitious than a generalisation bound and it produces things that work.

§55 What to carry forward

Five things, from this movement and the six before it

Learning works because averages concentrate, and the classical bounds stop being informative exactly where modern models live. Movements A to F establish both halves; Figure 3 measures a bound of 1.1 to 10.0 on an error rate that cannot exceed 1.

The infinite-width limit is exactly solvable and cannot learn features. Figure 9 measures the alignment with the direction the target depends on falling 3.5× at identical training loss: the lazy network fits the data without finding its structure.

"Constant during training" is a limit, not a description. Figure 8 measures 4.8% kernel movement at width 2,048, falling like $m^{-0.65}$ rather than the $m^{-1/2}$ the scaling argument suggests.

μP is why a small-scale sweep transfers, and a coordinate check is how you know. Figure 10 measures the output update growing 53× with width under standard parameterisation; Figure 11 measures the optimal learning rate moving 17.8× because of it. One optimiser step at three widths catches this before a sweep does.

Initialisation chooses a depth scale, and the two ways to get it wrong are not equally bad. Figure 12 measures the gradient scale within 10× of one at exactly one variance of seven, but the ordered side destroys information and does not train, while the chaotic side only destroys scale and trains fine with a tuned rate.

Sixty seconds

Learning works because averages concentrate, and choosing a hypothesis after seeing the data costs only the logarithm of how much freedom the choice had. That argument is exact, and it stops being informative once the model can fit anything, which modern models can, demonstrably. What remains is that the optimiser, not the objective, selects among the perfect fits, and it happens to select well. Nobody can yet say why in general. Taking the width to infinity instead of the sample makes the network exactly solvable and removes the only thing that made it interesting: in that limit it is kernel regression with features fixed before it saw the data, and it fits just as well while never finding the structure the data has. What the limit does buy is scales. Keeping activations, updates and gradients order one as width or depth grows is what makes a learning rate tuned on a small model correct on a large one, and what decides how many layers a signal survives. Meanwhile the assumption doing the most work is not any of the theorems but the one under all of them: that tomorrow's data looks like today's.
