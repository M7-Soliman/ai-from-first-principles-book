Part DRobots

A body imposes constraints a simulator does not: coordinates that must agree, a state nobody measures directly, and a configuration space whose volume defeats exhaustive search at about six joints. None of these is a learning problem, and all of them are load-bearing.

§25 Frames, and the bugs they cause

Everything is somewhere, relative to something

A pose is a rotation and a translation, and a system has many frames: world, vehicle, each sensor, each joint, each object. A transform composes: $T^{A}_{C} = T^{A}_{B} T^{B}_{C}$, and the notation exists because getting the order or the direction wrong is the most common bug in robotics code.

Rotations have several representations and each has a defect. Euler angles gimbal-lock; rotation matrices carry nine numbers for three degrees of freedom and drift from orthogonality; quaternions are compact and stable and have a sign ambiguity that produces a discontinuity if ignored. The practical rule is to store quaternions, compose with matrices, and never interpolate Euler angles.

§26 Kinematics

Forward is easy, inverse is not

Forward: joint angles to end-effector pose. A chain of transforms, always solvable, unique.

Inverse: desired pose to joint angles. May have no solution, one, several, or infinitely many, and the boundary between those cases is where a controller behaves strangely.

The Jacobian relates joint velocities to end-effector velocity and is the workhorse: invert it to convert a desired motion into joint commands. Near a singularity it loses rank, the inverse blows up, and a small commanded motion demands an enormous joint velocity, which is why damped least squares rather than a true inverse is what production code uses.

§27 Dynamics, and why torque control is different

Forces, not positions

$$M(q)\ddot{q} + C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q} + g(q) = \tau$$

Inertia, Coriolis, gravity, torque. Position control tracks a trajectory and resists anything that pushes; torque control commands forces and yields. For a robot working near people or handling objects, the second is what makes contact safe, and it needs the dynamics that the first can ignore.

Gravity compensation alone (computing $g(q)$ and cancelling it) transforms how a manipulator behaves, and it is the single highest-value term in that equation.

§28 Where the state comes from

Go and read

Kalman (1960), A new approach to linear filtering and prediction problems. Worth reading in the original for how little it assumes. The covariance recursion is the part that matters here: it is what lets a system say that it does not know.

Nobody measures the state

Wheel encoders drift, an inertial unit integrates its bias into a growing position error, GPS is metres-accurate and unavailable indoors, and a camera gives you geometry only after Part XXIX's whole movement D. The state is inferred from all of them, which is Part XXVIII §13's filter doing the work it was invented for.

The two properties that matter in practice are that the filter's covariance tells you when it does not know (which a point estimate cannot) and that it degrades gracefully when a sensor drops out, provided the sensor was modelled rather than assumed.

§29 Planning, and dimension

Go and read

LaValle, Planning Algorithms. Free online, and the reference for the planning in this movement. Chapter 5 on sampling-based methods explains what they give up as carefully as what they buy, which is rare.

What sampling gives up

Optimality. A random tree finds a path, not a good one, and the paths are characteristically jerky. The standard remedy is to smooth afterwards, which is a second algorithm with its own failure modes.

Completeness, in the useful sense. These planners are guaranteed to find a solution eventually if one exists, and give no bound on when, so a planner that has not returned tells you nothing about whether a path exists.

Determinism. The same query returns different paths, which matters more for testing than it sounds: a regression test on a randomised planner needs a fixed seed and a tolerance rather than an expected output.

§30 Estimation and mapping together

SLAM, in one paragraph

Localising in a map you are simultaneously building. The formulation that won is a pose graph: nodes are poses, edges are measured relative transforms, and the solution minimises total disagreement, which is Part XXIX §30's bundle adjustment with poses instead of points, and a graphical model's sparsity pattern.

The hard part is not the optimisation, it is loop closure: recognising a place seen before. A false positive corrupts the whole map irrecoverably, so the recognition threshold is set conservatively and a missed closure merely leaves drift.

§31 The stack, and its interfaces

Four layers, running at four rates

Perception at tens of hertz, producing objects and free space. Prediction of what the other agents will do. Planning at a few hertz, producing a trajectory. Control at hundreds of hertz, tracking it.

The rates matter as much as the contents. The control loop must be fast because §7 says so; the planner may be slow because the controller is tracking a trajectory rather than reacting. And each interface is a place where a representation is committed to, which is the argument both for modularity and, in §38, against it.

§32 What movement D establishes

Three things

A body brings bookkeeping that is not optional. Frames, rotation representations and singularities cause more real failures than any learned component.

Nobody measures the state, so a filter is not an enhancement, and its covariance is what tells you when it does not know.
