§35 Vision transformers, and Part X settled

Go and read

Dosovitskiy et al. (2021), An image is worth 16×16 words: transformers for image recognition at scale. Already read at Part X §32; it belongs here too, now that both architectures are on the page. It is honest about the condition on its own result: with limited data the transformer loses decisively, and it wins only once the dataset is very large: Part X §11's infinitely strong prior, priced by the people who removed it.

Part X §32 left a question open. Here is the honest answer, now that both mechanisms have been derived.

A vision transformer cuts the image into patches, typically $16\times16$, projects each to a vector, adds positional encodings, and runs an ordinary transformer over the resulting sequence. There is no convolution and the model has no notion that patch 5 is adjacent to patch 6 except what it learns from the positional encoding.

Why it loses on small data and wins on large

Part X §11 called convolution an infinitely strong prior: locality and translation equivariance, encoded so firmly the model cannot consider anything else. A prior substitutes an assumption for observations, so it is worth most when observations are scarce.

The vision transformer discards that prior. It must learn from data that nearby patches are related, which is possible, and which costs data. The original paper is explicit: below about a hundred million images the transformer loses; above it, it wins.

That is Part VI §7's no-free-lunch theorem showing up as an empirical curve. The prior is not free and not worthless; it is a loan against data you do not have, repaid when you get it.

What has happened since is convergence rather than victory. Modern vision transformers reintroduced hierarchy, local windows and downsampling, which are convolutional design ideas, while convolutional networks adopted the transformer training recipe, and Part X §26 measured how much of the original gap that was.

§36 What attention maps do not explain

In 60 seconds

A high attention weight is routinely read as "the model found this position important." It is not a safe reading: the weight multiplies a value that may be near zero, the residual stream carries information the map never touches, and different weight patterns can produce the same output. Attention maps generate hypotheses about a model. They do not test them.

Go and read

Jain & Wallace (2019), Attention is not explanation. Constructs substantially different attention distributions that produce near-identical predictions. If several maps explain the same behaviour then none of them is the explanation, and that is a logical point rather than an empirical one. Read it before trusting any attention heatmap, including one you made yourself.

Attention weights are the most-visualised quantity in deep learning and the most over-interpreted. The specific claim to be careful about is that a high weight means a position was important.

Pitfall: attention is not explanation, and the reasons are structural

The weight multiplies a value. Output is $\sum_i a_i \mathbf{v}_i$. A position with $a_i = 0.9$ and $\|\mathbf{v}_i\| \approx 0$ contributes nothing. Influence is the product, and only one factor is what gets plotted.

The residual stream carries information around the attention. §16: attention adds to a stream that already holds the position's own content. A position can be influential without being attended to at all.

Different weights, same output. It has been demonstrated repeatedly that one can construct substantially different attention distributions producing near-identical predictions. If several maps explain the same behaviour, no one of them is the explanation.

$W^O$ mixes the heads. §12: what reaches the stream is a learned combination, so a single head's map is one term of a sum you are not looking at.

None of this makes attention maps useless. It makes them a hypothesis-generating instrument rather than evidence, and the difference is Part V's whole subject.

§37 Induction heads

Go and read

Elhage et al. (2021), A mathematical framework for transformer circuits. Where §16's residual-stream reading and this section's circuit both come from. Long; the first half, the one- and two-layer analysis, is the part to read, and it is the clearest worked example in the literature of a mechanism predicted from structure and then found.

Against §36, here is a case where a mechanism was identified, predicted, and confirmed, and it is the best-understood circuit in any transformer.

The induction pattern

Given a sequence containing … A B … A, predict B.

The rule is not about any particular tokens. It is: find the earlier occurrence of the current token, and copy whatever followed it. A general-purpose, content-addressed copy, which is exactly Part XI §26's memory read, learned rather than designed.

Why it takes exactly two layers

The mechanism is a composition of two heads and neither can do it alone.

Layer 1: a previous-token head. Each position attends to the one before it and copies its content. Now position $t$'s representation carries "the token at $t-1$ was $X$".

Layer 2: the induction head. The current token forms a query; the keys, thanks to layer 1, encode what came before each position. So the query "I am token A" matches positions preceded by A, and attending there retrieves the token that followed the earlier A.

One layer cannot do this. The information "what preceded position $j$" is not available at position $j$ until a layer has put it there. This is a genuine two-layer composition, and it is the clearest known example of depth being used for something specific.

Why the control panel is the important one

Without the fixed-offset comparison the figure would prove much less. A one-layer model handed a predictable repeat offset solves the task by attending to "position $t - 16$", a positional rule needing no composition at all, and no induction head.

Randomising the offset removes that shortcut, and only then does the one-layer model fail. So the measurement that matters is not "two layers beat one" but "two layers beat one exactly when the positional shortcut is unavailable", which is what identifies the mechanism rather than merely the gap.

This is Part IX §29's discipline in a new setting: a comparison establishes a mechanism only when you have removed the alternative explanations, and the control is where that work happens.

Induction heads matter beyond the toy. In real language models they appear during a narrow window of training, coinciding with a visible bump in the loss curve, and their appearance coincides with the emergence of in-context learning. That correlation is the strongest available evidence about where §38's capability comes from.

§38 In-context learning

In 60 seconds

Show a model a few examples of a task in its prompt and it does the task, without any weight changing. This was not designed, not predicted, and is not explained. It is the most consequential property of the architecture and the least understood.

Go and read

Brown et al. (2020), Language models are few-shot learners. In-context learning, reported. Read it for what it does not contain: no mechanism, no explanation, and a capability nobody set out to build. That absence is still, five years later, the state of the question, which is worth sitting with.

Why the left panel is the right measurement

Averaging accuracy over every position mixes two populations. For a query whose answer has never been shown, the model cannot know it: the rule is random and nothing in the prompt determines it. Chance is the correct performance there.

Conditioning on the number of demonstrations separates them, and the resulting curve is the actual claim: performance rises with evidence supplied in the context, from chance at zero demonstrations upward.

This is worth doing carefully because in-context learning is easy to overstate and easy to understate, and the aggregate number does both at once.

What is and is not known

Known: it improves with model scale, sharply and somewhat unpredictably. It correlates with the appearance of induction heads. It is sensitive to the order and format of the examples in ways that a genuine learning algorithm would not be. And it happens with no gradient step: the weights are frozen.

Proposed: that the forward pass implements something like an optimisation step over the examples in context; that attention over demonstrations implements a kernel regression; that the model is doing Bayesian inference over latent tasks it saw in training. There are constructions showing transformers can implement gradient descent in their forward pass, which establishes possibility rather than actuality.

Not known: which of these, if any, describes what trained models do. The mechanism is open.

It is worth being blunt about the significance. Next-token prediction on text produced a system that adapts to new tasks from examples at inference time, and nobody was trying to build that. The capability was discovered by scaling something else. That is a disconcerting fact about the field's understanding of its own artefacts, and Part XX and Part XXI are both downstream of it.

§39 What actually limits context

Four separate constraints, routinely conflated, and they have different fixes.

Memory, at training time
The $O(n^2)$ score matrix: §13 measured 17.2 GB at 65,536 positions. Solved by §29's FlashAttention, which never materialises it.
Memory, at inference time
The KV cache, $O(n)$ per layer and growing with every token: §28 measured 68.7 GB at 131,072 tokens. Mitigated by §30's grouped-query attention and by cache compression, not solved.
Positional extrapolation
A model trained to 4,096 positions has never seen the rotation angles that position 40,000 produces (§11), and degrades sharply past its trained length. Mitigated by interpolating the frequencies or raising RoPE's base, usually with a short fine-tune. This is where most practical long-context work happens.
Whether the model uses what it has
The one nobody can engineer around. A model with a 128,000-token window may attend reliably to the beginning and end of its context and poorly to the middle, a robustly reproduced finding. Context length is a capacity, and capacity is not use.
Pitfall: a context window is a specification, not a capability

The advertised number says what the model will accept without erroring. It says nothing about retrieval accuracy at depth, and the two diverge substantially.

The check is cheap and you should run it on anything you depend on: place a specific fact at a controlled depth in a long context, ask for it, and sweep the depth. Part XIV makes this a standard instrument; here it is enough to know that the number on the box is not the measurement.

§40 The convergence

Go and read

Gu & Dao (2024), Mamba: linear-time sequence modeling with selective state spaces. The recurrent end of this section's axis, taken seriously: a state large and structured enough to hold something useful, and an input-dependent transition, which is Part XI §18's gating promoted from repair to central mechanism. Read it knowing that §32 already showed linear attention is a recurrence; this is that observation pursued rather than regretted.

Part XI ended by noting that state space models are recurrences designed to keep a recurrent form at inference and gain a parallel form for training. Having derived attention, the trade is now statable exactly.

Why this is one trade and not two architectures

§32 already found it: linear attention is a recurrence, because reassociating the product turns the keys and values into a fixed-size running state. The quadratic cost and the unbounded state are the same fact.

So the axis runs from "keep everything, pay $O(n)$ per token, retrieve exactly" to "keep a summary, pay $O(1)$ per token, retrieve what you compressed". Modern state space models sit toward the recurrent end with two additions: a state large and structured enough to hold usefully more, and an input-dependent transition, which is Part XI §18's gating, made the central mechanism rather than a repair.

And they can be trained in parallel, which removes Part XI §28's binding limitation. What they cannot do is retrieve an arbitrary detail from far back on demand, because the state is finite. Hybrid architectures (mostly recurrent layers, a few attention layers for exact recall) are the current practical answer, and it is a genuinely open question which end of the axis the field settles on.

The one thing to carry forward

Attention buys a constant path length between any two positions, and pays for it with a state that grows without bound.

Everything in this part is that trade. The $O(n^2)$ cost, the KV cache, FlashAttention, grouped-query attention, sparse and linear approximations, the context-length limits: every one is a consequence of keeping everything rather than summarising.

Part XI's architecture made the opposite choice and paid in path length. Neither is correct; they are two settings of one dial, and the reason attention won is that parallel training mattered more than either, which is a fact about hardware, not about sequences.

Which is the question Part XVII takes up: if the binding constraint on what a model can learn is how much data you can push through it, what happens when you push a great deal?
