BuildThe project

Six to eight hours. By the end you will have written, from nothing, the machinery that every deep learning framework provides, and you will trust it, because you tested it.

P.1 What you are building

tinygrad-zero: a numerical differentiation and optimisation toolkit, then used to train an actual model.

The rule: no autograd, no scipy.optimize, no frameworks. NumPy for arrays and arithmetic only. If a library already does it, that is the thing you are writing.

P.2 Stage 1: derivatives you can trust

FunctionNotes
deriv_forward(f, x, h)$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$: the naive version.
deriv_central(f, x, h)$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}$: noticeably better.
gradient(f, x, h)Central difference on each coordinate in turn. Returns a vector.
gradient_check(f, grad_f, x)Compare an analytic gradient against the numerical one. Return the worst relative error.

Deliverable: a log-log plot of error against $h$ for both derivative methods on a function whose derivative you know. You should see error fall as $h$ shrinks, bottom out around $h \approx 10^{-5}$ (central) or $10^{-8}$ (forward), then get worse again. Explain that upturn: it is §26, not a bug.

P.3 Stage 2: optimisers

  • gradient_descent(grad_f, x0, lr, steps): return the whole path, not just the endpoint.
  • gradient_descent_momentum(...): keep a running velocity, $v \leftarrow \beta v + \nabla f$, then step along $v$.
  • line_search(f, x, direction): pick a step size by trying several and keeping the best.

Deliverable: reproduce Figure 11 with your own code, then add a fourth panel showing momentum on the elongated bowl $f(x,y) = x^2 + 20y^2$. Momentum should visibly reduce the zigzag. You have just motivated all of Part IX.

P.4 Stage 3: train something real

Fit $\hat y = \sigma(wx + b)$ to a small binary classification dataset you generate yourself.

  1. Write the forward pass and the squared-error loss.
  2. Derive $\partial L/\partial w$ and $\partial L/\partial b$ by hand, on paper, using §24's worked example as the template.
  3. Implement those analytic gradients.
  4. Verify them with your own gradient_check. Do not skip this: it is the point of Stage 1.
  5. Train with gradient descent. Plot loss against iteration.
  6. Sweep the learning rate over several orders of magnitude and plot the final loss for each.

Deliverable: a loss curve that goes down, a gradient check that passes below $10^{-6}$, and a learning-rate sweep showing the too-small / just-right / too-large regimes.

P.5 Stretch

  • Two layers. Extend to $\hat y = \sigma(w_2\sigma(w_1x + b_1) + b_2)$. Derive the gradients by hand: this is genuine backpropagation, done manually.
  • Cross-entropy loss instead of squared error. Compare the training curves; cross-entropy should train faster, and Part II §25 explains why.
  • Symbolic differentiation. Represent expressions as trees and differentiate them structurally. This is one honest step from writing an autograd engine, which is the Part VII project.
  • Second derivatives. Approximate $f''$ numerically and implement Newton's method. Compare its convergence to gradient descent.

P.6 Done when

  • Central difference is demonstrably more accurate than forward difference.
  • You can explain why very small $h$ makes accuracy worse.
  • Your gradient check passes on hand-derived gradients, and you tried breaking a gradient on purpose to confirm the check catches it.
  • The loss curve decreases and the learning-rate sweep shows all three regimes.
  • Momentum visibly beats plain descent on the elongated bowl.
Why this project

Gradient checking is the single most useful debugging technique in hand-written deep learning code, and almost nobody learns it before they need it. Building it here, before you have a network complicated enough to hide bugs in, means you will already own it when Part VII asks you to write backprop for real.

The learning-rate sweep matters for a different reason: it converts "the learning rate is important" from a thing you were told into a thing you have seen.

Onward

You are done with the ground floor. Part I (linear algebra) is the next rung, and it now has everything it needs from you: function notation, composition, exponentials, and (for its final section on PCA) the idea of maximising a function subject to a constraint.

One thing worth saying plainly. If this took you three weeks, that is not slow; that is what relearning a subject takes. The mathematics in this document represents roughly two semesters of coursework, compressed to what deep learning actually uses. Getting through it at all puts you ahead of most people who start this book, because most people skip this step and hit the wall in Part VII instead.
