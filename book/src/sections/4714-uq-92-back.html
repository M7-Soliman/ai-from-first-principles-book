BuildThe project

Ten to fourteen hours. You build four ways of producing an interval, measure what each one's intervals actually contain, then break the only one with a guarantee and watch it fail without saying so.

P.1 What you are building

howsure, an uncertainty toolkit: a Gaussian process from scratch, an ensemble, a conformal wrapper that works on any model, and a coverage harness that scores all of them against the same nominal level.

The rule: no interval ships without a measured coverage number beside its nominal one. That comparison is the entire subject, and producing it is one line that almost nobody writes.

P.2 Stage 1: the coverage harness

  1. Implement coverage, coverage by group, and mean width.
  2. Apply them to any model you already have, with whatever intervals it currently produces.
  3. Compute coverage on your oldest and newest data slices separately.

Deliverable: the nominal and measured numbers side by side, plus the by-group table. Step three is the early-warning system for the rest of the project.

P.3 Stage 2: separating the two kinds

  1. Build a generator whose noise level you set.
  2. Fit an ensemble across sample sizes and decompose the predictive variance.
  3. Plot both terms against $n$.
  4. Now check your ensemble is real: measure member disagreement in-distribution and out-of-distribution.

Deliverable: the two curves, and the disagreement ratio. If disagreement does not rise off-distribution, fix the ensemble before continuing: everything downstream depends on it.

P.4 Stage 3: the exact case

  1. Implement a Gaussian process with a Cholesky factorisation.
  2. Reproduce the widening interval over a gap in the observations.
  3. Verify the posterior variance is unchanged when you replace every observed value with nonsense.
  4. Sweep the length-scale across three orders of magnitude.
  5. Implement the marginal likelihood and recover a length-scale you generated from.

Deliverable: the posterior plot, the invariance check, and the length-scale sweep. Step three surprises people and is two lines.

P.5 Stage 4: four methods, one number

  1. On a problem where the exact posterior is available, measure coverage for the exact posterior, a bootstrap ensemble, MC dropout and a point estimate.
  2. Query inside the training range only, then partly outside it, and compare.
  3. Rank them by coverage error and by width.

Deliverable: the four-method table, computed twice. The gap between the two query regimes is why in-distribution benchmarks do not detect over-confidence.

P.6 Stage 5: a guarantee

  1. Implement split conformal. Verify coverage at five nominal levels.
  2. Run it with a deliberately useless model and compare coverage and width.
  3. Implement the $n$ version of the quantile and measure the under-coverage.
  4. Implement normalised scores and prediction sets.
  5. Break exchangeability by shifting the test distribution, and track coverage down.
  6. Implement weighted conformal and recover it.

Deliverable: the coverage table, the good-versus-useless comparison, and the shift curve. Step five is the one to internalise: nothing in the method reports that the promise has stopped holding.

P.7 Stage 6: turning it into a decision

  1. Build a calibrated model by construction: draw the probability, then draw the outcome.
  2. Implement the optimal threshold and verify by brute force that the formula wins.
  3. Sweep the cost ratio and compute what the default threshold costs.
  4. Implement value of information, and construct a case where an informative signal is worth nothing.
  5. Price an abstention band.

Deliverable: the cost-ratio table, and the two value-of-information cases. Then find the threshold in a system you run and compute what it has been costing.

P.8 Stretch

  • Conformal on top of an ensemble, using the spread as the difficulty estimate.
  • Bayesian optimisation with expected improvement, against random search.
  • A Laplace approximation, diagonal against Kronecker-factored.
  • Weighted conformal across two real time periods.
  • A rolling coverage monitor on a running system.
  • The five-item coverage report for something you have deployed.

P.9 Done when

  • You have a measured coverage number beside a nominal one, for a model you already had.
  • You have that number broken down by group, and it is not uniform.
  • You have separated aleatoric from epistemic on data whose noise you set.
  • You have confirmed your ensemble disagrees more off-distribution than on.
  • Your GP's posterior variance is unchanged when the observed values are replaced by nonsense.
  • You have four methods scored against one nominal level, inside and outside the training range.
  • Your conformal implementation hits nominal at five levels, and still does with a useless model.
  • You have watched coverage fall under shift without the method reporting anything.
  • You have computed what your current decision threshold costs relative to the optimal one.
  • Your README names one interval you previously reported that you now know was wrong, and in which direction.
Why this project

Uncertainty is the part of a model most often produced and least often checked. A number comes out, it is reported, and no one asks whether intervals of nominal 90% contain the truth 90% of the time, which takes one line to answer and is frequently no.

Stage 5 is the reason for the project's shape. Conformal is the only method here that promises rather than hopes, the promise survives an arbitrarily bad model, and it collapses silently the moment the data stops resembling the calibration set. Holding both of those at once is what it means to understand a guarantee.

The framework, and where the interval attaches

Dataset, model family, loss, optimiser; then Part XXI's claim about what the result establishes, and Part XXII's estimand. This part adds the seventh: the interval, and the assumption it rests on.

Every method here attaches to a different place. A Gaussian process puts it in the prior over functions. An ensemble puts it in the diversity of the optimiser's solutions. Conformal puts it nowhere in the model at all: it attaches to a held-out set and an exchangeability assumption. Naming which one you are relying on is what turns an error bar into a claim, and Part XXIII's lesson applies unchanged: a guarantee is worth exactly as much as the assumption underneath it, and the assumption is usually the thing nobody wrote down.
