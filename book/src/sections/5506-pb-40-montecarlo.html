Part DTurning inference into sampling

The other answer: do not approximate the posterior, draw from it. Sampling methods are asymptotically exact where movement C's are not, and they exchange that guarantee for a different problem: you cannot tell from the samples whether they have arrived.

§25 Estimating an expectation

What you actually need

Posteriors are rarely wanted for their own sake; what is wanted is an expectation under one: a predictive mean, a probability, a decision. With samples, $\mathbb{E}_p[f] \approx \frac{1}{n}\sum f(x^{(i)})$, unbiased, with error falling as $1/\sqrt{n}$.

Two things about that rate. It does not depend on dimension, which is the reason Monte Carlo exists. And it is slow (a further decimal place costs a hundred times the samples), which is why everything in this movement is about reducing the constant.

§26 Importance sampling, and when it dies

Sampling from the wrong distribution on purpose

$$\mathbb{E}_p[f] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[f(x)\,\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right] \;\approx\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_i f(x^{(i)})\, w_i, \qquad w_i = \frac{p(x^{(i)})}{q(x^{(i)})}$$

Correct for any $q$ covering $p$'s support, and the weights only need $p$ up to a constant if you normalise them. This is Part XXII §19's inverse-propensity weighting and Part XVIII §40's off-policy correction, in their general form.

Pitfall: the estimator can be unbiased and useless

If $q$ is a poor match the weights are dominated by a handful of samples, and the effective sample size collapses. In high dimensions this is the normal case, not the pathological one: mismatch compounds per dimension, so a proposal that is 10% off in each of fifty dimensions has essentially all its weight on one draw.

The diagnostic is one line: $(\sum w)^2 / \sum w^2$, the effective sample size, and it is the number to look at before any estimate computed from weights. If it reads 3 out of 10,000, your unbiased estimator is a single sample with extra steps.

§27 Markov chains

Constructing a chain whose stationary distribution is your posterior

Rather than drawing independently, take a random walk whose long-run distribution is the target. Two conditions suffice: the chain can reach anywhere from anywhere, and it leaves the target unchanged.

Detailed balance ($p(x)T(x \to y) = p(y)T(y \to x)$) is the usual way to get the second, and it is sufficient rather than necessary, which matters because some good samplers do not satisfy it.

What you give up is independence. Consecutive draws are correlated, so $n$ samples are worth fewer than $n$, and §30 is about how many fewer.

§28 Metropolis–Hastings and Gibbs

Two constructions

Metropolis–Hastings. Propose $y$ from any $q(\cdot \mid x)$, accept with probability $\min\{1, \frac{p(y)q(x\mid y)}{p(x)q(y\mid x)}\}$. The normalising constant cancels, which is the whole trick. The proposal scale is the tuning problem: too small and the chain crawls, too large and everything is rejected.

Gibbs sampling. Update one variable at a time from its conditional given the rest. No rejections and no tuning, and it moves only along the axes, so on a strongly correlated posterior it takes tiny steps for the same reason mean field misreports the variance. The two failures have the same cause.

§29 Using the gradient

Go and read

Betancourt (2017), A conceptual introduction to Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. Why the gradient helps, argued geometrically rather than algebraically. It also carries the best available discussion of what the diagnostics can and cannot establish, which is Figure 6's subject.

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

Add a momentum variable, treat the negative log-density as a potential energy, and simulate the resulting dynamics with a symplectic integrator. The trajectory travels a long way while keeping the density high, so proposals are distant and still accepted.

Its two knobs are the step size and the number of steps; the second is what modern implementations tune automatically by detecting when a trajectory doubles back.

§30 How many samples do you have

Effective sample size

$$n_{\text{eff}} \;=\; \frac{n}{1 + 2\sum_{k \ge 1} \rho_k}$$

With $\rho_k$ the autocorrelation at lag $k$. This is the number to quote: the Monte Carlo standard error is $\sigma/\sqrt{n_{\text{eff}}}$, not $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, and a chain of a million draws can have an effective size of forty.

Thinning a chain to reduce correlation throws information away and lowers $n_{\text{eff}}$. It is worth doing only when storage is the binding constraint, which it usually is not.

§31 Diagnostics, and what they cannot see

What R̂ is actually testing

The Gelman–Rubin statistic compares variance between chains with variance within them. That is a test of whether the chains agree, which is a necessary condition for convergence and not a sufficient one. Chains that started in the same place and never left it agree perfectly.

So the diagnostic is only informative if the chains were dispersed to begin with, and Figure 6's right panel shows what a correct answer then looks like: a diagnostic screaming failure because the chains genuinely disagree, which is the truth about the target rather than a defect of the sampler.

The honest summary is uncomfortable. No diagnostic can prove convergence; they detect specific failures, and a mode nobody visited leaves no trace in the samples. What helps is dispersed initialisation, many chains, and simulating data from your own model to check the method recovers parameters you set.

§32 What movement D establishes

Three things

Sampling is asymptotically exact where variational inference is not, and pays for it in cost and in the impossibility of knowing when you have arrived.

The gradient is worth a lot and more as the problem stiffens. Figure 5 measures HMC's advantage growing from 1.6× to 7.0× with the condition number.

A clean diagnostic is not evidence of a correct answer. Figure 6 measures R̂ = 1.0000 on a run that missed half the distribution, and 10.47 on the run that found it.
