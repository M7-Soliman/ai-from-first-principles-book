Part EConstrained optimisation

§19 Lagrange multipliers and KKT

In 60 seconds

Sometimes you must minimise subject to a restriction: weights on a sphere, probabilities summing to one. At a constrained optimum, the objective's gradient is parallel to the constraint's gradient: there is no way left to move that both satisfies the constraint and improves the objective. Writing that condition down gives the method of Lagrange multipliers.

The problem:

$$\min_{\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) \quad \text{subject to} \quad g(\mathbf{x}) = 0$$

You cannot simply set $\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$, because the unconstrained minimum will generally violate the constraint. The question is what replaces that condition.

Intuition: tangency is the whole idea

Walk along the constraint curve. At a point where a contour crosses it, the objective is changing as you walk; one direction goes downhill, so you are not at an optimum.

The only way to be stuck is if the contour is tangent to the constraint, so that moving along the constraint changes the objective not at all to first order. And tangency of a contour to a curve is exactly the statement that their two gradients (each perpendicular to its own curve) point along the same line.

That perpendicularity is not a new fact. Part 0 §23 established that a gradient is always perpendicular to the contours of its own function, because moving along a contour leaves the value unchanged and the direction of fastest change must be at right angles to it. The constraint set $g(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ is a contour of $g$. So $\nabla g$ is perpendicular to it for exactly the same reason $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to the objective's contours, and the tangency condition follows.

Two vectors being parallel means one is a multiple of the other:

$$\nabla f = -\lambda \nabla g$$

for some scalar $\lambda$, the Lagrange multiplier. Rearranged, this says that the stationary points of

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \lambda) = f(\mathbf{x}) + \lambda\, g(\mathbf{x})$$

with respect to both $\mathbf{x}$ and $\lambda$ are exactly the constrained optima. Differentiating with respect to $\lambda$ returns $g(\mathbf{x}) = 0$, which is the constraint itself; so the single function $\mathcal{L}$ encodes both conditions and the constrained problem becomes an unconstrained one in more variables.

Worked: the largest a coordinate can be on a sphere

Maximise $f(x,y) = x$ subject to $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. The answer is obviously $x=1$; the point is to watch the machinery produce it.

$\mathcal{L} = x + \lambda(x^2 + y^2 - 1)$

$\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x = 1 + 2\lambda x = 0$

$\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial y = 2\lambda y = 0$

From the second equation, either $\lambda = 0$ or $y = 0$. If $\lambda = 0$ the first equation reads $1 = 0$, which is impossible, so $y = 0$. The constraint then gives $x = \pm 1$, and the first equation gives $\lambda = -1/(2x)$.

The maximum is at $(1, 0)$. The method found it without ever parameterising the circle.

What the multiplier means

In the worked example $\lambda$ appeared, did its job, and was discarded, which is how it is usually taught, and it leaves the impression that the multiplier is scaffolding. It is not. It carries information you frequently want, and knowing what it means is what makes the connection to regularization below more than an analogy.

The multiplier is a price. Specifically, it measures how much the best achievable objective value would improve if the constraint were relaxed by one unit. Loosen the constraint slightly, and the optimum moves by $\lambda$ times the amount you loosened it.

So a large multiplier says the constraint is expensive; it is holding you back hard, and buying a little more room would pay well. A multiplier of zero says the constraint costs you nothing, which is exactly the situation where it was not binding in the first place. That observation is the whole content of complementary slackness below, arrived at from the other direction.

Why this matters: it is why penalties and constraints are the same knob

This is the reading that makes regularization make sense. "Minimise the loss subject to $\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \leq c$" and "minimise loss $+\ \lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$" are the same problem, and $\lambda$ is the multiplier of the first stated as a coefficient in the second.

The price reading tells you how they trade. Shrinking the budget $c$ raises the price $\lambda$, because room you are short of is worth more. So turning up the regularization coefficient is not a separate operation from tightening a constraint on the weights; it is the same operation, quoted in the other currency. In economics this quantity is called a shadow price, and it is the same mathematics.

Inequality constraints

Constraints are often inequalities: a probability at most 1, a variance at least 0. The extension is natural once you notice that an inequality constraint is either active (you are pressed against it, and it behaves like an equality) or inactive (you are strictly inside, and it may as well not exist).

The KKT conditions

For $\min f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $g(\mathbf{x}) \leq 0$, a solution must satisfy:

  1. Stationarity: $\nabla f + \mu \nabla g = \mathbf{0}$
  2. Feasibility: $g(\mathbf{x}) \leq 0$
  3. Non-negativity: $\mu \geq 0$
  4. Complementary slackness: $\mu\, g(\mathbf{x}) = 0$

The fourth condition is the elegant one. It forces at least one of $\mu$ and $g(\mathbf{x})$ to be zero; so either the constraint is active ($g = 0$) and its multiplier may be non-zero, or the constraint is slack ($g < 0$) and its multiplier must vanish. Inactive constraints contribute nothing, stated algebraically.

The third condition encodes direction: an inequality can only push you one way. For an equality constraint $\lambda$ may take either sign, because the constraint restrains you from both sides.

History: a master's thesis that went unread for thirty years

These are called the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions, and the first name was added decades late.

Harold Kuhn and Albert Tucker published them in 1951, and the result was foundational to the new field of nonlinear programming. It carried their two names for years.

Then it emerged that William Karush had derived the same conditions in his 1939 master's thesis at Chicago, twelve years earlier. He never published it. Master's theses were not widely circulated, the mathematical audience for optimisation barely existed at the time, and the work simply sat.

When the priority came to light, Kuhn wrote to Karush acknowledging it and pressed for the attribution to be corrected; the three-name form became standard from the 1970s. It is a rare case of Stigler's Law (invoked in Part I §9 and Part II §15) being caught and partially reversed, and it happened because the person who benefited from the error insisted on fixing it.

Why this matters

Constrained optimisation appears throughout what follows. Regularization has a constrained reading: penalising $\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ is equivalent to constraining $\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \leq c$ for a corresponding $c$, and $\lambda$ is the multiplier, which is why the penalty strength and the constraint radius are two views of one knob.

Support vector machines are a constrained problem whose entire theory runs through the KKT conditions, and the support vectors are precisely the examples whose constraints are active. Maximum entropy derivations (including the Gaussian result quoted in Part II §15) are Lagrangian arguments: maximise entropy subject to matching a known mean and variance.

Part FThe payoff

§20 Least squares, three ways

One problem, three algorithms, all correct in exact arithmetic. Two of them break. This section is the argument of the whole part, made concrete.

The problem is the overdetermined system from Part I §12: given $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with $m > n$, find the $\mathbf{x}$ minimising $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2$.

Method 1: the normal equations

Part I's drill S3 derived the condition that the residual be orthogonal to every column, giving

$$A^\top A\,\mathbf{x} = A^\top \mathbf{b}$$

This is the formula in every introductory treatment. It is a square system, it is cheap, and it is numerically the worst option available, because forming $A^\top A$ squares the singular values, and therefore squares the condition number:

$$\kappa(A^\top A) = \kappa(A)^2$$

By §5's accounting, that doubles the number of digits you lose. A matrix with $\kappa = 10^8$ (unremarkable in real data) leaves you with nothing at all.

Method 2: QR factorisation

Write $A = QR$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $R$ upper triangular. Because orthogonal matrices preserve lengths (Part I §16), multiplying the residual by $Q^\top$ changes nothing about its norm, and the problem collapses to solving the triangular system

$$R\mathbf{x} = Q^\top \mathbf{b}$$

$A^\top A$ is never formed, so the conditioning is never squared. This is the standard approach in serious software.

Pitfall: "use QR" is not specific enough

There is more than one way to compute a QR factorisation, and they are not equally stable. This matters enough that the drills for this part were rewritten once it surfaced.

Gram–Schmidt comes in two forms that are algebraically identical and numerically very different. Classical Gram–Schmidt (the version most linear algebra courses teach, which orthogonalises each new vector against the whole already-computed set in one shot) loses orthogonality at a rate proportional to $\kappa^2$. Modified Gram–Schmidt, the same formula computed in a different order (reorthogonalising one vector at a time as it goes), only loses it proportional to $\kappa$. That is a real improvement, and it is still not enough. Measured on the problems behind Figure 13, using modified Gram–Schmidt:

$\kappa(A)$$\|Q^\top Q - I\|$, modified Gram–SchmidtHouseholderError, modified Gram–Schmidt
$10^{5}$$2\times10^{-12}$$1\times10^{-15}$$1\times10^{-8}$
$10^{9}$$8\times10^{-8}$$8\times10^{-16}$$24$
$10^{11}$$9\times10^{-6}$$1\times10^{-15}$$4\times10^{5}$

The computed $Q$ drifts away from being orthogonal exactly when you need it most, and the drift eventually costs more than it looks: by $\kappa = 10^{9}$ modified Gram–Schmidt's own solve error ($24$) already exceeds the normal equations' error at the same conditioning (order 1), and by $\kappa=10^{11}$ it has degraded to roughly $4\times10^{5}$, tens of thousands of times worse than the normal equations, the method it was supposed to replace. Classical Gram–Schmidt, losing orthogonality at $\kappa^2$ instead of $\kappa$, is worse again. Householder reflections hold orthogonality at machine precision across the whole range, which is why every serious library uses them.

This is §6's conditioning-versus-stability distinction one level down. The problem is the same, and the approach is the same; the difference is entirely in the algorithm, and it is the difference between six good digits and none.

Method 3: the SVD

Take $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ and use the pseudoinverse from Part I §20. The most expensive of the three and the most robust; and uniquely, it still returns a sensible answer when $A$ is actually rank-deficient, because you can simply discard the singular values below a threshold rather than dividing by them.

Why this matters: the lesson generalises

Three algorithms. Identical in exact arithmetic. In floating point, one of them fails at a condition number where the others are comfortable.

Algebraic equivalence does not imply numerical equivalence. That single sentence is what this part exists to establish, and it is why np.linalg.lstsq does not implement the formula from the textbook. When you find a derivation that ends in a clean closed form, the remaining question is always whether that form is the one to compute.

Try it: the last one

Your data matrix has $\kappa(A) = 10^6$. You fit by the normal equations in float64 and get an answer. Roughly how many digits of it can you trust, and what should you have done?

Answer. Forming $A^\top A$ gives $\kappa = 10^{12}$. Starting from about 16 digits and losing 12, you retain roughly four, and that is optimistic, since it ignores every other source of error along the way.

Four digits may even be sufficient, depending on what you need. The failure is not having known. The right move is to use QR or the SVD, which would have cost you six digits instead of twelve for a modest increase in computation, or in practice to call lstsq, which already does this. And in either case to have checked $\kappa$ first, because that number tells you in advance how much of your answer is real.

Constrained least squares: §19 and §20 meeting

The KKT machinery of §19 and the least-squares problem of §20 connect directly in one concrete case: minimise $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|^2$ subject to $\|\mathbf{x}\|^2 \leq 1$.

The Lagrangian is

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \lambda) = \|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|^2 + \lambda(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x} - 1)$$

Setting $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L} = 0$:

$$2A^\top(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}) + 2\lambda\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$$ $$(A^\top A + \lambda I)\,\mathbf{x} = A^\top\mathbf{b}$$

This is ridge regression, and $\lambda$ is the ridge parameter, except here $\lambda$ is not a free choice. It is fixed by the requirement that the constraint be satisfied: $\|\mathbf{x}\|^2 = 1$. Increasing $\lambda$ shrinks $\mathbf{x}$, so you can always find one that lands on the sphere.

Worked: constrained least squares with numbers

$A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 6\end{pmatrix}$. The unconstrained solution is $\mathbf{x}^* = \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 3\end{pmatrix}$ (reading off $A^\top A = \text{diag}(1,4)$, $A^\top\mathbf{b} = (2, 12)$), with $\|\mathbf{x}^*\| = \sqrt{13} \approx 3.61$, well outside the unit sphere.

With the constraint $\|\mathbf{x}\|^2 \leq 1$, the KKT solution satisfies $(A^\top A + \lambda I)\mathbf{x} = A^\top\mathbf{b}$, which here gives

$$x_1 = \frac{2}{1+\lambda}, \qquad x_2 = \frac{12}{4+\lambda}$$

We need $\lambda \geq 0$ such that $x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 1$. Solving numerically: $\lambda \approx 8.29$ gives $x_1 \approx 0.215$, $x_2 \approx 0.977$, with $\|\mathbf{x}\| = 1.000$. Verify: $(1+8.29) \times 0.215 = 2.00 = (A^\top\mathbf{b})_1$ ✓, $(4+8.29) \times 0.977 = 12.00 = (A^\top\mathbf{b})_2$ ✓.

The multiplier $\lambda \approx 8.29$ is the shadow price of the constraint exactly as written, $\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x} \leq c$ with $c=1$: the price per unit of the squared-norm budget, not per unit of radius. A numerical check confirms that relaxing the budget from $c=1$ to $c=1+\delta$ reduces the residual by approximately $8.29\,\delta$, matching $\lambda$ exactly. The radius $r=\sqrt{c}$ is a different variable, related by $c=r^2$, so relaxing the radius from 1 to $1+\delta$ moves $c$ by $dc/dr = 2r = 2$ at $r=1$: it reduces the residual by approximately $2\lambda\delta \approx 16.6\,\delta$, twice the naive reading. The multiplier prices whatever variable the constraint is written in terms of, and here that variable is $\|\mathbf{x}\|^2$, not $\|\mathbf{x}\|$.
