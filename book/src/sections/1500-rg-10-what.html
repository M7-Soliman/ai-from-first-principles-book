Part AWhat regularization is

§1 The definition, and why it is broader than a penalty

In 60 seconds

Regularization is any modification made to a learning algorithm intended to reduce its generalisation error but not its training error. That definition is deliberately wide: it covers penalties, but also early stopping, data augmentation, dropout, ensembling and architecture. What unites them is not a formula: it is that each expresses a preference among functions that fit the data equally well.

You already know why something like this is needed. Part VI §4 split the risk into training error plus a generalisation gap, and Part VI §6 showed the two conflict: capacity that drives the first down widens the second. Regularization is the machinery for managing that trade, and Part V §4 gave the licence, since total error is variance plus squared bias and you may pay one to reduce the other.

What is worth being careful about is how wide the category is. Regularization is usually introduced as "add a penalty term", and a reader who stops there will fail to recognise most of it.

Definition: regularization

Any modification to a learning algorithm intended to reduce generalisation error, but not training error.

Note the second clause. A change that reduces both is not regularization; it is simply a better model. Regularization is specifically the trade: you accept worse performance on the data you have, in exchange for better performance on data you do not.

That clause is the test to apply. Adding a feature that genuinely carries signal improves both errors and is not regularization. Adding a penalty, deleting units at random, or stopping before convergence all make the training fit worse, and are worth doing anyway.

Intuition: a preference among equally good fits

The cleanest way to hold the whole category at once.

Part VI §22 established that many functions fit any finite dataset, and that the data alone cannot choose between them. Something must. Regularization is that something: a statement of which of the fitting functions you would rather have, imposed before you see which ones fit.

Every technique in this part is such a statement, and they differ only in what they prefer. $L^2$ prefers small weights. $L^1$ prefers few non-zero weights. Augmentation prefers functions invariant to transformations you consider irrelevant. Early stopping prefers functions reachable quickly from where you started. Dropout prefers functions that do not depend on any single unit.

Reading a regulariser as a preference tells you when it will help: exactly when the preference happens to be true of the problem. This is Part VI §7's no-free-lunch theorem arriving in a very practical costume: there is no universally good regulariser, only ones whose assumptions match your data.

§2 The five views, reconciled

Regularization has appeared in every part of this book so far, each time from a different direction and each time apparently about something else. Before adding anything new, it is worth putting the five side by side, because they describe one object.

These are not five analogies. They are five true descriptions of the same operation, and which one you reach for should depend on what you are trying to work out.

Why the multiplicity is useful rather than confusing

Each view answers a question the others answer badly.

Why does $L^1$ give exact zeros and $L^2$ not? The geometric view answers this in one picture and the others struggle; §7's figure is the argument.

How strong should the penalty be? The probabilistic view turns this into a question about how confident you are, which is at least a question about the world: $\lambda = 1/2\tau^2$, so a strong belief that weights are small is a large $\lambda$. (§4 writes the penalty as $(\alpha/2)\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$, so $\alpha = 2\lambda = 1/\tau^2$, a different symbol for the same strength, scaling by the factor of 2 in the prior's exponent.)

Why does it stabilise a fit that was wildly unstable? Only the numerical view explains that, and Part III §7 showed the mechanism has nothing to do with overfitting; it is the condition number.

Will it actually help here? Only the statistical view answers this, and its answer is a genuine trade that can go either way. Part V §5's drill made the point sharply: with four data points, the useless constant estimator beat the sample mean by a factor of 25.

When a regularization question is hard, the productive move is usually to change which of the five you are thinking in.

§3 Where the preference comes from

If a regulariser is a preference, the obvious question is where the preference is supposed to come from, and there are only three honest sources.

From knowledge of the domain
You know a rotated digit is still that digit, so you prefer functions invariant to rotation, and you get there by augmenting the data. This is the best case, and it is the only one where the regulariser is true rather than merely useful.
From a generic preference for simplicity
You do not know much, but you would rather have small weights than large ones, because large weights mean the output swings wildly for small input changes. This is where $L^2$ comes from, and it is a weak assumption that is usually mildly right.
From the optimiser, whether you asked or not
Early stopping, a finite learning rate, and the initialisation you happened to use all restrict which functions you can reach. These are regularisers you did not choose and frequently do not notice: §14 shows early stopping is a penalty in disguise, and Part VI §6's "effective capacity" is exactly this effect.
Pitfall: a wrong preference is worse than none

Because a regulariser is an assumption, it can be false. Penalising the weights of a model whose true solution genuinely has large weights makes the fit worse in both errors, which is the one outcome the definition in §1 rules out as a good idea.

The symptom is underfitting: training error rises and test error rises with it, and no amount of extra data helps because data is not the constraint. Part VI §6's learning curves diagnose it, and the fix is to weaken or remove the regulariser rather than to tune it harder.

This is why $\lambda$ is chosen on a validation set rather than reasoned about. You are not measuring how much regularization is good in general; you are measuring whether this preference is true of this problem, and by how much.
