PracticeDrills

Forty-three drills. The harness in parts/21-alignment/drills/ covers twenty of them with twenty-eight tests; several assert a negative result, because this part's subject is what evidence does not establish.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · Testing for a rare failure

You need 95% confidence that a failure mode occurs less than once in a thousand. Write down how many samples you must test, seeing zero failures, before deriving it.

Answer. 3,000, from the rule of three: with zero events in n trials the 95% upper bound on the rate is 3/n. Zero failures in 300 tries is entirely consistent with a 1% failure rate, which is the arithmetic that should govern every safety claim made from a clean evaluation. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

Warm-upW1 · The rule of three

You run 300 adversarial evaluations and see no failures. State the 95% upper bound on the failure rate, exactly and by the approximation.

Answer. Exactly $1 - 0.05^{1/300} = 0.99\%$; by the rule of three, $3/300 = 1\%$. Carry the approximation: it is accurate to a couple of percent for any $n$ you will use, and it makes §27's arithmetic something you can do in a meeting.
Warm-upW2 · The other direction

A failure occurs one time in 20,000. How many attempts before you have a 95% chance of having seen it once?

Answer. About $3 \times 20{,}000 = 60{,}000$ (exactly 59,914). The same factor of three, running the other way.
Warm-upW3 · A million users

Take the bound from W1 and convert it into expected failures at a million uses.

Answer. Up to ten thousand. The point of the drill is the conversion step: a rate that reads as reassuring becomes a count that does not, and the count is what reaches a user.
Warm-upW4 · Orthogonality's ceiling

How many mutually orthogonal directions fit in 768 dimensions? How many nearly orthogonal ones, at a tolerance of 0.1 cosine?

Answer. Exactly 768 orthogonal. Nearly orthogonal: exponentially many in the dimension: this is the Johnson–Lindenstrauss regime, and it is the reason superposition is possible at all rather than merely desirable.
Warm-upW5 · The sparsity/capacity trade

Recall Part XV §33's superposition sweep. At which sparsity does the model first represent more features than it has dimensions, and what is the exchange rate it is accepting?

Answer. Immediately: at 50% active it already stores 7 features in 5 dimensions, and only the fully dense case stops at 5. The trade is interference against capacity: an extra feature costs a little accuracy on every other feature it overlaps, and that cost is only paid when the two happen to fire together.
Warm-upW6 · Zero is not neutral

Why is ablating a component to zero a worse-behaved intervention than replacing it with its dataset mean?

Answer. Zero is usually far outside the range the component actually takes, so you are measuring the network's response to an impossible input. The mean is at least in-distribution. §9's list is the fuller answer.
Warm-upW7 · Present against used

State, in one sentence each, what a probe establishes and what an ablation establishes.

Answer. A probe establishes that information is recoverable from the representation. An ablation establishes that the network's output depends on it. Figure 2 is a case where the first holds at 0.775 and the second is a negligible +0.001, no more than a random direction costs.
Warm-upW8 · The correlation that does not settle it

Both arms of Figure 4 have a proxy correlating 0.706 with the truth. Why does that not determine the outcome?

Answer. Correlation is a property of the bulk; selection samples the tail. Two errors with identical variance and correlation can have completely different behaviour far from the mean, and far from the mean is the only place a maximiser looks.
Warm-upW9 · Where reward hacking hides

You are training against a learned reward and the reward curve rises smoothly for the whole run. What does that tell you about whether reward hacking occurred?

Answer. Nothing whatsoever. In Figure 4 the proxy is at its maximum exactly when the true reward has collapsed to 0.097. Detection requires a measurement that does not route through the objective being optimised.
Warm-upW10 · Uplift, not capability

A report says a model "can provide detailed instructions for $X$." What is missing before this bears on a release decision?

Answer. The counterfactual. What could the same actor obtain from a library, a search engine, and the models already released? The decision quantity is the difference, which requires a control group and therefore an experiment rather than a benchmark.
Warm-upW11 · The lower bound

A model fails a dangerous-capability evaluation. What has been established?

Answer. That this elicitation, at this effort, did not succeed. Not that the capability is absent (§31). This is why serious reports state the elicitation budget, and why one that does not cannot be interpreted.
Warm-upW12 · Which fairness

Two groups have different base rates. A vendor reports the system is fair. What is the first question?

Answer. Which criterion. Demographic parity, equalised odds and within-group calibration cannot all hold when base rates differ (a theorem, not a gap in the method), so the claim is incomplete until the choice is named and defended.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · The evaluation-sizing calculator

Write zero_failure_bound(n), rule_of_three(n) and trials_needed(p). Check the approximation against the exact bound across four orders of magnitude.

Answer. Within 2% everywhere you will use it. Keep this function; it is the one from this part you will actually run, and it belongs in the design phase of an evaluation rather than the write-up.
CoreC2 · Attack success against attempts

Implement attack_success(p, n) and verify it inverts trials_needed. Then plot it for a jailbreak that works one time in fifty.

Answer. $1-(1-p)^n$. At $p = 0.02$ a hundred attempts succeed 87% of the time. Per-attempt robustness is not the quantity a determined attacker faces.
CoreC3 · Confidence intervals that behave at the edge

Implement a binomial interval and check it against the exact bound at zero failures, where the normal approximation fails hardest.

Answer. The Wald interval gives $[0,0]$ at zero failures, which is absurd and is still widely used. Wilson or Clopper–Pearson behave, and both agree with $1-0.05^{1/n}$ closely at that edge.
CoreC4 · Build the toy model of superposition

No figure here: Part XV Figure 10 already shows this experiment. Project $m$ features into $n \lt m$ dimensions, reconstruct with a ReLU, sweep sparsity.

Answer. Dense inputs keep exactly $n$ features and discard the rest. At 0.1% density, 19 of 20. The transition is gradual and the mechanism is a trade you can watch happen.
CoreC5 · Measure the interference

Implement mean_overlap. Show it is zero for an orthogonal set and necessarily positive when features outnumber dimensions.

Answer. Positive, always, for $m > n$: there is no packing that avoids it. This is why a neuron reads as polysemantic: you are seeing a sum of overlapping features, and no amount of staring decomposes it.
CoreC6 · Importance changes what survives

Rerun C4 with uniform feature importance rather than a decaying one. Which features does the dense model keep now, and does the sparse regime change?

Answer. With uniform importance the dense model's choice of which $n$ to keep becomes arbitrary: it is breaking a tie. The sparse regime is largely unchanged, which says superposition is driven by sparsity rather than by the importance structure.
CoreC7 · Train the sparse autoencoder

Reproduce Figure 1. Sweep the dictionary size and the $\ell_1$ coefficient, and measure recovery against the known directions.

Answer. Recovery improves with dictionary size and does not reach 20 of 20: 12 at four times overcomplete. The $\ell_1$ coefficient trades reconstruction against sparsity, and there is a broad middle where both are acceptable and the recovered set differs between them. That last observation is §6's feature-splitting problem arriving in your own run.
CoreC8 · The control PCA provides

Run PCA on the same activations and compare recovery. Explain the ceiling before you run it.

Answer. At most $n$ components for $n$ dimensions, so at most $n$ features: the measured result is 1 of 20. A method that can exceed the dimension is a genuine requirement, not a preference, and this is the control that shows it.
CoreC9 · A probe for something the model ignores

Reproduce Figure 2. Train on a task depending on one block of the input; probe for a property of a different block.

Answer. The probe reaches 0.775, above the network's own task accuracy of 0.753. Nothing is wrong: the hidden layer is a function of the whole input, so the information is there whether or not anything uses it.
CoreC10 · Ablate what is present against what is used

Project the activations off the unused property's probe direction, and separately off the direction the network's own output layer reads for the task (its weights, not a second probe's approximation of them). Include a random-direction control of the same norm.

Answer. +0.001 for the unused property's probed direction, −0.003 for a random one (indistinguishable from each other, which is why the control is not optional) and −0.249 for the direction the network's own readout actually uses, collapsing the task to chance. A second probe trained to find "the direction the task uses" is not guaranteed to find the one the network's own output layer reads, and in a redundantly-encoded hidden layer it may not: only the network's literal decision boundary is guaranteed to cost something when removed.
CoreC11 · Patch a known computation

Reproduce Figure 3. Train on $(a+b) \bmod 10$ with an irrelevant third input, then sweep the patch site over layers and positions.

Answer. 0.98 at $a$'s position in layer 0, 1.00 at the answer position by layer 3, and the crossover between layers 1 and 2. You have localised a computation without interpreting anything.
CoreC12 · The column that reads zero

In C11, explain $b$'s zero before you look at $c$'s. Then corrupt $b$ instead and rerun.

Answer. $b$ is identical in both runs, so splicing it changes nothing: necessary to the computation and invisible to this experiment. $c$ is zero for a different reason: it is genuinely unused. The two zeros mean opposite things and the method cannot distinguish them. Only your choice of corruption can.
CoreC13 · Corrupt both

Now corrupt $a$ and $b$ together and try to read the map.

Answer. It stops decomposing: restoring either alone gives a fraction that no longer corresponds to a share of the computation. Minimal pairs are what make the arithmetic mean anything, and this is the fastest way to see why.
CoreC14 · A steering vector, swept

Build a difference-in-means direction for a property in a small model. Sweep the coefficient and measure both the target property and one you did not intend to change.

Answer. A real direction moves the target monotonically and leaves the other alone until the coefficient is large. If both move together, the direction is entangled and the name you gave it is doing work the measurement does not support.
CoreC15 · Goodhart, measured

Reproduce Figure 4. Match the correlation between the two arms exactly, then sweep the selection pressure.

Answer. Light-tailed climbs to 2.599; heavy-tailed peaks at 2.042 near $n = 118$ and collapses to 0.097. Matching the correlation is the step that makes the comparison mean something: without it the result is confounded and proves nothing.
CoreC16 · What ensembling actually buys

Average $k$ independent heavy-tailed proxies. Measure the standard deviation and the normalised tail statistic separately, then the best-of-$n$ reward. Predict all three first.

Answer. The scale falls as expected; the tail shape does not improve, and by the normalised statistic it can look worse. Averaging preserves a tail index below 2, so an ensemble moves you along the heavy curve rather than onto the light one. The common claim that ensembling fixes Goodhart is stronger than this measurement supports.
CoreC17 · What a KL penalty does

Add a penalty on distance from the reference and sweep its coefficient, measuring true reward and proxy reward separately.

Answer. It caps the effective search, so it holds you near the peak of Figure 4's heavy curve at the cost of never reaching the light curve's ceiling. That is the whole trade, and it explains why the coefficient is tuned rather than derived.
CoreC18 · Hack a judge on purpose

Build a judge with a known bias: length, or position. Optimise against it with best-of-$n$ and measure the true quality of what you selected.

Answer. Judge score rises, true quality does not, and the outputs get longer. Part XX §42 measured the bias; this measures what optimising into it costs. Do it once and model-as-judge will never again look like a free evaluation.
CoreC19 · The three fairness criteria, in code

Implement all three on a calibrated score with different base rates per group. Check whether all three can hold.

Answer. They cannot. Construct the data by drawing the probability first and the outcome from it; then the score is calibrated by construction, and you can watch parity and equalised odds fail regardless of what you do downstream.
CoreC20 · Size a real evaluation

Pick a system you use. Write the claim you would want to make about its failure rate, compute the evaluation size it needs, and cost it in hours.

Answer. Usually unaffordable, which is the finding. The right response is to weaken the claim to what the budget supports and say so, not to run the smaller study and state the stronger claim, which is what happens by default.
CoreC21 · Correlated trials

Simulate an evaluation suite where prompts come in templated clusters. Compare the true coverage of a nominal 95% interval against the independent-trials calculation.

Answer. Below 95%, and further below as the clusters get tighter. §27's arithmetic is the optimistic case, and most real suites are clustered by construction.
CoreC22 · The randomised-weights sanity check

Take any attribution method. Run it on a trained network and on the same architecture with random weights, and compare the outputs.

Answer. For several published methods the two are nearly identical: Adebayo et al. (2018). A method that cannot distinguish a trained model from an untrained one is not describing the model. Run this before trusting any new interpretability method, including the ones in this part.
CoreC23 · Write a safety case

For a system you have built, write the four sections of §32: claim, evidence, assumptions, defeaters.

Answer. The assumptions section is where it becomes uncomfortable, which is the point of the format. If you cannot state a defeater, you have written a summary rather than an argument.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · A dictionary on a real model

Train a sparse autoencoder on activations from a small pretrained language model. Then run the verification: pick a dictionary entry, predict which inputs should activate it, and test the prediction on inputs you constructed.

Answer. The gap between "the top activations look coherent" and "my prediction held" is the whole subject of §15, and running it once is worth more than reading about it.
StretchS2 · Find a circuit

Take a small transformer and a specific behaviour. Localise it with patching, identify the components, then run the sufficiency and necessity checks from §11.

Answer. Sufficiency usually fails first: ablate everything outside your circuit and the behaviour degrades more than expected, which means the circuit is a partial account. Report the fraction it explains rather than declaring it found.
StretchS3 · Reproduce a reward hack

Train a reward model on preference data you generate, then optimise a policy against it with PPO. Track proxy and true reward on the same axes.

Answer. Divergence appears earlier than with best-of-$n$, because gradient ascent finds the extreme more efficiently than sampling. This is Part XVIII's machinery producing Part XXI's failure, and seeing them as one system is the objective.
StretchS4 · Weak-to-strong, at your scale

Supervise a larger model using labels from a smaller one and measure how much of the larger model's ceiling is recovered.

Answer. More than the weak labels' accuracy, which is the effect. Then vary the capability gap and see whether the recovery fraction holds: that extrapolation is the paper's load-bearing assumption and the thing nobody can currently check where it matters.
StretchS5 · Red-team something you built

Run all three modes from §26 against your own system for a fixed budget. Fix what you find. Then have somebody who did not see the first round run it again.

Answer. If the second round's success rate matches the first, you patched instances rather than the vulnerability. This is the single most informative experiment in the part and almost nobody runs the second round.
StretchS6 · Interpretability that predicts

Form a mechanistic hypothesis about a model. Derive from it a specific input on which the model should fail. Construct the input and test.

Answer. The only interpretability evidence that does not require knowing the ground truth in advance, and therefore the only kind that scales to systems you did not build. A hypothesis that generates no such prediction is a description.
StretchS7 · Audit a published safety claim

Take a model card or system card. For each safety claim, identify the evaluation size, the elicitation effort, and the assumption that would have to hold. Write the safety case the document implies.

Answer. Most claims will turn out to be bounded by §27's arithmetic far more loosely than the prose suggests, and the elicitation budget is usually absent. Doing this once changes how you read every such document afterwards.
