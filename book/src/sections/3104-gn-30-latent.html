Part CLatent variables and the VAE

§14 The latent variable model

The construction

Draw a latent $\mathbf{z}$ from a simple prior, a standard Gaussian will do, and push it through a network to get a distribution over $\mathbf{x}$:

$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \int p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})\, p(\mathbf{z})\, d\mathbf{z}$$

Each $p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})$ can be trivially simple, a Gaussian with mean $g(\mathbf{z})$, and the mixture over $\mathbf{z}$ is arbitrarily complex. That is the appeal: complexity comes from the integral, not from the pieces.

Sampling is ancestral (§6) and therefore easy: draw $\mathbf{z}$, push it through, draw $\mathbf{x}$. One pass, whatever the dimension.

Why the integral is the whole problem

To train by maximum likelihood you need $\log p(\mathbf{x})$, which needs that integral, which is over every possible $\mathbf{z}$. It has no closed form once $g$ is a network.

Monte Carlo is available and useless: draw $\mathbf{z}$ from the prior and average $p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})$. In high dimensions almost every prior sample explains the observed $\mathbf{x}$ terribly, so essentially all the mass of the estimator comes from the rare draws that happen to land near the right $\mathbf{z}$, and you will not draw them.

The fix is to draw $\mathbf{z}$ from somewhere informed by $\mathbf{x}$, which is what the posterior $p(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x})$ is, and which is equally intractable. §15 is the way out.

§15 The evidence lower bound

Derived in four lines

Introduce any distribution $q(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x})$ you like. Then

$$\log p(\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, q) \;+\; D_{KL}\big(q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\big)$$

where

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, q) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim q} \big[\log p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})\big] - D_{KL}\big(q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p(\mathbf{z})\big)$$

Because a KL divergence is never negative, $\mathcal{L} \leq \log p(\mathbf{x})$ always. It is the evidence lower bound, and maximising it is the closest thing to maximum likelihood available here.

The two terms read directly. The first is reconstruction: encode $\mathbf{x}$, decode it, and see how well you did. The second is a penalty for the code being unusual under the prior. Part XV's autoencoder, with a term that says the code must also look like noise. The evidence lower bound returns as the central object of Part XXVIII §18, where variational inference is studied for its own sake rather than as the training rule for a generative model.

Why the gap is exactly the thing you cannot see

The bound's slack is $D_{KL}(q \parallel p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}))$: how wrong your approximate posterior is. It vanishes only when $q$ is the true posterior, which is the object you introduced $q$ to avoid computing.

So the gap is not merely unknown in practice; it is unknown for the same reason the problem was hard. You are reporting a number that is below the quantity you care about by an amount you cannot measure, and §17 shows what that does.

§16 The reparameterisation trick

Go and read

Kingma & Welling (2014), Auto-encoding variational Bayes. The VAE, whose real contribution is narrower and more useful than the architecture: §2.3's reparameterisation, which converts an expectation you cannot differentiate into one you can. Then Burda, Grosse & Salakhutdinov (2016) for the tighter bound that §17 argues should be what you report.

Moving the randomness out of the way

$\mathcal{L}$ contains an expectation over $\mathbf{z} \sim q_\phi$, and $\phi$ is what you are differentiating. You cannot backpropagate through a sampling operation.

Unless you rewrite it. For a Gaussian $q$, instead of drawing $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_\phi, \boldsymbol{\sigma}_\phi^2)$, write

$$\mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_\phi(\mathbf{x}) + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_\phi(\mathbf{x}) \odot \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$$

Now the randomness is in $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, which does not depend on $\phi$, and the path from $\phi$ to $\mathbf{z}$ is an ordinary differentiable expression. Part VII §12's computational graph, with a noise source hanging off the side.

Why this one line is what made the method work

The alternative estimator, the score-function or REINFORCE estimator, works for any $q$ including discrete ones, and has variance so high that training is impractical without heavy variance reduction.

Reparameterisation gives a low-variance gradient for free, because it uses the derivative of the thing being averaged rather than only its value. The restriction is real: it needs $\mathbf{z}$ continuous and $q$ expressible as a differentiable transform of fixed noise. That restriction is why VAEs use Gaussian latents essentially always, and it is the reason discrete latent variables remain awkward.

§17 How loose is the bound

Pitfall: comparing two models by their bounds

This is done constantly, and the figure shows why it is not safe. Model A's bound may exceed model B's while model B's true likelihood exceeds model A's, because the two bounds are loose by different amounts.

A tighter bound is a better inference network, not a better generative model. Reporting ELBOs and concluding something about the models is a comparison of the encoders, partly.

The remedy is available and cheap at evaluation time: the importance-weighted estimate

$$\log p(\mathbf{x}) \approx \log \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}^{(i)})}{q(\mathbf{z}^{(i)} \mid \mathbf{x})}, \qquad \mathbf{z}^{(i)} \sim q$$

which is also a lower bound but tightens as $k$ grows and is what the figure's orange curve is, at $k = 2000$. It costs $k$ decoder passes per example and nothing else. If you are going to report a likelihood, report this one and state $k$.

§18 The VAE, assembled

The pieces, in order

An encoder $q_\phi(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x})$ emitting a mean and variance. A sample drawn by reparameterisation. A decoder $p_\theta(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})$ emitting a distribution over $\mathbf{x}$. One loss, the negative ELBO, and ordinary gradient descent on both networks at once.

Everything Part XV said about autoencoders applies, with one structural addition: the code is required to be distributed like the prior. That is what makes it a generative model rather than a compressor: you can draw $\mathbf{z}$ from the prior and decode it, and get something sensible, which an ordinary autoencoder cannot do.

§19 Posterior collapse

The failure

The KL term is minimised, exactly, when $q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) = p(\mathbf{z})$: the encoder ignores its input entirely. If the decoder is powerful enough to model the data without help, that is a good solution: the KL is zero and the reconstruction term is as good as an unconditional model can make it.

The latent code then carries no information. The model still trains, still reports a respectable ELBO, and has quietly become a plain density model with an unused input.

Why it is worst with the best decoders, and how to detect it

The stronger the decoder, the less it needs $\mathbf{z}$. An autoregressive decoder is the extreme case: it can model the data by itself, so it does, and the latent is abandoned. This is why VAEs with powerful decoders were a recurring disappointment.

The detection is one number and it is not the loss: report the KL term per latent dimension. Dimensions with KL near zero are unused. The figure's right panel is this: at $\beta = 8$ the code carries 1.136 nats and only one of two dimensions is active, and the ELBO does not obviously report a problem.

The standard remedies all work by making the collapsed solution less attractive: KL annealing (start $\beta$ near zero and raise it), free bits (do not penalise the first few nats in each dimension), or weakening the decoder. Note that $\beta$ below 1 means you are no longer optimising a bound on the likelihood at all, a defensible choice that should be stated rather than absorbed.

§20 Why the samples are blurry

Three contributions, and they are separable

The complaint is old and usually attributed to a single cause. There are three, and they can be addressed independently.

The decoder's output distribution. A Gaussian $p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})$ with fixed variance makes the reconstruction term a squared error, and the optimal prediction under squared error is a mean. Where the model is uncertain between two sharp possibilities it outputs their average, which is blurry by construction. This is Part XIV §33's loss-is-not-the-metric, arriving in image space.

The direction of the KL. §4: maximum likelihood is mode covering, so the model spreads mass rather than committing. Blur is what spreading looks like when the data is images.

The bound. The model is not trained on the likelihood but on something below it by an unmeasured amount, and the slack need not be uniform across the data.

Only the first is specific to VAEs, and it is the one that was fixed: by using a discrete decoder over quantised values, or by handing the final step to a different model entirely. Latent diffusion is precisely this: a VAE-style encoder for compression, and §29's machinery for the distribution inside the latent space.

§21 Normalising flows, briefly

Exact likelihood from an invertible network

If $\mathbf{x} = f(\mathbf{z})$ with $f$ invertible and differentiable, the change-of-variables formula gives the density exactly:

$$\log p(\mathbf{x}) = \log p\big(f^{-1}(\mathbf{x})\big) + \log \left| \det \frac{\partial f^{-1}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \right|$$

No bound, no MCMC, exact sampling and exact density. The catch is the determinant: computing it for a general Jacobian costs $O(n^3)$, so every flow architecture is a scheme for making it cheap: triangular Jacobians, coupling layers or restricted transforms.

Why the constraint proved expensive

Invertibility forces $\mathbf{z}$ to have the same dimension as $\mathbf{x}$. There is no bottleneck, no compression and no low-dimensional latent, which contradicts the manifold assumption the whole subject rests on (Part XV §5).

Flows remained an elegant and comparatively underperforming family for exactly this reason: the constraint that buys the exact likelihood is also the constraint that prevents the model from exploiting the structure of the data. They persist where an exact density is genuinely required, and §33 notes where their machinery came back.

§22 What this movement establishes

The trade, stated plainly

A latent variable model buys one-pass sampling and a representation you can manipulate, neither of which the autoregressive family offers, at the price of an intractable integral.

The bound is how that price is paid, and it is paid in a currency you cannot count: the gap is unknown by construction, it varies between models, and it is large enough to reverse a comparison. Everything uncomfortable about evaluating VAEs traces to that one fact.

The next movement takes a different route entirely. Instead of bounding an intractable likelihood, it learns an object that never contained the intractable part in the first place.
