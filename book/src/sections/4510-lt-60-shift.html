Part FDistribution shift

The assumption every guarantee in this part rests on, train and test drawn from the same distribution, is the one that fails first in deployment. This movement classifies the ways it fails, measures when the standard correction helps and when it does nothing, and ends on the quantity that matters when the average stops being the right summary.

§39 Three shifts, and why the distinction is operational

What moved

Covariate shift. $p(x)$ changes, $p(y \mid x)$ does not. The same rule, applied to different inputs.

Label shift. $p(y)$ changes, $p(x \mid y)$ does not. Same classes, different prevalence: a disease becomes rarer, the symptoms it causes are unchanged.

Concept shift. $p(y \mid x)$ itself changes. The rule is different now. Nothing is recoverable from unlabelled data, because the thing that moved is precisely the thing you would need labels to see.

The distinction is operational because the first two are correctable using unlabelled test data and the third is not. Which you face determines whether monitoring can help before anyone hand-labels anything.

§40 Importance weighting

The correction, and its condition

Under covariate shift, reweight each training point by $w(x) = q(x) / p(x)$:

$$\mathbb{E}_q[\ell] = \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} \ell\right]$$

The same identity as Part XXII §32's off-policy evaluation and Part XXII §19's inverse-probability weighting, and it carries the same requirement: the ratio must be finite, so the training distribution must cover the test one. Positivity, arriving for the third time.

Why misspecification is the whole story

If the model can represent $p(y \mid x)$ exactly, then fitting it anywhere recovers it everywhere: the conditional is the same by assumption, and where you sampled the inputs does not matter asymptotically.

When the model cannot, fitting is a compromise: it approximates well where the training data is dense and poorly elsewhere. Move the test distribution into the poorly-approximated region and the error rises. Weighting relocates the compromise to where the test data will be.

Since every real model is misspecified, this reads as an argument for always weighting. The left panel is the counterargument: weights inflate variance, and when the shift is small or the model is good, that cost is paid for nothing. Estimate the shift before correcting for it.

§41 Label shift, which is easier

Correctable from unlabelled data alone

With $p(x \mid y)$ fixed, the correction is a reweighting by the ratio of class priors, a vector of $K$ numbers rather than a function of $x$. The new priors are estimable from unlabelled test data by inverting the confusion matrix of a classifier trained on the source.

This is the most tractable shift and among the most common: class prevalence moves constantly in fraud, disease, moderation and demand. It is worth recognising, because the fix is cheap and the alternative, retraining, is not.

§42 Distributionally robust optimisation

Optimising for the worst nearby distribution

Rather than correcting for a shift you have measured, minimise the worst-case risk over a set of distributions near the training one:

$$\min_{h} \; \max_{q \in \mathcal{U}} \; \mathbb{E}_{q}[\ell(h)]$$

The uncertainty set $\mathcal{U}$ is the design choice, and it decides everything. Too large and the solution is uselessly conservative: the worst case includes distributions nobody will face. Too small and it protects against nothing.

Defining $\mathcal{U}$ by groups rather than by a divergence ball turns out to be the version that works, because groups are the shifts people actually care about.

§43 Worst-group risk

Two things in that figure worth separating

Below chance is not a bad score, it is a signature. A model at 0.5 on the conflicting group would be uninformative. At 0.215 it is systematically inverted: it learned the shortcut so thoroughly that where the shortcut misleads, it is confidently wrong. Part XXII Figure 6 is the same phenomenon in its causal form.

The average was misleading in both directions. The overall figure of 0.600 concealed a group at 0.215, and it was also worse than the robust model's 0.808, because "average" was computed on a training distribution whose group balance did not survive to test. Optimising the average optimises for the group mixture you happened to sample.

Pitfall: you need the group labels

Group-robust training requires knowing which group each training point is in. When the groups are unknown (which is the usual case, because the spurious feature is usually the one nobody identified), the method is unavailable.

Methods that infer groups from the model's own errors exist and are considerably less reliable. The honest summary is that the technique works when you already know what you are worried about, and the failures that matter most are the ones you do not.

§44 Detecting shift, and what detection is worth

What can be seen without labels

Input distribution tests. Two-sample tests on features or embeddings. Detect covariate shift; say nothing about whether it matters.

Confidence and calibration drift. Cheap, and Part XIV §5's calibration is the instrument. A drop in confidence is evidence of shift; stable confidence is not evidence of none, because a model can be confidently wrong.

Output distribution monitoring. The prediction mix moving is often the earliest signal and the easiest to compute.

Nothing detects concept shift without labels. The conditional changed; only labels can see it. This is the case for the sampled human review of Part XXI §35, and it is not substitutable.

§45 What movements A to F establish

Five things

Generalisation has a theory, and it is exact for what it covers. Finite VC dimension is necessary and sufficient for PAC learning, with matching bounds. Figure 2 measures the dimension of a linear classifier by enumeration and gets exactly $d+1$.

That theory is numerically vacuous at modern scale, provably. Figure 3 reads 1.1 to 10.0 where an error rate cannot exceed 1, and because networks can fit random labels, no distribution-free repair is available.

Past the interpolation threshold the question changes. Figure 4's error peaks at 4.708 where the fit becomes exact and then falls to 0.224, better than any underparameterised model, because what matters becomes which exact fit the algorithm selects.

The algorithm is doing the regularising. Figure 5's four solutions all fit exactly and differ in test error by a factor of eighteen; gradient descent finds the minimum-norm one to fifteen digits. This is the right shape for an explanation, and for deep networks the contents are missing.

Every guarantee here assumes a fixed distribution, and deployment breaks it. The correction for covariate shift does nothing when the model is well specified (Figure 6) and the average conceals a group at 0.215 (Figure 7). Know which assumption you are relying on and measure whether it holds.
