Part EWhen the rows are ordered

A table with a time index is a different problem, and treating it as an ordinary table breaks the assumption every guarantee in this book rests on. This movement establishes what changes, borrows what the classical forecasting literature settled decades ago, and measures what a careless split is worth.

§32 What a time index changes

Four assumptions that stop holding

Independence. Consecutive observations are correlated by construction. Part XXIII §6 named this as the first thing concentration needs, and a series violates it maximally.

Exchangeability. The rows have an order that matters, so any procedure invariant to permutation is discarding the signal, including the conformal guarantee of Part XXIV §29.

A fixed distribution. Trends, regime changes and seasonality mean the distribution moves by design rather than as a deployment accident. Part XXIII's movement F is not an edge case here, it is the setting.

Symmetric information. At prediction time you have the past and not the future, so any quantity computed over the whole series (a mean, a scaler, a target encoding) is a leak.

§33 Stationarity, and what differencing buys

The property the classical models require

A series is stationary when its distribution does not depend on when you look: constant mean, constant variance, and autocovariance depending only on the lag.

Most real series are not. The standard repairs: differencing $\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}$ removes a trend, seasonal differencing removes a seasonal pattern, and a log transform stabilises variance that grows with the level.

Differencing is not free. It amplifies noise, and differencing a series that did not need it introduces a moving-average structure that was not there. The question to ask is whether the series has a unit root (a stochastic trend that never reverts) rather than whether it looks like it drifts. The standard instruments are the augmented Dickey–Fuller test, whose null hypothesis is that a unit root is present, and the KPSS test, whose null is the opposite: that the series is stationary. Running both is more informative than either alone, because they can disagree in exactly the region where the answer matters.

§34 Autocorrelation

The two functions to plot before anything else

The autocorrelation function is the correlation of the series with itself at each lag. The partial autocorrelation is the same with the intervening lags' contributions removed.

Between them they diagnose the structure: an autoregressive process has a partial autocorrelation that cuts off at its order and an autocorrelation that decays; a moving-average process is the reverse. A slow linear decay in the autocorrelation says the series is not stationary and should be differenced first.

These two plots cost nothing and are the time-series equivalent of Part XXIV §40's coverage check, the diagnostic that should precede the modelling and usually does not.

§35 The classical models, and why they are still baselines

Go and read

Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice. Free online, and the single best source for this movement and the next. Read Chapter 5 on evaluation before any of the modelling chapters: the split is what decides whether a forecasting result means anything, which is §36's argument made independently by the field's own reference text.

Four, and what each assumes

ARIMA. Autoregressive terms, a differencing order, and moving-average terms. Linear, interpretable, and with a principled order-selection procedure.

Exponential smoothing. A weighted average of the past with geometrically decaying weights, extended to trend and seasonality. Almost no parameters, and it is the method that most often wins forecasting competitions outright at short horizons.

State-space models. Level, trend and season as unobserved states evolving with noise, with a Kalman filter doing the inference. Handles missing observations and irregular sampling naturally, and generalises the previous two.

Seasonal naive. Predict the value from one season ago. It is a baseline, it is frequently hard to beat, and a paper that does not report it should be read with that in mind.

Why these have not been displaced

Forecasting problems are usually short (thousands of observations, not millions) and heavily structured. A method with three parameters and the right structure beats a flexible method with the wrong one at that sample size, which is Part VI §7's no-free-lunch in a setting where it bites.

Neural forecasters win at scale and when many related series share structure. Which is a claim about $n$, not about the year.

§36 What a random split is worth

Where the 18.8 comes from

Under a random split the training set contains observations from immediately before and immediately after each test point. Since the series is smooth and highly autocorrelated, interpolating between two neighbours is nearly trivial, and interpolation is not the task.

Note that nothing here is a bug. The model is fitted correctly, the test set is genuinely held out, no row appears twice, and every diagnostic in Part XIV passes. The estimand was wrong, which is Part XXII §1's distinction arriving in the most common form it takes.

§37 Validation that respects the order

Three schemes

Hold out the tail. Train on the first part, test on the last. Simple, and it gives one estimate from one period, which may be unrepresentative.

Rolling origin. Repeatedly train on everything up to a point and test on what follows, advancing the point. Several estimates, each honest, and it is the standard.

Expanding against sliding window. Expanding uses all history; sliding uses a fixed recent window. Sliding is better when the process changes, and comparing the two is itself a diagnostic for whether it does.

In all three, every fitted quantity (scalers, encoders, imputers, the differencing order) belongs inside the fold. A scaler fitted on the whole series has seen the future.

§38 What movement E establishes

Three things

A time index removes the assumptions everything else rests on: independence, exchangeability, a fixed distribution and symmetric information, all four at once.

A random split reports a model that does not exist. Figure 8 measures 18.8× on a series with ordinary structure, with every conventional diagnostic passing.

The classical methods are baselines rather than history. At the sample sizes forecasting problems actually have, structure beats flexibility, and seasonal naive is the number a result should be reported against.
