Part DThe distribution zoo

A handful of named distributions cover most of what you will meet. Each one is a shape plus a story about when that shape arises. Learn the stories; the formulas follow.

§14 Bernoulli and categorical

Bernoulli: one binary event

A single trial with two outcomes, governed by one parameter $\phi \in [0,1]$:

$$P(X = 1) = \phi, \qquad P(X = 0) = 1 - \phi$$

Both cases fold into one expression, which is a trick worth noticing because it reappears throughout machine learning:

$$P(X = x) = \phi^x (1-\phi)^{1-x}$$

When $x = 1$ the second factor becomes $(1-\phi)^0 = 1$ and vanishes; when $x = 0$ the first does. Exponents used as switches like this show up constantly in likelihood expressions, and recognising the pattern saves you a lot of case analysis.

Its mean is $\phi$ and its variance is $\phi(1-\phi)$, which peaks at $\phi = 0.5$ and vanishes at 0 or 1, matching the obvious intuition that a certain outcome has no variability.

Why deep learning cares

Every binary classifier outputs a Bernoulli. "Spam or not", "fraud or not", "cat or not": the model's job is to produce the single parameter $\phi$ for each input. That is what the sigmoid in §19 is for: it converts an unbounded network output into a legal $\phi$.

Categorical: one event with $k$ outcomes

The generalisation to $k$ possibilities, with a probability vector $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ whose entries are non-negative and sum to 1. The book calls this the multinoulli; most other sources call it categorical.

This is the distribution over class labels in every multi-class classification problem. A 1,000-class image classifier outputs a categorical distribution with 1,000 parameters, and the softmax of §20 is what produces it.

§15 The Gaussian, and why it is everywhere

In 60 seconds

Two knobs (mean and variance) and three separate reasons it dominates: sums of many independent things converge to it (the central limit theorem), it is the maximum-entropy distribution for a given variance so it assumes the least, and it is mathematically convenient in ways that make derivations close in closed form.

Go and read

Jaynes (1957), Information theory and statistical mechanics. The maximum-entropy argument for the Gaussian, the second of the three justifications below, made properly. Jaynes's claim is stronger than it first appears: the Gaussian is not merely convenient, it is the distribution that assumes least given what you know.

History: named after the wrong person, which is the rule not the exception

Abraham de Moivre derived the bell curve in 1733, as an approximation to binomial probabilities for large numbers of coin flips. He published it in an appendix, in Latin, and it attracted almost no attention.

Laplace developed it substantially and proved an early form of the central limit theorem. Gauss used it in 1809 for the distribution of errors in astronomical observation (the same Ceres work described in Part I §13) and made it indispensable.

The name that stuck was Gauss's, roughly seventy-five years after de Moivre had the result. This is Stigler's Law of Eponymy again, met in Part I §9: no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer.

The alternative name, "normal distribution", carries its own problem. Pearson popularised it and later regretted doing so, on the grounds that it invites people to believe other distributions are somehow abnormal. Plenty of real quantities are not Gaussian at all, and the pitfall below is about what happens when you forget that.

Definition: the Gaussian (normal) distribution

$$\mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$ with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$.

The formula looks worse than it is. Read it in three pieces:

  • $(x - \mu)^2$: squared distance from the centre. This is the only place $x$ appears, so the curve is symmetric about $\mu$.
  • $\exp(-\,\cdot\,)$: turns "far from centre" into "small probability", and does so very quickly, since the exponential of a large negative number is minuscule.
  • $1/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}$: a constant chosen purely so the area comes out to 1. It carries no meaning; it is bookkeeping.

Why this distribution and not another

Three genuinely distinct reasons, and it is worth knowing all three because they get invoked in different contexts:

1. The central limit theorem. Add up many independent random contributions (of almost any shape) and the sum tends toward a Gaussian. Since a great many real quantities are sums of many small influences (height, measurement error, noise in a sensor), Gaussians appear constantly in nature without anyone arranging it.

2. Maximum entropy. Among all distributions with a given variance, the Gaussian is the one that assumes the least; it is maximally noncommittal about everything except spread. Section 23 makes "assumes the least" precise, though it does so for a discrete variable; here $X$ is continuous, and the entropy of a continuous variable has its own name, differential entropy (replace the sum with an integral). It behaves less politely than the discrete kind (it can be negative), but the "assumes the least" reading still holds. If all you honestly know is a mean and a variance, choosing anything else means smuggling in assumptions you cannot justify.

3. It is mathematically convenient. Less noble but perfectly real. Gaussians are closed under many operations (sums of Gaussians are Gaussian, marginals of Gaussians are Gaussian, conditionals of Gaussians are Gaussian), and this closure makes otherwise intractable derivations work out in closed form.

Pitfall: assuming Gaussian when the data is not

The Gaussian's tails decay extremely fast, which means it treats far-out values as essentially impossible. Many real quantities have much heavier tails: financial returns, city sizes, word frequencies, network traffic. Modelling those as Gaussian will make you systematically underestimate the chance of extreme events, and the failure is silent until an extreme event arrives.

Also worth remembering: the central limit theorem is about sums. It says nothing about individual measurements, and it requires finite variance, which some real distributions do not have.

§16 The multivariate Gaussian

The generalisation to a vector-valued variable replaces the mean with a vector and the variance with a covariance matrix:

$$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^n \det(\Sigma)}} \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right)$$

Structurally it is identical to the scalar version. The squared distance $(x-\mu)^2$ has become the quadratic form $(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})$ from Part I §18, and division by $\sigma^2$ has become multiplication by $\Sigma^{-1}$. Same shape, more dimensions.

Some sources parametrise the univariate Gaussian with precision $\beta = 1/\sigma^2$ rather than variance, and the multivariate version with the precision matrix $\Lambda = \Sigma^{-1}$ rather than the covariance. The density then reads $\mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \beta^{-1}) \propto \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{\beta}{2}(x-\mu)^2\right)$. The choice is algebraic convenience: precision appears directly in the exponent and eliminates a matrix inversion in log-likelihood calculations.

That quadratic form is a distance measure that accounts for correlation; it is called the Mahalanobis distance. A point two units away along a direction the data rarely varies in is far more surprising than a point two units along a direction it varies in a lot, and $\Sigma^{-1}$ encodes exactly that rescaling.

Why deep learning cares: the cost of a full covariance

A full $\Sigma$ over $n$ variables carries $n(n+1)/2$ parameters and requires inverting an $n \times n$ matrix. At $n = 1000$ that is half a million parameters and an expensive, potentially ill-conditioned inverse (Part I §13).

So models overwhelmingly assume a diagonal covariance (the middle panel), which costs only $n$ parameters and inverts by reciprocating entries. The price is assuming no correlation between dimensions, which is usually false. Variational autoencoders (Part XVI §14–20) make exactly this assumption, and knowing it is an approximation rather than a fact is part of reading that movement properly.

§17 Exponential, Laplace, Dirac, empirical

Exponential and Laplace

The exponential distribution puts all its mass on non-negative values, decaying from a peak at zero. It is the natural model for waiting times: how long until the next bus, the next request, the next failure.

The Laplace distribution is its two-sided cousin, with a sharp peak at some point $\mu$ and exponential decay in both directions.

Why deep learning cares: this is $L^1$ regularization

Part I §15 said that $L^1$ regularization produces sparsity and explained it geometrically, through the corners of the $L^1$ ball. Here is the probabilistic explanation of the same fact.

Putting a Laplace prior on the weights and taking the negative log gives a penalty proportional to $\|\mathbf{w}\|_1$: that is $L^1$ regularization. A Gaussian prior gives $\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$, which is weight decay. The sharp peak of the Laplace at zero is a statement that weights are a priori likely to be exactly zero, and that belief is what drives them there. Two chapters, two derivations, one phenomenon.

Dirac and empirical distributions

The Dirac delta $\delta(x - \mu)$ places all probability at a single point. It is not a function in the ordinary sense (it is infinitely tall and infinitesimally wide, with total area 1), and it is best thought of as the limit of a Gaussian whose variance shrinks to zero.

Its real use is assembling the empirical distribution: put a Dirac spike on each of your $m$ data points, each with weight $1/m$.

$$\hat{p}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \delta(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^{(i)})$$
Intuition: the empirical distribution is your dataset

This formalises something you already do without thinking. Sampling from the empirical distribution means picking a training example uniformly at random, which is exactly what a data loader does every step.

It also frames the central problem of the whole field. You have $\hat{p}$, the distribution of the data you collected. You want to do well on $p$, the distribution of data in the world. Those are not the same, and every phrase you have heard about generalisation, overfitting, and distribution shift is a statement about the gap between them. Part V §20 is largely about that gap.

§18 Mixtures and latent variables

Combine several distributions by choosing among them at random:

$$P(x) = \sum_{i} P(c = i)\, P(x \mid c = i)$$

Sampling happens in two steps: pick a component $c$ according to its weight, then sample from that component. The variable $c$ is latent; it participates in generating the data but is never observed.

Intuition: latent variables are the hidden cause

Heights in a population are roughly bimodal, and the reason is that the population contains subgroups with different average heights. If you never record which subgroup someone belongs to, that membership is a latent variable, and the observed distribution is a mixture.

Latent variables let a simple model produce complicated behaviour by supposing a hidden structure that explains it. This idea scales all the way up: the "code" in an autoencoder is a latent variable, and so is the noise vector fed to a generative model. Parts XV and XVI are, from one angle, an extended study of what you can do with variables you never get to see.

Try it

A mixture has two components: with probability 0.7, $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$; with probability 0.3, $\mathcal{N}(10, 1)$. What is the mean of the mixture, and would you describe it as a typical value?

Answer. By linearity of expectation, the mean is $0.7 \times 0 + 0.3 \times 10 = 3$. But the mixture has essentially no mass anywhere near 3; the samples cluster around 0 and around 10, and a value of 3 would be quite unusual. This is Figure 5's warning at full strength: for a multimodal distribution the mean can be deeply unrepresentative, and reporting it alone actively misleads. Always look at the shape, not just the summary.
