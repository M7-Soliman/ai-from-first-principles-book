Part EUncertainty and adversaries

Two ways the clean picture breaks. Either the value of a state cannot be computed and must be sampled, which turns search into a statistical estimation problem; or somebody else chooses half the moves and is trying to make you lose. The first produced Monte Carlo tree search, the second produced alpha–beta, and modern systems use both at once.

§29 The exploration problem, in miniature

Multi-armed bandits

$K$ arms, each returning a random reward from an unknown distribution. Pull one per round for $T$ rounds and maximise the total. There is no state and no transition: it is a search problem stripped to nothing but the choice of where to look next.

The performance measure is regret: the total you would have got by always pulling the best arm, minus what you got. Minimising regret forces the trade every search algorithm faces: spend a pull confirming the arm you like, or spend it on one you know little about.

§30 Optimism

Upper confidence bounds

$$\text{UCB}_i = \hat{\mu}_i + c\sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{n_i}}$$

Pull the arm with the largest score. The first term is the estimated mean; the second is a confidence width that shrinks with the pulls given to that arm and grows with total time, so an arm ignored for long enough eventually looks attractive again regardless of its history.

This is optimism in the face of uncertainty: act as though each arm is as good as its evidence permits. It gives regret growing like $\ln T$ rather than $T$ (you stop paying for exploration almost entirely) and the same rule reappears as the exploration bonus in Part XVIII §35, which is not a coincidence but the same theorem.

§31 Monte Carlo tree search

Four steps, repeated until the budget runs out

Selection. From the root, descend by a bandit rule until reaching a node with unexpanded children. Expansion. Add one child. Simulation. Play out to a terminal state, by any cheap policy, historically random. Backpropagation. Add the outcome to the visit counts and value sums of every node on the path.

Two properties make this different from everything in Part B. The tree grows asymmetrically, deep where the search believes the action is and shallow elsewhere, with no depth limit anywhere. And it is anytime: stopping after any number of simulations leaves a usable answer, and no evaluation function is required, only the ability to play to the end.

The move that made it work is worth stating on its own, because it is the piece people skip. Applying UCB at every internal node (treating each node's children as a bandit) is UCT, and it is what turns a Monte Carlo average into a search. The value estimate at a node is not the mean of its rollouts; asymptotically it converges to the value under optimal play, because the bandit rule concentrates the samples on the branch that is actually best. Averaging alone converges to the value of a random player, which is a different and useless quantity.

§32 The exploration constant is not a property of the algorithm

The line to remember is the flat one

At $c=0.05$ the success rate is 36%, 32%, 31% as the budget grows sixteenfold. It does not improve at all. Too little exploration commits to the arm with the best average early, and then spends every additional simulation confirming it: more compute makes the wrong answer more confident, not less wrong.

Two things follow. A badly tuned exploration constant is not rescued by compute, so "it will be fine with a bigger budget" is a specific claim that is specifically false here. And because the optimum moves with the budget, a constant tuned during development at small budgets is the wrong constant in production at large ones, a mismatch that shows up as a system that improves less than it should with more thinking time, and gets diagnosed as everything except its exploration parameter.

§33 What replacing the rollout changed

From random playouts to learned priors

Classical MCTS estimated a node's value by playing randomly to the end. That works when random play is informative about the position: true in Go, false in chess, which is why MCTS conquered one and not the other for a decade.

The modern form replaces both halves with a network. A policy head supplies a prior over moves that biases selection toward plausible ones, entering the bandit rule as an extra term that decays with visits; a value head replaces the rollout entirely, so no game is ever played to the end. The search then improves on the policy (the visit distribution is a better move distribution than the raw prior) and training the network to imitate the search closes the loop. That loop is the whole of AlphaZero, and it is a policy-improvement operator in the sense of Part XVIII §10 rather than a new idea about search.

The general lesson survives the specific system: search amplifies a policy. It converts computation at the moment of use into quality, at a rate set by how good the policy and value already are. A weak prior makes search expensive, a strong prior makes it cheap, and neither removes the need for it.

§34 Adversaries

Minimax

Two players, alternating, opposing objectives. The value of a node is the maximum over children if it is your move and the minimum if it is theirs; leaves are evaluated directly. The recursion computes what the position is worth under optimal play by both sides.

Cost is $O(b^d)$ and the depth is what you want, so the entire subject is how to get more depth for the same nodes. Two things buy it: cutting off the recursion early and applying a learned or hand-built evaluation function in place of the true value, and proving that some branches cannot matter.

§35 Alpha–beta

Two numbers that make most of the tree irrelevant

Carry $\alpha$, the best value the maximiser can already guarantee, and $\beta$, the best the minimiser can. If at any node $\alpha\ge\beta$, the opponent will never allow this position to be reached, so the remaining children need not be examined at all.

The pruning is exact: alpha–beta returns precisely the minimax value, never an approximation. It is not a heuristic and it costs nothing in quality, which is rare enough to be worth saying twice.

§36 Ordering is the whole game

Why this is the most valuable line in the movement

Node count $b^{d/2}$ instead of $b^{d}$ means that for the same budget you search twice as deep. No evaluation function improvement in the history of chess engines has been worth as much as a doubling of depth, and it is bought entirely by trying the good move first.

Which is circular (you would have to search to know which move is good) and the resolution is that you do not need to be right, only better than random. Engines use the best move from a shallower search of the same position (iterative deepening pays for itself several times over here), moves that caused a cutoff at the same depth elsewhere, and captures before quiet moves. All are cheap approximations and all of the benefit in Figure 7 is available to approximations, because the curve between random and perfect ordering is steep at the random end.

The transferable statement: in any pruning search, the order in which you try things is worth more than the pruning rule. It applies to branch and bound in §19, to constraint solvers, and to any system that expands candidate reasoning steps in some order and stops when it finds one that works.

§37 When the game is not chess

Four generalisations, each of which breaks something

Normal form. Players choose simultaneously; a payoff matrix says what everyone gets. A Nash equilibrium is a profile where nobody gains by deviating alone; one always exists in mixed strategies. In two-player zero-sum games the equilibrium value is unique and computable by linear programming, which is why that case feels like optimisation and the general case does not.

General sum. Equilibria multiply, they need not be comparable, and the best one for everybody may not be an equilibrium at all: the prisoner's dilemma is the standard demonstration that individually rational play can be collectively terrible. There is no longer a single number to search for.

Extensive form. Sequential play with a game tree; minimax is the zero-sum special case. With imperfect information, states you cannot distinguish form an information set, and the search must reason about a set of possible worlds rather than a position. Optimal play now requires randomising (any deterministic poker strategy is exploitable), which breaks every algorithm in this part, all of which return a single best move.

Mechanism design. The inverse problem: choose the rules so that self-interested play produces the outcome you want. The relevant idea for anyone building systems is incentive compatibility: an evaluation, a benchmark or a reward model is a mechanism, and if reporting honestly is not the best strategy for the thing being measured, the measurement will eventually be gamed. Part XXI's reward hacking is that statement in a different vocabulary.
