Part EWhat goes wrong

§18 Vanishing and exploding gradients

In 60 seconds

Equation 2 of §14 multiplies the gradient by one factor per layer. Products of many factors do not stay put: if they are consistently below 1 the gradient vanishes exponentially with depth, and if above 1 it explodes. This was the practical reason deep networks were untrainable for two decades.

Go and read

He, Zhang, Ren & Sun (2016), Deep residual learning for image recognition. One structural change (add the input of a block to its output) and depth stopped being the limiting factor. The paper's most useful observation is the one it opens with, and it is not about accuracy: a 56-layer plain network had higher training error than a 20-layer one, which rules out overfitting and points squarely at this section.

Unrolling the backward recursion across $L$ layers gives a product:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(1)}} \;=\; \prod_{k=2}^{L} \Big( W^{(k)\top} \cdot \operatorname{diag}\big(g'(\mathbf{z}^{(k-1)})\big) \Big) \cdot \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)}$$

Part 0 §17 predicted exactly this from the chain rule alone, before any neural network had been mentioned. Here it is with numbers.

Intuition: the 0.25 ceiling, compounded

Part 0 §18 computed that the sigmoid's derivative peaks at exactly $0.25$. So every sigmoid layer multiplies the gradient by at most a quarter, and usually much less.

Ten layers gives at best $0.25^{10} \approx 10^{-6}$. Twenty gives $10^{-12}$. That is the best case, assuming every unit sits at its most favourable point. The early layers of a deep sigmoid network are, for practical purposes, frozen at their initial values.

ReLU's derivative is exactly 1 wherever the unit is active. It neither shrinks nor amplifies, which is the entire reason it replaced the sigmoid, and it is a property of the derivative, not of the function.

The four fixes, and what each attacks

FixAttacks
ReLU-family activationsthe $g'$ factor: makes it 1 instead of $\leq 0.25$
Scaled initialisation (§11)the $W^\top$ factor: sets its scale to roughly 1
Normalisation layersrescales activations mid-network, so drift cannot accumulate
Residual connectionsadds a path that skips the multiplication entirely

The last is the most direct. If a layer computes $\mathbf{a} + f(\mathbf{a})$ rather than $f(\mathbf{a})$, then differentiating gives $1 + f'$, so the gradient has a route through the $1$ that is never multiplied down. Very deep networks became trainable largely because of this one structural change.

Exploding gradients have a blunter remedy: clipping, from Part I §15. Rescale the gradient whenever its norm exceeds a threshold. It is inelegant and it works, and it is close to mandatory for recurrent architectures.

§19 Dead units and other pathologies

ReLU solves vanishing gradients and introduces its own failure.

Dead ReLU

If a unit's pre-activation is negative for every input in the dataset, its output is always zero and its gradient is always zero. It therefore never updates, and it is dead permanently.

Remedies: a smaller learning rate, leaky ReLU so the gradient is never exactly zero, or a small positive initial bias so units start active.

The other failures worth recognising

Saturation
Sigmoid or tanh units pushed far from zero, where the derivative is negligible. The unit is not dead, but it learns very slowly.
Exploding loss, then nan
Almost always the learning rate. Part III §9 gave the stability bound; exceeding it diverges, and after a few steps the values exceed float range. Lower the rate before investigating anything else.
Loss stuck exactly flat from step one
Usually a broken gradient path: a detached tensor, a missing connection, or a layer whose output the loss does not depend on. Check that the gradient is non-zero before concluding anything about optimisation.
Loss decreasing then plateauing far above zero
Either genuine underfitting, or the irreducible error of Part V §20. The learning curve of Part VI §6 distinguishes them.

§20 Diagnosing a training run

The diagnostic order
  1. Can it overfit a tiny sample? Take ten examples and train until the loss is near zero. If it cannot, the bug is in the model or the gradient, not in generalisation. This is the single most useful test available and almost nobody runs it first.
  2. Is the gradient finite and non-zero? Print its norm. Zero means a broken path; nan means an overflow upstream.
  3. Does a gradient check pass? Part 0's drill C16, layer by layer. This finds hand-written backward passes that are subtly wrong.
  4. Sweep the learning rate over orders of magnitude. Figure 12 is what you are looking for.
  5. Only then consider architecture, regularization or data.
Why this order

Because the failures are ordered by how cheap they are to rule out, and because a bug masquerading as an optimisation problem will absorb days.

Step 1 is decisive. A network that cannot memorise ten examples has something structurally wrong (a shape error, a detached gradient, a label misalignment) and no amount of tuning will help. A network that can memorise ten and fails on ten thousand has a genuine learning problem, and Part V §20's diagnosis applies.

Part FThe payoff

§21 The autograd engine

Everything in this part converges here. An automatic differentiation engine is about two hundred lines, and writing one is the point at which neural networks stop being something you use and become something you understand.

The idea, in full:

  1. Wrap every value in an object that records what produced it.
  2. Each operation, when it runs, stores a small function saying how to send a gradient backward through itself.
  3. Calling backward() on the final scalar walks the graph in reverse, accumulating gradients into every value that contributed.
class Value:
    def __init__(self, data, parents=()):
        self.data = data
        self.grad = 0.0
        self._parents = parents
        self._backward = lambda: None   # set by whichever op made this

    def __mul__(self, other):
        out = Value(self.data * other.data, (self, other))
        def _backward():
            # local derivative of a product, times the incoming gradient
            self.grad  += other.data * out.grad
            other.grad += self.data  * out.grad
        out._backward = _backward
        return out

    def backward(self):
        order, seen = [], set()
        def build(v):                      # topological sort
            if id(v) in seen: return
            seen.add(id(v))
            for p in v._parents: build(p)
            order.append(v)
        build(self)
        self.grad = 1.0                     # dL/dL
        for v in reversed(order):
            v._backward()
Intuition: why the topological sort

A node's gradient is only complete once every consumer of it has contributed. If a value feeds two later operations, both must run before its gradient is final.

Sorting the graph topologically and walking it in reverse guarantees exactly that. It is also why gradients are accumulated with += rather than assigned: a value used twice receives two contributions, and both belong in the total.

This is the one part of the implementation people get wrong, and its symptom is a gradient check that fails only on graphs with reuse.

Why building this matters

After writing it, several things that felt like framework magic become obvious:

  • Why gradients must be zeroed between steps: because += accumulates.
  • Why the graph must be retained until backward(): §17.
  • Why detaching a tensor stops gradient flow: it severs the parent link.
  • Why in-place operations can corrupt a backward pass: they overwrite a value some _backward still needs.
  • Why a custom layer needs a custom backward: nothing else knows its local derivative.

None of these are memorised facts once you have written the twenty lines they follow from.

Try it: the last one

In the engine above, why is self.grad += ... an addition rather than an assignment? Construct an expression where using = gives the wrong answer.

Answer. Because a value can feed more than one operation, and each consumer contributes a separate term to its gradient.

Take $y = x \cdot x$. The single node $x$ appears twice as a parent, so _backward fires twice (once for each slot), each contributing $x \cdot \bar{y}$. Accumulating gives $2x$, which is correct.

With assignment, the second write overwrites the first and you get $x$: exactly half the true gradient. The bug is invisible on expressions where nothing is reused, and appears the moment anything shares a value, which in a real network is constantly. This is precisely what a gradient check catches, and why Part 0 made you build one before you needed it.

In the framework: your Value class, at industrial scale

PyTorch's autograd is the engine you have just written. Not something like it, but the same three ideas, with a great deal of engineering on top. Every piece has a counterpart:

import torch

a = torch.tensor(2.0, requires_grad=True)
b = a * 3
print(type(b.grad_fn).__name__)      ### MulBackward0

That name is your _backward closure for multiplication, with a class instead of a lambda. The 0 is just an index over overloads. When you read a stack trace full of AddmmBackward and ReluBackward, you are reading the list of local derivatives the graph recorded on the way forward.

The reuse case, checked

x = torch.tensor(3.0, requires_grad=True)
y = x * x
y.backward()
print(x.grad.item())                 ### 6.0   -- not 3.0

Exactly the expression from the answer above, and exactly the result the accumulation produces. Had PyTorch used = instead of += it would print 3.0, and every network in the world would train at half the correct gradient on any shared value.

The five consequences, now with names

The "why building this matters" box listed five things that stop being magic. Here is what each is called:

  • Zeroing gradients: opt.zero_grad(). Because +=.
  • Retaining the graph: a second backward() raises an error unless you pass retain_graph=True, because the buffers were freed. §17's memory argument is why they are freed by default.
  • Detaching: x.detach() returns a tensor with the parent link cut; requires_grad becomes False and the gradient stops there. This is how you freeze part of a model, or stop a recurrent network backpropagating through all of history.
  • In-place operations: anything ending in an underscore, like x.add_(1), overwrites storage some _backward may still need. PyTorch detects this with a version counter and raises rather than returning a wrong answer, which is a kindness your own engine does not extend to you.
  • Custom backward: subclass torch.autograd.Function and write forward and backward yourself. That is your _backward, with a form to fill in.

And the project's deliverable is a built-in

torch.autograd.gradcheck(f, (x,))    ### True

It compares the analytic gradient against numerical differentiation, exactly as Stage 1 of the project asks, and it requires float64 inputs, for the reason Part III §3 gives: the central difference loses digits to cancellation, and float32 does not have enough of them to spare. That constraint is not arbitrary; it is a numerical fact you have already derived, showing up as an argument requirement.

TensorFlow records the same graph with explicit scoping (with tf.GradientTape() as tape: ... then tape.gradient(loss, vars)), which makes the recording visible rather than implicit. Same engine, same topological reverse walk, different opinion about whether you should have to say when it starts.
