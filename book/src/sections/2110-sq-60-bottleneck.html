Part FThe bottleneck

§27 Encoder–decoder

In 60 seconds

Everything so far maps a sequence to an output of the same length, or to a single vector, or a vector to a sequence. Translation needs none of those: the input and output lengths differ and are not known in advance. Bolting a sequence-to-vector model onto a vector-to-sequence model solves it, and creates the problem this part ends on.

Go and read

Sutskever, Vinyals & Le (2014), Sequence to sequence learning with neural networks. The architecture of this section, and the first competitive neural translation. Then Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (2015), which identifies the fixed-size context as the bottleneck and replaces it with a variable-length one the decoder queries. Read the second immediately after the first: it is the moment attention enters the field, and it enters as a repair rather than as a revolution.

§5 gave the two halves. An RNN reading a sequence and emitting its final state is a sequence-to-vector map. An RNN conditioned on a fixed vector and generating until it stops is a vector-to-sequence map. Compose them.

The sequence-to-sequence architecture

An encoder reads $\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(n_x)}$ and emits a context $C$, usually a simple function of its final hidden state. A decoder, conditioned on $C$, generates $\mathbf{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{(n_y)}$.

The two are trained jointly to maximise $\log P(\mathbf{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{(n_y)} \mid \mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(n_x)})$ over the training pairs. Crucially $n_x$ and $n_y$ are unconstrained: the earlier architectures all required them equal.

The context enters the decoder as its initial state, as an extra input at every step, or both (§10 of ch10's three options, and all three are used). The encoder and decoder need not be the same size.

This was proposed twice, independently, in 2014, and produced the first competitive neural machine translation. It is a genuinely good idea and the reason to dwell on it is the flaw that is visible in the definition if you look for it.

Every fact the decoder will ever need about the input must pass through $C$. A fifty-word sentence and a five-word sentence are compressed into the same fixed-size vector. §2 said the state is a lossy summary and that lossy is fine, and here is the case where it is not, because translation is exactly the task that needs the input recoverable.

Why widening $C$ does not work, and what that rules out

The right panel is the load-bearing measurement. If the problem were simply that $C$ was too small, then making $C$ large would solve it, and the curves would rise steeply and flatten near the top. They do not: they rise gently and stay low.

Two things are going on. Capacity is only half the difficulty: the encoder must compress a variable-length input into a fixed representation, and the decoder must decompress it in a different order, and both operations have to be learned through the same recurrence with all of Part C's difficulties. Widening $C$ adds capacity and makes the learning problem no easier.

More fundamentally, the architecture forces a commitment. At the moment the encoder finishes, it must have decided what to keep: before knowing which output token it will be asked for first. A translation whose first output word depends on the last input word requires the encoder to have anticipated that. No fixed-size summary computed in ignorance of the question can be right for every question.

§28 What this part could not do

Four limitations, stated as precisely as this part can state them, because the next part is organised around removing them.

The path between distant positions is long
Information from step 1 reaches step 1,000 by passing through 999 applications of the same transformation. §11 showed that product decays or explodes exponentially; §12 showed that the decay is the price of stable memory rather than a fixable defect; §18 showed that gating puts the decay under the model's control without removing it. The shortest path between two positions is still $O(\tau)$.
Training cannot be parallelised over the sequence
$\mathbf{h}^{(t)}$ requires $\mathbf{h}^{(t-1)}$. This is not an implementation detail; it is what recurrence means. §5 noted that giving up hidden-to-hidden recurrence buys complete parallelism at the cost of expressiveness, and filed the trade for later. It is the single largest reason recurrent models were displaced, because it caps how much data a model can be trained on in a given time, and Part XVII is about what happens when that cap is lifted.
A fixed-size context cannot serve every question
§27, measured in Figure 9. Any architecture that summarises before it knows what will be asked must guess, and widening the summary does not stop it being a guess.
Stationarity is assumed and never checked
§8. One transition function for all time. Where that is false the model cannot express that it is false, and the symptom is a fit that is quietly worse rather than an error.
Where this stands in 2026

Recurrent networks are no longer the default for sequences, and the reason is the second limitation rather than the first. Gating handled long dependencies well enough for most practical purposes; what could not be handled was that a model which must process step $t-1$ before step $t$ cannot use a machine built to do ten thousand things at once.

They are not gone. Where memory is bounded and the sequence arrives one item at a time (streaming audio, on-device inference, control), a recurrent model has an advantage no attention-based model has: constant memory per step, regardless of how long the sequence has been running. An attention model's cost grows with everything it has seen; a recurrent model's does not.

That advantage is why the line is alive rather than historical. State space models, S4 and its descendants, are recurrences designed so that the sequential form is available at inference and a parallel form is available for training, which is an attempt to keep the memory property while removing the second limitation. Whether that succeeds is genuinely open and is one of the more interesting live questions in the field.

The one thing to carry forward

Recurrence shares parameters across time, and pays for it with a path length that grows with the sequence.

Everything in this part follows. The parameter saving of §3 and the generalisation to unseen lengths; the vanishing and exploding of §11; the trade of §12; gating as a way to control the decay rather than remove it; the bottleneck of §27; and the impossibility of parallel training.

So the question to take into Part XII is the one this part could not answer: can you share parameters across positions without making the path between them long?

The answer is yes, it costs something else entirely, and it reorganised the field.
