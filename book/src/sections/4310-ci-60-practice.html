Part FPractice

What to do differently on Monday. The design comes before the data, the assumptions go in writing, and a causal claim owes its reader a specific list of things, none of which is a p-value.

§40 The design comes before the data

The order that separates credible work from the rest

Write the question as an intervention: what would happen to $Y$ if we set $X$ for population $P$. Vague questions get vague estimands and unfalsifiable answers.

Draw the graph. Ten minutes, before you look at the data, and it is worth the discomfort because it forces the assumptions into view while they can still change the design.

Ask whether the quantity is identifiable (§14). If it is not, no analysis will fix it and the right move is to change the design or the question.

Pick the estimator and the checks then. Choosing after seeing results is Part XIV §20's biased maximum with a larger search space and no hold-out.

Pre-register, even privately

Write the estimand, the graph, the adjustment set and the checks into a file and commit it before the analysis. Nobody needs to see it. Its function is that you cannot afterwards convince yourself this was the plan, and that failure mode is nearly universal and nearly invisible.

This is the same instrument as Part XXI §42's "say what would change your mind, in advance," applied where the temptation is strongest.

§41 What a causal claim owes you

Six things, and their absence is informative

The estimand. Whose effect, of what, on what. Average over which population.

The identifying assumptions, named. Which backdoor set, or which design and its untestable claim. "We controlled for confounders" is not this.

The positivity check. The propensity distributions, plotted. One line of code, and Figure 3 is why.

The estimator, and what it assumes. Including which model has to be right, and whether weights were clipped or units trimmed.

Sensitivity. How strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be (§22).

The uncertainty, honestly. Including the model-selection step, if the specification was chosen after seeing data.

A paper missing the first two is not making a causal claim, whatever its verbs say. A paper missing the third has not checked the one assumption that is checkable.

§42 Reading a causal claim critically

In the order that finds problems fastest

Was anything randomised? If yes, most of this part is unnecessary: go to external validity and compliance. If no, continue.

What is the adjustment set, and why that one? Look for descendants of the treatment. "All available covariates" means colliders and mediators were included by default.

Could the sample have been selected on a collider? Ask how units entered. This is where Figure 2 lives, and it is rarely discussed.

Is there overlap? If the plot is absent, assume it was not looked at.

What would falsify it? If the design admits a placebo test, a pre-trend check or a negative control and none was run, ask why.

Does the estimand match the conclusion? An instrument gives compliers, a discontinuity gives the cutoff, matching gives the matched. Conclusions written about everyone frequently rest on estimates about somebody narrower.

§43 Where this changes the rest of the book

Seven places, specifically

Part VI's spurious correlation now has a mechanism: the shortcut is an effect of the target or a descendant of a confounder, and Figure 6 shows what that costs off-distribution.

Part XIII §7 and §17: selection bias and filtering are conditioning on colliders, which is why they distort in the direction they do.

Part XIV §21: an ablation is a $\mathrm{do}$ operation on a pipeline. The reason it isolates is that it severs incoming arrows, and the reason ablating several things at once does not decompose is that the graph has paths through both.

Part XVIII §40: offline reinforcement learning's conservatism is positivity: value outside the logged support is unidentified, not merely uncertain.

Part XX §26 and §31: a preference dataset is collected under a policy, and evaluating a different one from it is off-policy evaluation with the propensity usually unlogged.

Part XXI §9–§16: the interventions there are causal inference on a graph you wrote down: the easy case, and about the computation rather than the world (§36).

Part XXI §29: the observational fairness criteria have causal counterparts (§37), and the argument they are used to settle is usually about which paths are legitimate.

§44 What is unsolved

Four, distinguished by kind

Not yet measured. Whether invariance-based methods beat well-tuned empirical risk minimisation on realistic distribution shifts. The published comparisons disagree and the baselines are frequently undertuned: Part XIV §21's problem, in a young literature.

Not currently measurable. Whether an unmeasured confounder exists. There is no test, there will not be one, and §22's sensitivity analysis is the honest substitute rather than a solution.

Genuinely open. Causal representation learning: recovering variables that stand in causal relations from raw observations, rather than assuming them given. Identifiability results exist under strong assumptions and it is not settled what happens without them.

Not empirical at all. Which paths count as legitimate in a fairness argument, and whose causal model gets used. §37 makes the disagreement precise. It does not resolve it, and resolution is not a technical matter.

§45 What to carry forward

Five things

Prediction and intervention are different quantities. No accuracy on the first bounds the error on the second, and Figure 1's naive estimate is exactly right about the wrong question: reporting the opposite sign of a true effect it computed on forty thousand points.

Controlling for more is not safer. Confounders must be adjusted for, colliders must not be, mediators depend on the question, and nothing in the data distinguishes them. Figure 2 manufactures a correlation of −0.519 between independent variables by conditioning.

Identification precedes estimation, and can fail outright. For some graphs no estimator exists at any sample size. Recognising that early converts a modelling problem into a design problem, which is the only place it can be solved.

Designs relocate assumptions rather than removing them. Instruments, differences, discontinuities and synthetic controls each swap "the confounders are measured" for a different untestable claim, chosen because it is easier to defend. State which one you bought.

A model that uses causes is stable; one that uses effects is not. Figure 6's causal predictor is the worst on training-like data and the only one still working when the environment turns over. Random splits cannot see the difference: split by environment.

Sixty seconds

Write the question as an intervention. Draw the graph before the data. Check that the quantity is identifiable at all, and if it is not, change the design rather than the model. Adjust for confounders, never for descendants of the treatment. Plot the propensity overlap, because it is the one assumption you can check. Say which untestable claim your design bought, and how strong a confounder would have to be to overturn you. Then report the estimand you actually estimated, not the one you wanted.
