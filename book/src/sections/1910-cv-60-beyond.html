§27 Structured outputs, and the artefact you will recognise

Go and read

Odena, Dumoulin & Olah (2016), Deconvolution and checkerboard artifacts. A short interactive article that diagnoses an artefact everyone had seen and nobody had explained, and gives the arithmetic condition Figure 9 verifies. Then Ronneberger et al. (2015) on U-Net, for why a segmentation decoder needs skip connections from the encoder rather than merely benefiting from them.

Nothing about convolution requires the output to be a class label. A network can emit a tensor $S$ where $S_{i,j,k}$ is the probability that pixel $(j,k)$ belongs to class $i$, labelling every pixel, and tracing the outline of an object rather than naming it.

The difficulty is resolution. §15's Try it made the point: a classifier backbone downsamples aggressively, and a $16\times16$ output cannot localise anything to a few pixels. Three responses exist. Avoid pooling entirely, which is expensive. Emit a coarse label grid and accept it. Or downsample and then come back up, which needs an operation that increases resolution, and §16 already identified it.

Transposed convolution

§16 showed the backward pass of a convolution is multiplication by $M^\top$, which is itself a convolution. Used in the forward direction, that operation takes a small feature map to a large one, and it is called transposed convolution, or, misleadingly, deconvolution, which it is not: it does not invert anything.

An encoder–decoder for segmentation downsamples with strided convolutions and upsamples with transposed ones, usually with skip connections carrying high-resolution detail across from the encoder, because the fine spatial information was destroyed on the way down and cannot be recovered from the coarse representation alone.

Why this matters more than a tidy output

The checkerboard is not an aesthetic problem. It is a structured error the network must spend capacity correcting, and it appears in the gradient as well as the forward pass, so it biases learning in every layer below.

Three fixes, in ascending order of preference. Choose $k$ divisible by $s$, which is why $k=4$, $s=2$ is the conventional pairing and why PyTorch's transposed convolution requires you to state the kernel size rather than defaulting it. Upsample then convolve: nearest-neighbour or bilinear resize followed by an ordinary stride-1 convolution, which cannot produce the artefact at all because nothing overlaps unevenly. Or pixel shuffle, which produces the extra pixels in the channel dimension and then rearranges them, so no overlap occurs.

The second is now the common choice, and it is a small instance of a recurring pattern: the operation that is mathematically natural, being the transpose, turned out to have a defect that a less elegant construction avoids.

History: recurrent refinement and graphical-model post-processing

ch09 §9.6 covers two approaches to structured output that the encoder–decoder framing above superseded. They are worth naming because they appear in papers from the period.

Recurrent refinement. Pinheiro and Collobert (2014) applied a convolutional network to segmentation by running it iteratively: the output of one pass is fed back as an input to the next, allowing each pass to correct the previous one. This gets around the low-resolution bottleneck without a decoder; the network refines a coarse prediction in place. U-Net skip connections achieve a cleaner version of the same goal without the recurrence, which is why the recurrent approach is rarely used today.

Conditional random field post-processing. Early fully convolutional networks (Long et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2015, DeepLab) followed their predictions with a dense CRF, a graphical model that enforces spatial consistency by encouraging neighbouring pixels with similar appearance to share a label. It sharpened boundaries the convolutional decoder left blurry. As decoders became more powerful (larger kernels, dilated convolutions, better training) the CRF improvement shrank, and current architectures drop it. Part XXVIII covers the graphical-model machinery; the CRF itself was an application of that machinery to fix a limitation of early convolutional segmentation heads.

§28 Detection, in one section

Detection asks for a variable number of boxes with labels, which is a harder output type than either classification or segmentation because the number of answers is unknown.

The two families are worth knowing by their trade rather than their names. Two-stage detectors propose candidate regions and then classify each: accurate, slower. Single-stage detectors predict boxes and classes directly on a grid of positions: faster, and for a long while less accurate, until the gap was traced largely to the extreme imbalance between the few positions containing objects and the very many that do not, and closed by a loss that down-weights the easy negatives.

Why this book spends one section on it

Detection is a large literature and almost all of it is about the head: how boxes are parameterised, how predictions are matched to ground truth, how duplicates are suppressed, how the imbalance is handled. The convolutional backbone is shared with classification and is exactly what §§1–26 built.

That separation is the transferable lesson. Most applied computer vision is a backbone you did not design, pretrained on a task you do not care about (Part IX §26), with a head that encodes your actual output structure. The interesting engineering is nearly all in the head and the loss, and a part on convolution has little to say about it.

§29 Dilated convolution

§10 established the problem: the effective receptive field grows like $\sqrt{L}$, and downsampling to fix it destroys the resolution that §27's tasks need. Dilated convolution resolves the tension directly.

Dilation

Insert $d-1$ gaps between the kernel's taps. A $3\times3$ kernel with $d=2$ still has nine weights but spans $5\times5$; with $d=4$ it spans $9\times9$.

The receptive field becomes $1 + 2d$ per layer rather than $3$, at identical parameter count and identical compute. Stack layers with dilations $1, 2, 4, 8$ and the field grows exponentially with depth rather than linearly.

This is as close to free as anything in this part gets, and it is why dilated convolutions appear throughout segmentation architectures and in the audio models of §30, where the reach required is enormous and the resolution cannot be given up.

Pitfall: gridding

A stack of layers all using the same dilation $d$ has a defect. Each output depends only on inputs whose coordinates are congruent modulo $d$; the layers never mix the residue classes, so the network is effectively $d^2$ independent networks on interleaved sublattices, and the outputs show a grid pattern.

The fix is to vary the dilation between layers so that the strides are coprime, which is why published schedules look like $1, 2, 5, 9$ rather than $2, 2, 2, 2$. A schedule of powers of two is the natural first guess and it is the one that grids.

Why variable-size inputs come free, and when they do not

A dense layer has a fixed weight matrix and therefore a fixed input size. A convolution has a kernel that is simply applied a different number of times, so it accepts any size and produces a correspondingly sized output. Where the output must be fixed (one label per image), a pooling layer whose regions scale with the input restores it, which is what global average pooling does.

The condition is worth stating precisely, because it is easy to over-apply. Convolution handles inputs that vary in size because they contain varying amounts of the same kind of observation: longer recordings, wider images. It does not handle inputs that vary because they contain different kinds of observation. Convolving the same weights over an applicant's exam grades and their test scores is not a variable-length problem; it is a category error, and the grid topology that licenses the whole construction is absent.

§31 The neuroscience, and what the analogy buys

Go and read

Hubel & Wiesel (1962), Receptive fields, binocular interaction and functional architecture in the cat's visual cortex. The experiments this section is downstream of, and they read like nothing else on this list: two people, an electrode, and an animal being shown bars of light. Simple cells, complex cells and the detector-then-pool hierarchy are all here in 1962. Then Olshausen & Field (1996), which showed that a sparse coding objective on natural images recovers Gabor-like filters unsupervised; read §31's pitfall before drawing the obvious conclusion from it.

Convolutional networks are the field's clearest case of a design borrowed from biology, and the borrowing is specific enough to check.

History: recordings from a cat, and a Nobel prize

Through the 1950s and 60s Hubel and Wiesel recorded from individual neurons in cat visual cortex while projecting patterns onto a screen. Their finding, which won a Nobel prize, was that neurons in early visual cortex respond strongly to precisely oriented bars at particular locations, and hardly at all to anything else.

Three properties of area V1 were carried across into the architecture:

  • V1 is arranged as a two-dimensional map mirroring the retina, hence feature maps.
  • V1 contains simple cells, roughly linear in a small localised receptive field, hence the detector stage.
  • V1 contains complex cells, responding to the same features but invariant to small shifts, hence pooling.

Detection followed by pooling, repeated. The correspondence goes further than the cartoon: a convolutional network's late features resemble the inferotemporal cortex closely enough to predict its firing rates, and to match time-limited human performance on object recognition, where "time-limited" means the first 100ms, before top-down feedback arrives.

Two caveats from ch09 §9.10 belong alongside that history. The simple/complex split above is a convenient extreme rather than a clean boundary: Rust et al. (2005) found that real V1 neurons span a continuum of phase invariance between the two, not two separate populations. And further downstream, some neurons in inferotemporal and medial-temporal cortex are strikingly selective rather than distributed, the "Halle Berry neuron" that fired for photographs, drawings and even the printed name of the actor, and almost nothing else (Quiroga et al., 2005), a finding sometimes read as evidence for dedicated grandmother cells, in tension with the distributed population code the rest of this section assumes.

What are simple cells actually computing? You cannot read a biological neuron's weights, so you show it white noise and fit a linear model to its responses: reverse correlation. The answer is that most V1 cells are well described by a Gabor function: a sinusoid multiplied by a Gaussian envelope.

$$w(x,y) = \alpha \exp\!\big(-\beta_x x'^2 - \beta_y y'^2\big)\cos(f x' + \phi)$$

with $x', y'$ the coordinates rotated by $\tau$ and shifted to $(x_0, y_0)$. The Gaussian gates the response to a neighbourhood; the cosine makes it respond to a particular spatial frequency at a particular phase. So a simple cell reports a specific frequency of brightness change, in a specific direction, at a specific place.

Worked: the complex cell, and where invariance comes from

Take two simple cells identical except that one is a quarter cycle out of phase (a quadrature pair) and let the complex cell compute $c = \sqrt{s_0^2 + s_1^2}$.

Figure 10 measures what happens as a stimulus translates along the preferred direction. Each simple cell's response sweeps through its entire range, from maximum to minimum and back: measured at 199.8% of peak. The complex cell's response varies by 0.00%.

Negating the image (swapping black and white) flips each simple cell by 200% and leaves the complex cell unchanged to $10^{-14}$.

This is the detector-and-pool architecture, derived rather than asserted. Invariance is what you get when you take a quantity that oscillates with position and discard the phase, and the $L^2$ norm of a quadrature pair is the cleanest way to do it.

Pitfall: what the Gabor correspondence does and does not establish

An enormous variety of learning algorithms produce Gabor-like filters in their first layer when trained on natural images: sparse coding, ICA, and most deep networks. It is a striking correspondence and it is weaker evidence than it looks.

Because so many different methods find these filters, finding them tells you almost nothing about which method models the brain. They are a property of the statistics of natural images, not of any particular learner. It remains a bad sign if an algorithm fails to learn some kind of edge detector on natural images; it is not a good sign when one does.

This was checked while building this part. A network trained on the synthetic shapes used for Figure 8 learns clean oriented bar detectors; fitting Gabor functions to them gives a median $R^2$ of 0.18. The filters match the structure of that data, which is bars rather than natural edges. The correspondence is with the data, exactly as claimed.

The differences from biology are as instructive as the similarities, and one of them matters here more than the rest. The human eye is mostly low resolution. Only the fovea (an area about the size of a thumbnail at arm's length) sees sharply. The impression of a detailed scene is assembled by moving the eye several times a second to whatever is most relevant, and stitching. A convolutional network, by contrast, processes the entire image at full resolution, uniformly, with no notion of relevance.

ch09 noted in 2016 that incorporating such attention mechanisms was an active research direction, and that they had so far been most successful in natural language processing. Part XII is about what happened next.

§32 What convolution cannot do

Go and read

Dosovitskiy et al. (2021), An image is worth 16x16 words: transformers for image recognition at scale. The paper that reopened the question, and it is honest about the condition: with limited data the transformer loses, and it wins only once the dataset is very large. That is §11's prior being priced, stated by the authors themselves, and it is the setup for Part XII.

Four limits, each a place where the prior of §11 is the wrong prior.

It cannot relate distant things directly
Sparse interaction is the assumption that dependencies are local. Where they are not (the two ends of a shadow, a caption and the object it names, agreement between distant words), convolution must reach them by stacking, and §10 measured how slowly that reach actually grows. This is the limitation attention removes, at a cost that is quadratic rather than linear in the input size, and the trade is the subject of Part XII.
It knows only translation
§9 measured rotation and scale equivariance failing at 0.95 and 0.89 of the activation range. Every other symmetry your task has must be paid for out of data, augmentation, or a different architecture. Convolution is not a general theory of structured input; it is one symmetry, encoded exactly.
It assumes a grid
The whole construction rests on there being a fixed notion of adjacency, the same everywhere. Molecules, social networks, meshes and knowledge bases have neighbours without a grid, and generalising convolution to them is a real research area rather than a reparameterisation.
Its advantage narrows as data grows
§11's prior is worth most when data is scarce, because it substitutes an assumption for observations. Given enough observations, a model with a weaker prior can learn the same structure and is not bound by whatever the prior got wrong. That is the honest statement of what happened between convolutional networks and vision transformers, and it is Part VI §7's no-free-lunch theorem showing up as an empirical trend.
Where this actually stands in 2026

Neither of the two triumphalist accounts is right.

Attention did not make convolution obsolete. On limited data, convolutional networks remain better, for exactly the reason §11 gives, and vision transformers need either very large datasets or heavy augmentation and regularisation to close the gap, which is substituting a different prior for the one they discarded. Convolutions remain standard where latency or memory is binding, and in almost everything running on a device rather than in a data centre.

Nor did convolution survive unchanged. Modern architectures on both sides have converged: transformers acquired hierarchy, locality and downsampling from convolutional design, and convolutional networks acquired layer norm, GELU, inverted bottlenecks and the entire transformer training recipe, which §26 showed was most of the reported difference to begin with.

What survived intact is the part of this chapter that was derived rather than reported. The equivalence of convolution and a constrained matrix multiplication, the exactness of translation equivariance, the $\sqrt{L}$ receptive field, the $1/C_{\text{out}} + 1/k^2$ separable ratio, and the divisibility condition for checkerboards are all theorems. They will be true whatever wins the next round.

The one thing to carry forward

An architecture is a symmetry you have decided the data has, encoded so firmly that the model cannot consider anything else.

Convolution encodes exactly one (translation) and everything in this part is a consequence: the parameter saving, the data efficiency, the equivariance, the invariance bought by pooling, and every one of the four limits above. The question to carry into every later architecture is not "what does this layer compute?" but "what has it been told, and is that true here?"
