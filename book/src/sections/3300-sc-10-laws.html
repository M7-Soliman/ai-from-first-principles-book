Part AThe empirical laws

§1 The question scale asks

In 60 seconds

Make the model bigger, give it more data, spend more compute, and the loss falls, smoothly, over many orders of magnitude, following a power law you can fit and extrapolate. That regularity is the reason the last decade looked the way it did, and it is an empirical finding with no accepted derivation.

Three quantities, and one constraint

$N$, parameters. $D$, training tokens. $C$, total compute, and for a dense transformer $C \approx 6ND$: six FLOPs per parameter per token, two forward and four backward.

The constraint is that $C$ is what you have and $N$ and $D$ are what you choose. Movement B is entirely about that choice, and it is worth noticing that it only became a question once the laws made both sides predictable.

§2 Power laws, and how to read them

The form

$$L(x) = \frac{A}{x^{\alpha}} + E$$

Two pieces. The reducible term falls as a power of $x$: a straight line on log–log axes, with slope $-\alpha$. The irreducible term $E$ is a floor the model cannot go below however large it gets.

A power law is the statement that equal multiplicative increases buy equal decreases: each doubling of $N$ removes the same fraction of the reducible loss. That is a strong claim and it is the reason extrapolation is possible at all.

Pitfall: a straight line on log–log axes, without subtracting $E$

Plot $L$ itself against $N$ on log–log axes and you get a curve that flattens, because of the additive floor. Plot $L - E$ and you get a straight line, if $E$ is right.

Which means the exponent and the floor are fitted together, and they trade off: a slightly larger $E$ can be compensated by a steeper $\alpha$ over the range you measured. Two studies quoting different exponents may not disagree about anything except how they handled the floor.

This is why §4's protocol matters, and why any comparison of exponents has to state how the floor was handled. Figure 1 is the two pieces separated.

§3 What the irreducible term is

The entropy of the data

For a model trained by maximum likelihood on a stochastic source, $E$ is the entropy of the source, Part II §23's quantity. No model can predict better than the data's own randomness allows, and cross-entropy is bounded below by entropy.

So $E$ is a property of the data, not of the model or the method. Two architectures fitted on the same corpus should agree about $E$ and may disagree about $A$ and $\alpha$; if they disagree about $E$, at least one fit is wrong.

Why this is almost never checked

The entropy of a natural corpus is unknown and unknowable: estimating it is as hard as modelling it, since the best estimate available is the best model. So $E$ is a free parameter fitted from the same data as everything else, and there is no independent way to confirm it.

That is worth stating plainly because it is a real limitation of the whole enterprise, and it is invisible in the published curves. A synthetic source whose entropy is computable is the only setting where the fit can be graded at all, and the project's Stage 3 builds one.

§4 Fitting a law honestly

A protocol
  1. Tune the learning rate at every size. One rate across sizes penalises the largest models systematically, and §11 is the expensive version of that mistake.
  2. Match the schedule to the budget of each run. Also §11.
  3. Span at least two orders of magnitude in the variable being scaled. A power law fitted over one decade is an extrapolation dressed as a fit.
  4. Vary one thing. If $N$ and $D$ move together, the exponent you recover belongs to neither.
  5. Report the fitted $E$, and say whether it was free or pinned. Two exponents fitted with different floors are not comparable.
  6. Report the residuals. A power law that fits to 1% and one that fits to 15% support very different extrapolations, and only one of them is usually shown.

§5 The three laws, and their joint form

Separately and together

Holding everything else non-limiting, each of the three quantities has its own law: $L(N)$, $L(D)$ and $L(C)$, each a power law with its own exponent.

The joint form used for allocation is

$$L(N, D) = \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}} + E$$

which says the two deficits are additive and independent: too few parameters and too little data each cost you separately. That additivity is an assumption, it is not derived from anything, and it is what makes §13's optimisation tractable, so it is worth knowing that the whole compute-optimal apparatus rests on it.

§6 What the laws do not say

Five things routinely read into them

They are not about capability. The laws describe loss: average next-token cross-entropy. The relationship between loss and anything a user cares about is not a power law and is not smooth.

They are not architecture-independent. The exponents are similar across reasonable architectures, which is a striking empirical fact, and "similar" is not "identical". The constant $A$ varies a great deal, and it is the constant that decides the cost.

They are not data-independent. Change the corpus and you change $E$ and $A$ both. Curves fitted on one corpus do not transfer to another, and Part XIII §21 is why.

They do not extrapolate indefinitely. Every empirical law in the history of the subject eventually bent. These are fitted over a finite range and predict outside it by assumption.

They say nothing about what to do. A law is a description of a frontier, not a reason to move along it; §38 is about the cases where moving along it is the wrong project.

§7 Emergence, and the measurement artefact

Go and read

Schaeffer, Miranda & Koyejo (2023), Are emergent abilities of large language models a mirage?. A clean piece of methodological work: the same runs produce a sharp curve or a smooth one depending only on whether the metric is discontinuous. Read it as an instance of Part XIV §33 rather than as a claim about language models, because that is the transferable part.

The claim, and the deflation

The observation: some capabilities appear absent at small scale and present at large, with a sharp transition rather than a smooth improvement. Reported as emergence, and taken as evidence that scaling produces qualitative discontinuities.

The deflation is largely convincing. Many reported emergent curves are measured with discontinuous metrics: exact-match accuracy on a multi-step problem, which is zero until every step is right. Under a continuous metric on the same runs (per-token loss, or partial credit), the improvement is smooth and was happening all along.

The mechanism is arithmetic: if per-step accuracy $p$ improves smoothly and the metric is $p^k$ for a $k$-step task, the metric looks flat and then rises sharply while nothing discontinuous happened.

Why this belongs in a book about method

It is Part XIV §33's loss-is-not-the-metric, at the largest scale anyone works at, producing a research literature about a phenomenon that is substantially an artefact of the reporting choice.

The honest residue: some transitions do look sharp under continuous metrics too, and the question of whether anything genuinely discontinuous happens is open. The point is that the default explanation for a sharp curve should be the metric, and checking costs nothing.

§8 Extrapolation, and its limits

What the laws are actually used for

Their practical value is that a run costing millions can be predicted from runs costing thousands. Fit the surface on small models, extrapolate, and decide whether the large run is worth starting. This works well enough to have become standard practice.

The failure modes are the ones Part XIV §14 named: an extrapolation is a claim about a regime you have not measured. Here the specific hazards are that the reducible term may hit a floor you fitted wrongly, that the data may run out (§13), and that something in the training recipe that was fine at small scale (a learning rate, a schedule, a precision format) may not be.

The discipline: extrapolate no more than one order of magnitude beyond the fit without checking, and treat the first large run as a measurement of the extrapolation as much as a model.
