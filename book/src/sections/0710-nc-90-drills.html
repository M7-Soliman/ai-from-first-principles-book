PracticeDrills

Thirty-four problems. More of these are code than in earlier parts, because numerical behaviour is something you have to watch; the failures are invisible on paper and obvious in an experiment.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · What float32 can hold

float32 has a 24-bit significand. Write down the largest integer it represents exactly, and the spacing between representable numbers near 1.0.

Answer. 224 = 16,777,216 and 1.19×10−7. Past 16.8 million, consecutive integers stop being distinguishable, which is why a step counter, a running sum over a large dataset, or an ID stored as a float will silently stop incrementing. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

WarmW1 · Machine epsilon

Without looking it up: roughly how small must $x$ be for $1 + x$ to equal $1$ in float64?

Answer. About $10^{-16}$, since $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} = 2^{-52} \approx 2.22\times10^{-16}$. Anything smaller rounds away entirely.
WarmW2 · Where does 1 stop mattering

For roughly what $x$ does $(x+1) - x$ return $0$?

Answer. Around $10^{16}$. Past that the gap between neighbouring floats exceeds 1, so adding 1 cannot change the value. Figure 1, right panel.
WarmW3 · Digits lost

A problem has $\kappa = 10^{5}$. Roughly how many digits of accuracy do you expect to lose?

Answer. About five, leaving eleven of the original sixteen. The rule is one digit per order of magnitude of $\kappa$.
WarmW4 · Squaring the conditioning

$\kappa(A) = 10^{7}$. What is $\kappa(A^\top A)$, and what does that leave you?

Answer. $10^{14}$. From 16 digits, that leaves about two, and those two are not trustworthy. This is why the normal equations are a poor algorithm.
WarmW5 · Stability limit

A quadratic has Hessian eigenvalues $\{1, 50\}$. Largest stable learning rate?

Answer. $\alpha < 2/\lambda_{\max} = 2/50 = 0.04$. The largest curvature sets the cap, regardless of how flat the other direction is.
WarmW6 · Momentum amplification

What is the effective step multiplier along a consistent direction at $\beta = 0.95$?

Answer. $1/(1-\beta) = 20$.
WarmW7 · Saddle counting

Under the naive independence assumption, what is the chance a random critical point in 50 dimensions is a minimum?

Answer. $2^{-50} \approx 10^{-15}$. Effectively zero. Critical points in high dimensions are saddles.
WarmW8 · Complementary slackness

A KKT solution has $g(\mathbf{x}) = -3$. What must $\mu$ be, and why?

Answer. $\mu = 0$. The constraint is strictly satisfied, so it is inactive and exerts no force; $\mu g = 0$ forces the multiplier to vanish.
WarmW9 · Which is the problem

Your solve is inaccurate. $\kappa = 10^{14}$. Is this a conditioning problem or a stability problem?

Answer. Conditioning, a property of the problem. No algorithm can fix it. Reformulate, regularize, or accept the loss; do not go hunting for a better solver.
WarmW10 · Detecting nan

Without calling any library function, how do you test whether x is nan?

Answer. x != x. nan is the only value not equal to itself, by definition.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Measure machine epsilon

Find $\epsilon_{\text{mach}}$ by search: the smallest $x$ with $1 + x \neq 1$. Repeat in float32.

Answer. Halve until $1+x$ equals 1, then back off one step. float64 gives $2.22\times10^{-16}$; float32 gives $1.19\times10^{-7}$, which is only seven digits, worth remembering when using half or single precision in training.
CoreC2 · Stable quadratic roots

Implement both the textbook and the rearranged quadratic formula. Compare on $a=1$, $b=10^{8}$, $c=1$.

Answer. The true small root is about $-10^{-8}$. The textbook formula returns something with almost no correct digits, or exactly zero; the rearranged one is accurate. Figure 2, right panel.
CoreC3 · Two variance algorithms

Implement variance by $\mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$ and by the two-pass method. Test on [1e8, 1e8+1, 1e8+2].

Answer. The identity returns 0 or a negative number, which is impossible for a variance. The two-pass method returns $2/3$. Catastrophic cancellation between two large nearly equal quantities.
CoreC4 · Compensated summation

Sum $10^{6}$ copies of $0.1$ naively, then with a compensation term tracking the lost low-order bits. Compare to the exact answer.

Answer. The naive sum drifts measurably; compensated summation recovers most of the lost accuracy for one extra variable and one extra subtraction per term. np.sum uses pairwise summation, which is why it beats a Python loop.
CoreC5 · Stable log-sum-exp

Implement it and verify on $[1000, 1001, 1002]$ and $[-1000, -1001]$.

Answer. $\text{LSE}(z) = \max(z) + \log\sum e^{z-\max(z)}$. The first input overflows naively; the second underflows to $\log 0 = -\infty$. Both work after the shift.
CoreC6 · Measure the amplification

Generate matrices of known condition number via the SVD. Perturb $\mathbf{b}$ by a fixed relative amount, solve, and plot the resulting error against $\kappa$.

Answer. Build $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ with chosen singular values. The error tracks $\kappa \times$ the input perturbation; see Figure 3. This is the most convincing way to internalise what the condition number is.
CoreC7 · Least squares, three ways

Solve one problem by normal equations, QR, and SVD, sweeping $\kappa$ from $10$ to $10^{12}$. Plot the errors.

Answer. The normal equations degrade at twice the rate on log axes and fail by $\kappa \approx 10^{8}$. Figure 13. This is the single most useful experiment in the part.
CoreC8 · Find the stability limit

For $f = \frac12\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}$ with $A = \diag(1, 30)$, run descent at many learning rates and find empirically where it diverges. Compare to $2/\lambda_{\max}$.

Answer. Divergence begins just above $2/30 \approx 0.0667$, matching the theory closely. Worth doing because it converts a formula into an observed cliff.
CoreC9 · Convergence versus conditioning

Count iterations to reach a fixed accuracy, for $\kappa \in \{1, 10, 100, 1000\}$ at the best step size. Plot iterations against $\kappa$.

Answer. Roughly linear in $\kappa$, as $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ predicts. At $\kappa = 1$ you converge in one step.
CoreC10 · Newton's method

Implement it for a quadratic and confirm one-step convergence. Then run it on a saddle and describe what happens.

Answer. One step on any quadratic, from any start. On a saddle it converges to the saddle; the gradient is zero there and Newton seeks zero gradients, not minima. That is §12's third objection, observed.
CoreC11 · Backtracking line search

Implement it with the Armijo condition. Compare total function evaluations against a well-tuned fixed step.

Answer. Line search costs extra evaluations per iteration and removes the need to tune. On deterministic problems it usually wins overall; on noisy ones it chases noise, which is §13's pitfall.
CoreC12 · Momentum, measured

Implement momentum and compare against plain descent on a valley with $\kappa = 20$. Sweep $\beta$ over $\{0, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99\}$.

Answer. Improvement grows with $\beta$ up to a point, then overshooting appears: the effective step along the valley floor is $1/(1-\beta)$ times a single gradient, a hundredfold at $\beta=0.99$, and a velocity that large overshoots the minimum before it can damp out. That overshoot is real. What does not follow is that the deterministic divergence threshold has tightened: for this heavy-ball update it is $\alpha < 2(1+\beta)/\lambda_{\max}$ (§18), which is higher at $\beta=0.99$ than at $\beta=0.9$. Any divergence you measure at $\beta=0.99$ on a noisy problem is coming from amplified gradient noise and transient overshoot, not from a tighter stability limit.
CoreC13 · Implement Adam

Write Adam from the definition, including bias correction. Then delete the bias correction and compare the first fifty steps.

Answer. Without correction the early steps are far too small, because both accumulators start at zero and badly underestimate their targets. The correction factor $1/(1-\beta^t)$ is large early and approaches 1 later, which is exactly the compensation required.
CoreC14 · Batch size and noise

Measure the variance of the minibatch gradient estimate as a function of batch size. Confirm the $1/\sqrt{b}$ law.

Answer. Sample many minibatch gradients at a fixed parameter value and take the standard deviation across them. It falls as $1/\sqrt{b}$, so a hundredfold increase in cost buys a tenfold reduction in noise, which is §14's argument.
CoreC15 · Schedules against a noise floor

Run SGD with constant, step and cosine schedules on a noisy objective. Plot loss against iteration.

Answer. The constant rate plateaus at a floor set by noise times step size; decaying schedules keep descending. Figure 10. This is Robbins and Monro's condition, observed empirically.
CoreC16 · Find the bug

This is meant to compute a softmax cross-entropy. It sometimes returns nan and sometimes returns $0$ when the model is confidently wrong. Find both faults.

p = np.exp(logits) / np.exp(logits).sum()
loss = -np.log(p[label])
Answer. (1) No max subtraction, so exp overflows for large logits and gives inf/inf = nan. (2) When $p$ underflows to exactly 0, log(0) is $-\infty$; combined with a zero weight elsewhere this can surface as $0$ or nan depending on the arithmetic. Compute the log-softmax directly: z - z.max() then z - logsumexp(z).
CoreC17 · Lagrange by hand

Minimise $x^2 + y^2$ subject to $x + y = 4$, using multipliers.

Answer. $\mathcal{L} = x^2+y^2+\lambda(x+y-4)$. The partials give $2x + \lambda = 0$ and $2y + \lambda = 0$, so $x = y$; the constraint gives $x = y = 2$, and $\lambda = -4$. The closest point on the line to the origin, which is what the geometry predicts.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · Regularization as conditioning

Add $\lambda I$ to an ill-conditioned $A^\top A$ and plot $\kappa$ against $\lambda$. Relate to ridge regression.

Answer. Adding $\lambda$ lifts every eigenvalue, so $\kappa \to (\sigma_{\max}^2+\lambda)/(\sigma_{\min}^2+\lambda)$, which falls sharply as $\lambda$ grows. Ridge regression is simultaneously a statistical device against overfitting and a numerical device against ill-conditioning. Same knob.
StretchS2 · Escaping a saddle

Start exact gradient descent precisely on a saddle. Then add small gradient noise. Compare.

Answer. Exact descent stops permanently; the gradient is zero. Noise knocks the iterate off and it escapes along the negative-curvature direction. This is the mechanism by which SGD's noise is an asset rather than a tolerated cost.
StretchS3 · Adam's counterexample

Construct a simple convex problem where Adam fails to converge but SGD succeeds.

Answer. The standard construction uses a sequence in which a large informative gradient appears rarely among many small opposing ones. Adam's exponential averaging forgets the rare large gradient faster than it arrives, so its influence is permanently discounted. This is the failure the 2018 paper exhibited, and that is also why it did not change practice much, since the construction is adversarial rather than typical.
StretchS4 · Quasi-Newton

Implement BFGS for a small problem. Compare its iteration count against gradient descent and true Newton.

Answer. Typically far closer to Newton than to gradient descent, at gradient-only cost, by accumulating curvature information from successive gradient differences. It assumes those gradients are exact, which is why it is rare in deep learning.
StretchS5 · Gradient checking at scale

Extend Part 0's gradient check to a small multi-layer network. Then deliberately break one layer's backward pass and confirm the check localises it.

Answer. Check layer by layer rather than globally; a single aggregate number tells you something is wrong but not where. This is the practical form of the technique and the one you will actually use.
StretchS6 · Float32 versus float64

Rerun several of these experiments in float32. Where does the behaviour change?

Answer. With about seven digits instead of sixteen, everything degrades roughly nine orders of magnitude earlier. A condition number of $10^{4}$ is now serious. Since most training runs in float32 or lower, this is the regime that actually applies, and it is why mixed-precision training keeps a float32 copy of the weights while computing in half precision.
