BuildThe project

Eight to ten hours. You establish, by measurement rather than argument, that six techniques taught as unrelated are the same technique.

P.1 What you are building

oneknob: a harness that fits one problem under every regulariser in this part, and reports where they agree and where they do not.

The rule: no claim without a number. Every equivalence this part asserts must be produced by your code, with the disagreement reported, not assumed.

P.2 Stage 1: the instrument

A quadratic problem you can control: choose $n$, $d$, and an eigenvalue spectrum spanning several decades, then generate data from a known $\mathbf{w}^*$.

  • Ridge by solve, never an explicit inverse (Part I §13).
  • The eigendecomposition of $X^\top X$, and a function returning the kept fraction of each direction for any fitted $\mathbf{w}$.
  • A held-out test set, and Part V §10's bootstrap interval on the reported error.

Deliverable: reproduce Figure 1, with measured kept fractions landing on $\lambda/(\lambda+\alpha)$, and a test-error curve whose optimum is strictly interior. Report how much the best $\alpha$ beats $\alpha \to 0$ on your spectrum.

P.3 Stage 2: four routes to one solution

The centrepiece. Fix a target $\alpha$ and reach it four ways:

  1. Ridge at $\alpha$.
  2. Input noise at $\sigma = \sqrt{\alpha/n}$, averaged over many corrupted copies.
  3. Early stopping at $\tau = 1/(\alpha\epsilon)$.
  4. A norm constraint at $c = \|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_\alpha\|_2$, by projected gradient.

Deliverable: a 4×4 table of pairwise relative distances. Three of the four should agree to about a percent. Early stopping will not, and the size and location of its disagreement is the interesting result; report where in the spectrum it drifts, and check it against §14's claim that the gap is largest for half-learned directions.

P.4 Stage 3: the ones that are not equivalent

Now the techniques that genuinely differ, on a problem with sparse true weights:

  • $L^1$ by proximal gradient. Count exact zeros, not small values.
  • Bagging. Measure bias² and variance separately against $k$; confirm the first is flat and the second falls like $1/k$.
  • Dropout, on a small network you write yourself. Sweep $p$, average several seeds, and locate the U.

Deliverable: for each, one sentence saying what preference it expresses and one measurement showing that preference being expressed.

P.5 Stage 4: the accumulation trap

The practical result, and the one most likely to change how you work.

Apply input noise and weight decay together at several combinations. Predict the total from $\alpha_{\text{noise}} + \alpha_{\text{explicit}}$, then measure it.

Then run the failure deliberately: fix $\sigma$ so that $\alpha_{\text{noise}} = 10$, and sweep explicit $\alpha$ over $[0.01, 1]$. Plot test error against $\alpha$.

Deliverable: a flat line, and a paragraph explaining why someone looking at it would reasonably conclude that weight decay does not help on this problem, and be wrong.

P.6 Stage 5: adversarial

  1. Fit a classifier and confirm high clean accuracy.
  2. Attack it along the sign of the input gradient; sweep $\epsilon$.
  3. Attack it with random perturbations of identical magnitude.
  4. Retrain including adversarial examples and repeat.

Deliverable: the two curves on one axis, and three numbers for the adversarially trained model: robust accuracy, clean accuracy, and wall-clock cost. Reporting only the first is how this technique gets overstated.

P.7 Stretch

  • Double descent, with and without regularization. Sweep width across the interpolation threshold at several $\alpha$. Watch the peak flatten. Part V §20 noted the default settings of most solvers hide it; make that concrete.
  • Label smoothing. Measure the largest logit with and without. Part II §25's loss curve predicts what you will see.
  • Noise on the weights rather than the inputs, and the penalty it corresponds to.
  • An augmentation that is false (vertical flips on digits), and the damage measured against none.

P.8 Done when

  • Measured kept fractions land on $\lambda/(\lambda+\alpha)$.
  • The 4×4 table exists, with early stopping's disagreement located rather than excused.
  • $L^1$ produces exact zeros; bagging's variance falls like $1/k$ with flat bias.
  • The dropout U is located, over several seeds.
  • The flat-line plot exists, with its explanation.
  • Adversarial training reported with all three numbers.
  • Every reported error carries a bootstrap interval.
  • Your README names one technique you had thought was unrelated to the others.
Why this project

Regularization is the part of the field where the largest number of things are taught as separate tricks and the largest number turn out to be one thing. Reading that claim is easy and unconvincing; producing four solutions that coincide to a percent is neither.

It also builds the habit this part most wants to leave you with. The accumulation trap in Stage 4 is not a curiosity; it is the most common regularization failure in practice, it looks exactly like a technique not working, and the only defence is knowing that strengths add and being willing to measure the total.

  • Give the five views of §2 and say which question each answers best.

  • Explain why $L^1$ reaches exactly zero and $L^2$ does not, twice: by gradient and by geometry.
  • State the correspondence between a penalty and a constraint, and what $\alpha$ means.
  • Explain why regularization gives an under-constrained problem a unique solution.
  • Derive that input noise equals an $L^2$ penalty, and say what the derivation assumed.
  • Give the $\alpha$ corresponding to noise of scale $\sigma$ on $n$ examples.
  • State the early-stopping kept fraction and the $\alpha$ it corresponds to.
  • Say precisely in what sense early stopping and weight decay are equivalent, and in what sense they are not.
  • Explain why parameter sharing is stronger than a tying penalty.

The one thing to carry forward

A regulariser is a statement about which functions you would rather have, made before you know which ones fit.

That is why techniques with nothing in common formally turn out to be identical in effect, why none of them is universally good, and why the useful question is never how much regularization but which preference is true here.

It is also why the honest parts of this chapter are the derived ones. The folklore around dropout and normalisation has shifted more than once and will shift again; the eigen-shrinkage, the noise identity and the variance algebra are theorems, and they will outlast it.
