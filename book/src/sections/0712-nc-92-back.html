BuildThe project

Six to ten hours. You build an optimiser library from nothing, then use it as an instrument to reproduce every claim in this part.

P.1 What you are building

tinyopt: a numerical optimisation toolkit, then a test bench that races the methods against each other.

The rule: NumPy for array arithmetic only. No scipy.optimize, no autograd, no framework optimisers. If a library provides it, that is the thing you are writing.

P.3 Stage 2: conditioning as an instrument

  • make_matrix(m, n, kappa): build a matrix with a chosen condition number by constructing its SVD directly. This is the workhorse for everything below.
  • solve_normal(A, b), solve_qr(A, b), solve_svd(A, b): three least squares solvers. Implement QR by Gram–Schmidt or Householder yourself.
  • digits_lost(A): predict accuracy loss from $\kappa$, then check the prediction.

Deliverable: reproduce Figure 13 with your own code. Then answer in a paragraph: at what $\kappa$ does each method lose half its digits, and does the normal-equations curve have twice the slope on log axes? It should.

P.4 Stage 3: optimisers

Implement each with the same interface, returning the full parameter path:

  • gd: plain gradient descent
  • gd_momentum
  • newton: using an exact Hessian you supply
  • adagrad, rmsprop, adam: with bias correction
  • backtracking_line_search: Armijo condition

Deliverable: a single figure with all methods on a quadratic of $\kappa = 50$, objective against iteration on log axes. Then the same figure with gradient noise added. The ranking should change, and explaining why it changes is the point of the exercise.

P.5 Stage 4: reproduce the part

Use your toolkit to verify each claim. One figure and one caption for each.

  1. Stability limit. Sweep the learning rate and locate the divergence cliff. Compare against $2/\lambda_{\max}$.
  2. Conditioning controls convergence. Iterations to a fixed accuracy against $\kappa$.
  3. Newton is conditioning-independent. One step on any quadratic, at any $\kappa$.
  4. Newton finds saddles. Start it near one and watch it converge to the wrong kind of point.
  5. Batch size and noise. Confirm the $1/\sqrt{b}$ law by direct measurement.
  6. Schedules beat constants under noise. The noise floor, and its removal.
  7. Noise escapes saddles. Exact descent stalls; noisy descent does not.

P.6 Stretch

  • BFGS: build the inverse-Hessian approximation from gradient differences.
  • Trust region: solve with $H + \mu I$ and adapt $\mu$ by how well the quadratic model predicted the actual decrease.
  • Float32 sweep: rerun Stage 2 in single precision and find where each curve moves.
  • A real training run: logistic regression on real data, all six optimisers, same budget. Report both training loss and held-out accuracy, and notice that they do not rank the methods identically.

P.7 Done when

  • Every stability function has a documented input where the naive version fails.
  • Your three solvers reproduce the shape of Figure 13.
  • Your measured divergence threshold matches $2/\lambda_{\max}$.
  • Newton converges in one step on a quadratic, and to a saddle when started near one.
  • Adam without bias correction is visibly slower for the first fifty steps.
  • Your README names one result you expected to go the other way.
Why this project

Numerical failures are invisible until you build the instrument that makes them visible. Every claim in this part is falsifiable in about thirty lines of code, and checking them yourself is the difference between knowing that ill-conditioning is bad and knowing what it looks like when it happens to you.

The optimisers matter for a second reason: you will spend years using them. Having written each one from its update rule means that when a training run misbehaves you will reason about what the optimiser is actually doing rather than swapping names until something works.

Part V builds the theory of learning on top of this. Be honest.

  • Explain why float spacing grows with magnitude.
  • State machine epsilon and what it means operationally.
  • Explain catastrophic cancellation and why it produces no warning.
  • Give the stable form of the quadratic formula and say what it avoids.
  • Define the condition number and convert it to digits lost.
  • Distinguish conditioning from stability, and say which one a better algorithm fixes.

  • Say what the Hessian's eigenvalues and eigenvectors mean geometrically.
  • Derive the Newton update from the quadratic approximation.
  • Give three reasons Newton's method is unusable for large models.
  • Explain why the minibatch gradient is unbiased, and what its noise costs and buys.
  • Explain what momentum cancels and what it accumulates.

  • Explain why a constant learning rate cannot converge under noise.
  • Explain why saddles dominate minima in high dimensions.
  • State the four KKT conditions and interpret complementary slackness.
  • Say why the normal equations are the wrong way to fit least squares.

The one thing to carry forward

If you retain a single idea from this part, make it this: algebraic equivalence does not imply numerical equivalence.

Two expressions that a proof says are identical can behave completely differently when computed. The formula that is cleanest to derive is frequently the worst one to evaluate. Every time you turn a derivation into code, that gap is the question worth asking; §20 is what it looks like when the answer matters.
