Part DStopping, and sharing

§13 Early stopping

In 60 seconds

Watch validation error while you train, keep the parameters from the best point, and stop when it has not improved for a while. It costs one extra evaluation per epoch and no change to the objective, it needs no hyperparameter chosen in advance, and it is probably the most-used regulariser in the field.

The procedure is trivial and the reason it works is not.

Early stopping
  1. Evaluate on a validation set every epoch.
  2. Whenever it improves, save the parameters.
  3. Stop after $p$ epochs with no improvement (the patience).
  4. Return the saved parameters, not the final ones.

Step 2 is the one people omit, and without it the procedure does nothing: you stop at a worse point than you passed through.

Why it is unusual among regularisers

Every method in Part B requires choosing a strength before training and re-training to change it. Early stopping chooses its own strength, by measurement, during the one run you were doing anyway.

That is close to free. The costs are honest but small: a validation set held out of training, one evaluation per epoch, and the memory to keep a copy of the best parameters. Set against re-training a model five times to sweep $\alpha$, it is a bargain, and it is why it is nearly universal.

It also composes with everything else here, which not all of these do.

Reclaiming the split

Early stopping was bought with a real cost: a slice of the data spent watching rather than training. Once step 3 has fired, call the number of updates at the saved point $i^*$; there is no reason to leave that watching slice idle in the model you actually ship, and there are two ways to get it back.

Two ways to retrain on everything

Retrain from scratch. Reinitialise, pool the training and validation sets, and train for $i^*$ updates, not epochs. The pooled set has a different number of examples per epoch than the run that produced $i^*$, and it was the count of gradient steps, not passes over the data, that decided how far §14's shrinkage had travelled.

Continue from where you stopped. Keep the parameters from the original run, fold the validation examples into the training set, and keep training, with nothing held out to watch any more, until the loss on those now-unheld-out examples falls to the value the training objective had already reached at $i^*$. That threshold is a number you measured during the first run; the second run is training until it catches up to it on data the model has not yet been fitted to.

Pitfall: the second strategy is not guaranteed to stop

The first strategy always terminates: $i^*$ steps is $i^*$ steps, known in advance. The second stops when a measured quantity crosses a threshold, and nothing guarantees it ever will. Pooling in the validation data changes the loss landscape slightly, and the validation loss on it can in principle approach the target asymptotically without crossing it; a real implementation needs its own patience-style escape hatch, on top of the one from §13's procedure.

Neither option is free: both spend the compute of a full second run, and the first also discards the parameters the original run produced, having used that run only to measure $i^*$. Whether the reclaimed data is worth a second full training pass is a judgement call: strongest when the validation slice was a real fraction of what little data you had, weak when data was never the bottleneck.

§14 Early stopping is weight decay

Now the result that makes this part cohere. Early stopping looks procedural, a stopping rule, nothing to do with the objective. It turns out to traverse the same family of solutions as $L^2$.

The argument is short if you have §4. On a quadratic loss, gradient descent from $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$ with rate $\epsilon$ moves in the eigenbasis independently per direction, and after $\tau$ steps has covered a fraction

$$1 - (1 - \epsilon\lambda_i)^{\tau}$$

of the distance to the unregularized optimum in direction $i$. Compare that against §4's ridge result, $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i + \alpha)$. Both are functions that rise from 0 to 1 as curvature grows; both delete flat directions and keep sharp ones; and setting

$$\alpha \approx \frac{1}{\tau\epsilon}$$

lines the two families up. Training for fewer steps is applying a stronger penalty.

Pitfall: "equivalent" is doing work in that sentence

This result is often stated as an equivalence and it is worth being precise, because the figure above is the measurement rather than the slogan.

What is true: both methods traverse a one-parameter family of solutions in the same order, shrinking low-curvature directions first, and they agree closely at both ends: a barely-trained model and a heavily-penalised one really are near-identical, and so are a fully-trained model and an unpenalised one.

What is not true: that they produce the same weights at matched settings. In the middle of the range they differ by up to 23% of a direction's magnitude, and the derivation assumed a quadratic loss, which a neural network's is not.

The useful reading is directional rather than numerical: if you are already using early stopping, you are already regularizing, and adding weight decay on top is adding to a total you had not counted, as in §12's Try it.

Intuition: why the order is the same

Both methods delete flat directions first, for the same underlying reason, and it is worth seeing that it is one reason rather than two coincidences.

Gradient descent moves fastest where the gradient is largest, which is where curvature is highest. A sharp direction is traversed in a handful of steps; a flat one takes thousands. Stop early and the flat ones are still near zero, not because you penalised them, but because you did not wait.

Ridge compares each direction's curvature against $\alpha$ and erases the ones that lose.

Same ordering, arrived at by opposite routes: one by patience, one by decree. And the quantity doing the ordering in both is the curvature, which is Part I's eigenvalues turning up for the fourth time.

§15 Parameter tying and sharing

A different kind of preference: rather than pulling weights toward zero, pull them toward each other.

Tying adds a penalty $\|\mathbf{w}^{(A)} - \mathbf{w}^{(B)}\|_2^2$ between two models, expressing a belief that two related tasks should have similar solutions. Sharing goes further and makes them the same parameters, not similar but identical.

Why sharing is the most important regulariser in this book

Sharing is a stronger statement than any penalty, and its effect on capacity is not incremental.

A penalty says "these weights should be small". Sharing says "these weights are the same weight", which removes parameters outright rather than constraining them. A convolutional layer applying one $3\times3$ filter across a million pixel positions has nine parameters where a fully connected layer would have millions.

And the preference it encodes is a real fact about images: an edge detector that works in the top-left corner works in the bottom-right. That is translation invariance, and it is true of photographs in a way that no generic smallness preference could capture. Part VI §7's theorem said the winning method is the one whose assumptions match the problem; convolution is the clearest instance in the field.

The same move gives recurrence its parameter savings (one transition matrix applied at every time step, expressing that the rules of a sequence do not change with position) and Part X and Part XI are, from this angle, two long studies of what to share.

Worth noticing: sharing is regularization by §1's definition. It raises training error, since a shared-weight model cannot fit everything an unshared one can, and it is not usually taught as regularization at all. It is taught as architecture. That is the same thing seen from a different chair, and Part VI §20's recipe said so: the model family is where your assumptions live.

§16 Multi-task learning

Sharing weights across layers was §15. Sharing them across tasks is the same mechanism pointed in a different direction, and it is one of the few regularisers that gets stronger the more you ask of it.

The arrangement: several tasks, one model, with the early layers shared and a small task-specific head on top of each. Every task's gradient updates the shared trunk.

Intuition: a representation that has to serve several masters

A trunk trained on one task may latch onto anything that predicts that task, including accidents of the dataset. A trunk that must simultaneously support four tasks cannot afford to: a feature that helps one and is useless for the others earns less of the representation's capacity than one that helps all four.

So the shared layers are pushed toward factors that genuinely underlie the data rather than toward whatever correlates with one label. That is a preference in §1's sense, and it is a strong one, because it comes from the other tasks rather than from a generic taste for small weights.

It is also the statistical argument in disguise. Each task contributes its own examples to the shared parameters, so the trunk is effectively fitted on the union of all the datasets, meaning more data for the shared part, which by §9 is the trade nothing else offers.

Pitfall: the assumption can be false, and then it costs you

Multi-task learning assumes the tasks share underlying structure. When they do not, the shared trunk is pulled in conflicting directions and every task ends up worse than it would have been alone, an effect with the unlovely name negative transfer.

The symptom is that the multi-task model underperforms single-task baselines on every task at once, which is diagnostic: a genuine capacity problem would not be so uniform. The check is the one this part keeps returning to: train the single-task baselines, properly tuned, and compare. Part VI §11 insisted on that for a reason.

§17 Semi-supervised learning

Labels are expensive and unlabelled data is usually abundant. Semi-supervised learning uses both, and the reason it counts as regularization is worth being precise about.

Unlabelled data carries no information about $p(y \mid \mathbf{x})$; that is what "unlabelled" means. What it carries is information about $p(\mathbf{x})$: where the data lives, how it clusters, what its manifold looks like.

Why that helps at all

It helps only under an assumption, and the assumption is worth stating because it is the whole method.

The structure of $p(\mathbf{x})$ must be informative about $p(y \mid \mathbf{x})$. Concretely: that points in the same cluster tend to share a label, or that the decision boundary lies in a low-density region rather than through the middle of a cluster.

If that is true, unlabelled data constrains the classifier: it tells you where the boundary may not go, and it does so using data that cost nothing. If it is false, the unlabelled data is noise dressed as a prior, and it will hurt.

Part VI §2 already made half of this argument: the chain rule factors $p(\mathbf{x}, y) = p(y \mid \mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})$, so learning about one factor is learning about the joint. Semi-supervised learning is that identity used deliberately.

The modern form is self-supervised pretraining, and it is the same idea at scale: invent a task from unlabelled data (predict a masked word, predict a rotated patch), then train a representation on it, then fine-tune on the small labelled set you actually have. Part VI §2 noted that a language model's whole training signal is manufactured this way.

Which makes semi-supervised learning the regulariser with the largest practical footprint in the modern field, and the one that looks least like a regulariser. It fits §1's definition exactly: it does not reduce training error on the labelled task, and it reduces generalisation error, by constraining the representation with information from somewhere else.

Try it

You train with early stopping at epoch 40 out of a planned 400, learning rate $10^{-2}$. A colleague suggests adding weight decay. Estimate the penalty you are already applying, and say what you would do.

Answer. By §14, $\alpha \approx 1/(\tau\epsilon)$. With $\tau = 40$ epochs and $\epsilon = 10^{-2}$, using epochs as the step unit, which is rough but the right order, that is $\alpha \approx 1/(40 \times 0.01) = 2.5$.

That is not a small penalty. Stopping at 40 of 400 epochs means the run was terminated after a tenth of its intended course, which is a strong statement about how little of the flat-direction structure you wanted.

What to do: not "add weight decay and see", because you would be adding to an unmeasured 2.5 and any small $\alpha$ you tried would be invisible. Either sweep weight decay with early stopping disabled, so you are measuring one knob, or accept that early stopping is already the regulariser and tune the patience instead. The failure mode this avoids is the common one: two regularisers, each looking ineffective, because each is being tested in the shadow of the other.
