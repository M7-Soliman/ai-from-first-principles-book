Part CUncertainty for networks

A network has millions of parameters and no closed-form posterior. Four approximations are in common use, they cost between nothing and a great deal, and their intervals contain the truth at rates worth measuring rather than assuming. This movement measures them.

§16 What a prior over weights means

The Bayesian neural network

Put a prior on the weights, condition on the data, predict by integrating:

$$p(y_* \mid x_*, \mathcal{D}) = \int p(y_* \mid x_*, \theta)\, p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})\, \mathrm{d}\theta$$

Every difficulty is in that integral. The posterior is over millions of dimensions, is multimodal by construction (Part IX §7's weight-space symmetries mean every mode has astronomically many copies) and has no analytic form.

The awkward question: what does a weight prior say?

An isotropic Gaussian on the weights is the default and nobody chose it because it expresses a belief about functions. It was chosen because it is convenient and corresponds to weight decay.

What it implies about functions depends on the architecture in ways that are hard to state, and different architectures with the same weight prior imply very different function priors. This is the practical reason movement B's approach (specify the prior over functions directly) is more interpretable, and the reason it does not scale is the reason nobody does it.

§17 Variational inference

Replacing integration with optimisation

Choose a tractable family $q_\phi(\theta)$, usually independent Gaussians per weight, and maximise the evidence lower bound:

$$\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\big[\log p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\big] - \mathrm{KL}\!\left(q_\phi \,\|\, p(\theta)\right)$$

Part XVI §15's ELBO, applied to weights rather than latents, with the same reparameterisation trick making it differentiable. The cost is roughly double the parameters and a noisier gradient.

The known weakness is inherited from Part XVI §17: a mean-field $q$ underestimates the posterior variance when the true posterior is correlated, and the posterior over a network's weights is enormously correlated.

§18 The Laplace approximation

A Gaussian at the mode

Train normally to get $\theta_{\text{MAP}}$, then fit a Gaussian there with covariance the inverse Hessian of the negative log-posterior: the loss's curvature plus the prior's, which is the loss alone only when the prior is flat. Curvature becomes uncertainty: flat directions are uncertain, sharp ones are not.

Its attraction is that it is post hoc: it costs one Hessian approximation on an already-trained model and changes nothing about training. Its limitation is that the Hessian is intractable in full, so a diagonal or Kronecker-factored approximation is used, and the quality of the intervals depends on which.

Part III §10's curvature and Part IX §19's Newton method are the same object read for a different purpose: there it told you how to step, here it tells you what you do not know.

§19 Dropout and ensembles

Go and read

Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel & Blundell (2017), Simple and scalable predictive uncertainty estimation using deep ensembles. The paper that established the recipe below: independently-initialised networks trained on a proper scoring rule, with no Bayesian derivation offered or claimed. Read it for the honesty of that framing: the argument is carried entirely by measurement against variational and MC-dropout baselines, not by theory, which is exactly the empirical-not-explained status §21 gives the method.

The two that are actually used

MC dropout. Leave dropout on at prediction time and average several forward passes. Costs nothing to implement if the model already uses dropout, which is the entire reason for its popularity. The Bayesian interpretation is contested; treat it as a cheap perturbation ensemble.

Deep ensembles. Train $K$ networks from different initialisations and average. Costs $K$ times the training and consistently performs best on calibration and out-of-distribution benchmarks. Not Bayesian in derivation: different initialisations find genuinely different modes, which is closer to sampling the posterior's structure than any single-mode method.

§20 What each one's intervals contain

Two failures, and only one of them is dangerous

Over-covering (dropout, 0.993) wastes information. Intervals wider than they need to be make the system look less useful than it is, and every decision built on them is conservative. Recoverable, and detectable by exactly this measurement.

Under-covering (point estimate, 0.790) is the dangerous one. One prediction in five falls outside an interval promising one in twenty, and every downstream decision is taken with more confidence than the evidence supports. Part XXI §32's safety case built on such an interval is wrong in the direction that matters.

Note that restricted to the densely observed region the point estimate would have scored close to nominal. It fails where the query moves away from the data, which is where an uncertainty estimate is load-bearing and where the benchmark rarely looks.

§21 Why ensembles do well

Mode diversity beats within-mode precision

Variational inference and Laplace both characterise a single mode carefully. An ensemble characterises several modes crudely. Empirically the second wins on calibration and on out-of-distribution detection, which says something about where the posterior's mass actually is.

The implication is uncomfortable for the theory and useful in practice: the thing to buy with a fixed budget is diversity of solution, not fidelity to one. Part VIII §18's bagging said the same about accuracy; this says it about uncertainty.

Pitfall: an ensemble of near-identical models

If the members share an initialisation scheme, an architecture, an augmentation policy and a data ordering, they may find the same mode and their spread will understate the uncertainty while looking like a proper ensemble.

Check it: measure the disagreement between members on inputs where they should disagree: out-of-distribution ones. Near-zero disagreement there means the ensemble is decorative, and this is drill C4.

§22 The cost table, honestly

What each buys, and what it costs

Point estimate: free. No epistemic term at all. Figure 3: 0.790.

MC dropout: a few extra forward passes. Over-covers here; quality depends on a dropout rate that was chosen for regularization rather than for uncertainty.

Laplace: one post-hoc Hessian approximation, no retraining. Reasonable, and limited by which Hessian approximation you can afford.

Bootstrap ensemble: $K\times$ refits and $K\times$ inference; Figure 3 measures this, resampling one dataset rather than retraining from different initialisations. Best measured quality here: 0.967. §19's genuine deep ensemble costs the same order.

Conformal (movement D): one held-out calibration set, no retraining, and a guarantee none of the above has.

§23 What movement C establishes

Three things

The intervals differ, and the differences are measurable. Figure 3 puts four methods between 0.790 and 0.993 against a nominal 0.95, on a problem where the right answer is known.

Under-covering and over-covering are not symmetric. Too wide is wasteful; too narrow is a decision taken on evidence that does not support it.

Diversity beats precision at fixed cost. Ensembles characterise several modes crudely and outperform methods that characterise one carefully, which is an empirical finding the theory does not yet explain.
