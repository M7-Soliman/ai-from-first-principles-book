Part COptimisation and curvature

§8 The optimisation problem

In 60 seconds

Nearly everything in this book is an instance of one problem: find the input that minimises a function. Training is that. Fitting is that. Inference frequently is too. The rest of this part is about how it is actually done, and about the two things that make it hard: curvature and noise.

The problem, stated once:

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*} = \argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \; f(\boldsymbol{\theta})$$

Here $f$ is the objective, and when it measures how badly a model is doing we call it the loss or cost. Maximising is the same problem with a sign flip, so nothing is lost by only ever discussing minimisation.

Part 0 §19 gave the condition for a minimum in one variable: the derivative vanishes, and the second derivative is positive. The multivariable version:

Conditions for a local minimum

First order (necessary): $\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}$, meaning every partial derivative is zero.

Second order (sufficient): the Hessian $H$ is positive definite, meaning the surface curves upward in every direction.

The word "every" in that second condition is doing far more work than it appears to, and §18 is about what it costs.

§9 Gradient descent: step size and convergence

Part 0 §24 gave the algorithm and showed empirically that the learning rate decides between crawling, converging and exploding. Here is why, quantitatively.

Take the simplest non-trivial objective, a quadratic bowl $f(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac12 \mathbf{x}^\top A \mathbf{x}$ with $A$ symmetric positive definite. Its gradient is $A\mathbf{x}$, so one step of gradient descent is

$$\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_t - \alpha A \mathbf{x}_t = (I - \alpha A)\,\mathbf{x}_t$$

Each step multiplies by the matrix $I - \alpha A$. After $t$ steps you have multiplied by $(I - \alpha A)^t$; Part I §17 tells us exactly what repeated multiplication does: work in the eigenbasis, where each direction is scaled independently by $(1 - \alpha \lambda_i)^t$.

The convergence condition

Descent converges if and only if $|1 - \alpha\lambda_i| < 1$ for every eigenvalue, which requires

$$0 < \alpha < \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}$$

The largest curvature sets the maximum safe step. Exceed it and that direction amplifies every iteration, and the whole thing diverges.

Now the trap. The step size is capped by $\lambda_{\max}$, but progress along the flattest direction is governed by $\lambda_{\min}$. With the best possible step size, the rate of convergence works out to depend on

$$\frac{\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min}} = \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}$$

where $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ is the condition number from §5. When $\kappa = 1$ that ratio is 0 and you arrive in a single step. When $\kappa = 1000$ it is $0.998$, and each iteration removes two-tenths of one percent of the error.

Intuition: the narrow valley

An ill-conditioned bowl is a long narrow valley. Standing on its wall, the steepest direction points mostly across the valley toward the opposite wall, not along it toward the minimum.

So descent bounces from wall to wall, making rapid progress in the direction that does not matter and creeping along the one that does. You cannot fix this by increasing the step size, because the step size is already capped by the steep direction. That is the bind, and every method in Part D exists to escape it.

The assumption the guarantee rests on

The convergence analysis above is for a quadratic, which has a constant Hessian. For a general function, the question is how much the gradient can change between two nearby points, because a very rapidly changing gradient means the local linear model expires almost immediately and the guaranteed improvement fails to materialise.

Definition: Lipschitz continuity of the gradient

The gradient $\nabla f$ is $L$-Lipschitz if there is a constant $L$ such that

$$\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) - \nabla f(\mathbf{y})\| \leq L\,\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|$$

for every pair of points $\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{y}$. $L$ is the Lipschitz constant, the maximum rate at which the gradient can change.

For a quadratic $f(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac12\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}$, the gradient is $A\mathbf{x}$ and the Lipschitz constant is $\lambda_{\max}$, the largest eigenvalue, exactly the quantity that set the stability limit above. That is not a coincidence. For any smooth function, $L$ is an upper bound on the spectral norm of the Hessian, and the step size condition $\alpha < 2/L$ is precisely the quadratic stability condition with $L$ in place of $\lambda_{\max}$.

The Lipschitz condition is the standard assumption under which first-order methods receive any formal guarantee. It says the gradient does not jump faster than you step, which is what allows a local measurement to be trusted for the length of one step. Without it, the gradient you computed is already stale before you land.

§10 Curvature and the Hessian

Definition: the Hessian

The matrix of all second partial derivatives:

$$H_{i,j} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial \theta_i \, \partial \theta_j}$$

It is symmetric whenever $f$ is twice continuously differentiable, because $\partial^2 f/\partial x\,\partial y$ and $\partial^2 f/\partial y\,\partial x$ are the same number.

Symmetric is exactly the hypothesis of Part I's spectral theorem, so the Hessian always has real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors. Those are not technical conveniences; they are the whole reason curvature is analysable:

What the eigenvalues mean

Each eigenvector of $H$ is a direction; the corresponding eigenvalue is the curvature along it. Positive means the surface bends upward, negative downward, near-zero means flat.

The Taylor expansion, since everything below rests on it

The next line introduces a tool this book has not used before and will not stop using after, so it is worth a paragraph rather than a symbol.

The idea is this: a complicated function can be replaced, near a single point, by a simple one that agrees with it there. Not everywhere (that would be too much to ask), but in a neighbourhood, and the smaller the neighbourhood the better the agreement. Which simple function? The one that matches as many derivatives as you are willing to pay for.

Take a function of one variable and stand at a point $a$. Three successively better descriptions:

ApproximationMatchesPicture
$f(a)$the valuea flat line at the right height
$f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)$value and slopethe tangent line
$f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \tfrac12 f''(a)(x-a)^2$value, slope and curvaturethe best-fitting parabola
Intuition: describing where you are on a road

A satellite navigation system reporting your position could say three things, each more informative than the last.

"You are at mile 12." True, and useless for predicting the next few seconds. That is the constant term.

"...heading north at 60 mph." Now you can predict where you will be shortly, and the prediction is good for as long as nothing changes. That is the linear term, the tangent line, and it is exactly what a gradient gives you.

"...and the road is curving left." Now your prediction survives longer, because you have accounted for the fact that the direction itself is changing. That is the quadratic term, and the curvature is the second derivative.

Each term extends how far ahead you can see before the description fails. And each is local: none of them knows about a bridge ten miles away. That locality is the permanent caveat on everything built from a Taylor expansion, and §12's fourth objection to Newton's method is precisely it.

Two things make this the right tool here. First, you already have the ingredients: the gradient and the Hessian are exactly the first and second derivatives, so the approximation costs nothing beyond what you were computing anyway. Second, the quadratic truncation is not merely a convenience: it is the simplest approximation that has a minimum at all. A linear approximation is a tilted plane, which slopes off to infinity and has no bottom to aim at. You need the quadratic term before "step to the minimum of the model" is even a sentence.

Note also what happens when the function is quadratic. Then the third derivative and everything after it are zero, the expansion is not an approximation but an exact rewriting, and any method built on it lands the answer in one move. That is the whole explanation of Figure 5, one section from now.

So: the Hessian gives the local quadratic picture of any function. Taylor expanding around a point $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$:

$$f(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \approx f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) + \nabla f^\top (\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) + \tfrac12 (\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)^\top H (\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)$$

Three terms: the current value, the slope, and the curvature. Gradient descent uses the first two and throws away the third. Everything in the rest of this part is about what you can buy with the term it discards.

§11 Newton's method

In 60 seconds

Fit a quadratic to the function at your current point and jump straight to that quadratic's minimum. The update is $\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - H^{-1}\nabla f$. On a genuinely quadratic objective it arrives in one step, regardless of conditioning, because it rescales each direction by its own curvature rather than using one global step size.

The derivation is short. Take the quadratic approximation above, differentiate it with respect to $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, and set the result to zero:

$$\nabla f + H(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_0) = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol{\theta} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_0 - H^{-1}\nabla f$$
Intuition: $H^{-1}$ undoes the distortion

Gradient descent applies the same step size to every direction, which is wrong whenever directions have different curvature. Multiplying by $H^{-1}$ divides each direction by its own curvature: a large step where the surface is flat, a small one where it is steep.

Equivalently: Newton's method changes coordinates so the valley becomes a circular bowl, takes the obvious step, and changes back. It is conditioning-independent by construction, which is precisely the property §9 showed gradient descent lacks.

History: three names, and only one of them is on it

Newton described a method for finding roots around 1669, but the version he wrote is nearly unrecognisable: it is algebraic, applies only to polynomials, and does not use derivatives.

Joseph Raphson published a simplified iterative form in 1690, which is closer to what is taught. Neither used calculus notation. It was Thomas Simpson in 1740 who gave the modern derivative-based statement, extended it to systems of equations, and (critically) applied it to optimisation rather than only to root-finding, by seeking the zero of the derivative.

The method used in this section is essentially Simpson's. It is called Newton's, and Raphson gets an occasional hyphen. Stigler's Law, met in Part I §9 and Part II §15, claims another one.

§12 Why nobody uses Newton at scale

Newton's method is better than gradient descent in every way except the ones that matter for large models. Four objections, in increasing order of severity.

1. The Hessian is enormous

For $n$ parameters the Hessian has $n^2$ entries. At a modest $n = 10^6$ that is $10^{12}$ numbers, several terabytes, for one matrix, that must be rebuilt every iteration. Contemporary models have far more parameters than that. This objection alone ends the discussion for deep networks.

2. Solving with it is expensive

Even when the matrix fits, solving $H\mathbf{d} = -\nabla f$ costs on the order of $n^3$ operations, against $n$ for a gradient step.

3. It is attracted to the wrong points

Newton's method solves $\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$. It does not distinguish minima from maxima from saddles; it converges happily to any of them. At a saddle, where $H$ has negative eigenvalues, the "step toward the minimum" of the local quadratic can point uphill. Given §18's result about how common saddles are in high dimensions, this is disqualifying rather than academic.

4. The quadratic model is only local

Far from a minimum the quadratic approximation may be poor, and the full Newton step can overshoot badly or leave the region where the model was valid at all.

Why this matters: what survives

The response is not to abandon curvature but to approximate it cheaply. Three families, all of which you will meet:

  • Quasi-Newton methods build up an approximation to $H^{-1}$ from the sequence of gradients already computed. L-BFGS is the standard one and is genuinely used, though rarely for deep networks, because it assumes gradients are exact and §14's are not.
  • Diagonal approximations keep only the diagonal of $H$, costing $n$ instead of $n^2$. This is the honest description of what Adam and RMSProp are doing in §16.
  • Trust regions and damping add a multiple of the identity, $H + \mu I$, which forces positive definiteness and interpolates between Newton and gradient descent as $\mu$ grows.

Every practical optimiser in this book is somewhere on the line between "ignore curvature entirely" and "use it exactly". Knowing what the exact method would do is what makes the approximations legible.

§13 Line search

A different response to the step size problem: rather than fixing $\alpha$ in advance, choose it at each iteration by trying.

Having computed a descent direction $\mathbf{d}$, the natural question is how far to go along it. That is a one-dimensional problem: minimise $\phi(\alpha) = f(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \alpha\mathbf{d})$, and it can be attacked directly.

In practice nobody minimises $\phi$ exactly; it is not worth the function evaluations. Instead you accept any step that decreases the objective by enough, where "enough" is made precise by requiring the decrease to be at least some fraction of what the slope predicted. That is the Armijo condition, and backtracking until it holds is the standard cheap line search.

Pitfall: line search is largely absent from deep learning

Everything above is standard in classical optimisation and rare in neural network training. The reason is §14: with stochastic gradients, the objective you evaluate is noisy, so "did the loss decrease?" is not a question a single evaluation can answer. A line search on a noisy objective chases noise.

So deep learning replaced adaptive step sizes with schedules (§17) and per-parameter scaling (§16), both of which work without needing to re-evaluate the objective. This is worth knowing when you read the classical literature and wonder why its central techniques are missing from practice.

Try it

A quadratic has Hessian eigenvalues $\lambda = \{0.5,\ 20\}$. What is the largest stable learning rate for gradient descent? What is the condition number? Roughly how many iterations to reduce the error by a factor of 100?

Answer. Stability requires $\alpha < 2/\lambda_{\max} = 2/20 = 0.1$.

The condition number is $20/0.5 = 40$. The per-iteration error factor is $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1) = 39/41 \approx 0.951$.

To reach a factor of $0.01$ we need $0.951^t = 0.01$, so $t = \ln(0.01)/\ln(0.951) \approx 92$ iterations. Newton's method would take one. That gap (92 against 1, on a two-dimensional problem with a mild condition number of 40) is why curvature is worth so much, and why Part D spends five sections trying to recover some of it cheaply.
