Glossary

2:4 sparsity XIX §20
Exactly two of every four consecutive weights are zero. Regular enough for hardware support and free enough to choose which two, giving close to the theoretical 2× at a modest accuracy cost: the case where the constraint is worth more than the freedom it costs.
A* XXXIII §8
Search that expands the node of least $f(n)=g(n)+h(n)$: cost paid plus cost estimated. With an admissible heuristic it returns an optimal solution; with a consistent one it may also close states safely. Every uninformed algorithm is the same procedure with a different priority, so this is a choice of what to sort by rather than a new idea.
Ablation XIV §21
Removing a component to measure its contribution. Two forms answer different questions: leave-one-out removes each part from the full system and asks whether it is necessary; add-one-in adds each to the baseline alone and asks whether it is sufficient. They disagree whenever components interact, which is usual. An honest ablation re-tunes whatever the removal invalidated, or says plainly that it did not.
Activation function VII §7
The nonlinearity between layers. Without one, depth collapses to a single linear map. ReLU is the default because its derivative is exactly 1 where active.
Activation patching XXI §10
Running a model on a corrupted input while splicing in one activation from a clean run, and measuring how much of the clean answer returns. Reported as a fraction between 0 and 1. It localises where the difference between two inputs is carried, so a component essential to the computation but identical in both runs scores exactly 0.
Active learning XIII §30
Labelling the examples the current model is least certain about. Effective, and it builds a feedback loop: the labelled set is no longer a sample of the frame, and a category the model is confidently wrong about is never selected, because confidence is what selection is keyed on.
Actor–critic XVIII §28
A policy (the actor) trained by policy gradient, with a learned value function (the critic) used both as a baseline and to bootstrap the return. Converts the policy gradient's variance problem into the critic's bias problem, which is the better of the two.
Adam III §16
Momentum plus per-parameter scaling by recent squared gradients, both bias-corrected. A cheap diagonal stand-in for curvature rescaling.
Admissible heuristic XXXIII §9
One that never overestimates the remaining cost, $h(n)\le h^*(n)$ everywhere. Buys optimality for A* in three lines of proof. It is a property of the maximum, not the mean: a heuristic accurate on 99.9% of states can be inadmissible, and one overestimate on an optimal path is enough.
Advantage XVIII §27
$A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)$: how much better an action is than the state's average. Using it in place of the raw return is what stops a policy gradient reinforcing every action taken during a good episode.
Adversarial example VIII §20
An input perturbed by the sign of the loss gradient: a step the size of a rounding error, in every dimension at once. Defeats a classifier at a magnitude where random noise of the same size does essentially nothing. The gap is a fact about dimension, and it appears in linear models, which is the observation that reframed the phenomenon.
Adversarial training VIII §20
Including adversarially perturbed examples during training. Augmentation whose transformations are chosen by the model's own weaknesses rather than by hand. Costs clean accuracy (the two objectives genuinely conflict) and roughly doubles the cost per step.
Aleatoric uncertainty XXIV §2
Uncertainty in the world rather than in the model: the same input genuinely produces different outcomes. It does not fall with more data (Figure 1 of Part XXIV measures it flat at 0.16 while the epistemic term falls 213-fold), which is the test that separates the two.
Aliasing XXVII §3
The appearance of a frequency above half the sampling rate as a different, lower frequency in the samples. Not attenuation and not blur: the content is relabelled and added to whatever was legitimately there, so nothing downstream can separate them. Every stride and every pooling layer in a network is a resampling, and Figure 1 of Part XXVII measures the resulting shift-inconsistency at 3.34×10−2 after four stages against float32 rounding at 1.19×10−7.
Alignment tax XX §32
Capability lost on tasks unrelated to the preferences that post-training optimised. The mechanism is fine-tuning on a narrow distribution distorting the model away from it (Part XIX §31), and the mitigations are the same: mix in pretraining data, keep the KL penalty, use small learning rates.
All-reduce XVII §21
The collective that averages gradients across data-parallel devices. A ring implementation moves about $2N$ values per device, independent of the batch size, which is why the only lever for efficiency is giving each device more tokens, and why weak scaling works where strong scaling does not.
Alpha–beta pruning XXXIII §35
Carrying the best value each player can already guarantee and abandoning a branch the opponent would never permit. Returns exactly the minimax value (the pruning is not an approximation), and with good move ordering costs about the square root of the full tree.
Amortised inference XXVIII §22
Training a network to map an observation to its approximate posterior's parameters, so inference on a new point is a forward pass rather than an optimisation. This is the VAE's encoder. Its cost is the amortisation gap (the difference between the network's output and the best member of the family for that point), which adds to the family's own error rather than replacing it.
Ancestral sampling XVI §6
Sampling a directed model by drawing each variable from its conditional once its parents are known. One pass, exact, no iteration. Every generative model that is pleasant to sample from is pleasant for this reason; undirected models have no generative order and must run a Markov chain instead.
Anisotropy XV §25
The tendency of learned embeddings to occupy a narrow cone rather than spreading over the sphere, so that the average cosine between two unrelated items sits far above zero. Costs resolution: most of the available range is spent on a constant offset. Largely repaired by subtracting the mean.
Anti-aliasing XXVII §6
Attenuating content above the new Nyquist frequency before subsampling: in a network, a small blur inserted before each stride. It cannot be done afterwards, because the information needed to separate real from aliased content was destroyed by the sampling. A real filter has a transition band, so it reduces rather than removes the effect: Figure 1 of Part XXVII measures 11.7×.
Anytime algorithm XXXIII §11
One that has a usable answer at every instant and improves it given more time. Anytime weighted A* starts at a large weight and decreases it, so an interruption returns not just a path but a path with a known worst case. What makes search usable inside a fixed control cycle.
Aperture problem XXIX §22
A local window on a straight moving edge determines only the motion component normal to the edge; the tangential component is not present in the data. Figure 5 of Part XXIX measures the brightness-constancy residual as exactly zero along a whole line of candidate velocities for an edge, and at a single point for a corner. One of two exact, provable capability limits in this book; the other is Part XXVI's Weisfeiler–Leman bound. A network can supply a prior from context, which is inference rather than measurement.
Argmin / argmax 0 §2
The input that minimises or maximises a function, as opposed to the resulting value. Training a model is always an argmin: you want the parameters, not the loss number.
Arithmetic intensity XIX §3 · XXXII §3
FLOPs performed per byte moved from memory. Compared against the hardware's ridge point (peak compute over bandwidth) it says whether an operation is compute-bound or memory-bound. Generating one token at a time has an intensity of 1 against a ridge of several hundred, which is why almost everything in Part XIX is about bytes rather than arithmetic.
Armijo condition III §13
Accept a step only if it decreases the objective by at least a fixed fraction of what the slope predicted. The stopping rule for backtracking line search.
Associative scan XI §33
Evaluating a recurrence in $\log_2 L$ depth by exploiting that its composition is associative: applying $(a_1,b_1)$ then $(a_2,b_2)$ is $(a_1a_2,\;a_2b_1+b_2)$. Only linear recurrences compose this way, which is why linearity is the price of parallelism. The Hillis–Steele form does $O(L\log L)$ work and the Blelloch form $O(L)$; both are called "parallel scan".
Associativity I §7
$(AB)C = A(BC)$. Holds for matrices, and is not merely decorative: regrouping a product can change the cost of a computation by orders of magnitude.
Asymptotic efficiency V §5
Attaining the smallest possible variance among unbiased estimators, in the large-sample limit. Maximum likelihood has this property, which is the real argument for it.
Attention XII §2
A weighted average over positions whose weights are computed from the content. Three requirements force the design: the weights must depend on what is being asked (a query), on what each position offers (a key), and must be differentiable (a softmax rather than a choice).
Autoencoder XV §7
An encoder $f$ and decoder $g$ trained so that $g(f(\mathbf{x}))$ reconstructs $\mathbf{x}$, and designed to be unable to do so perfectly. That last clause is the content: an autoencoder that copies exactly has learned the identity function. The constraint may be a narrow code, a penalty on the representation, or corruption of the input.
Autograd VII §16, §21
Automatic differentiation: applying the chain rule numerically at each node of a computational graph. Exact, unlike finite differences, and cheap, unlike symbolic differentiation.
Auxiliary head IX §27
An extra copy of the output attached to an intermediate layer and trained on the same task, then discarded. Delivers a large gradient directly to lower layers, which is greedy pretraining's benefit obtained in a single phase.
Average precision XXIX §4
Ranking detections by confidence and integrating precision over recall, with a match declared above an IoU threshold. Every detection number in the literature is this at some threshold, and the threshold moves the answer: Figure 1 of Part XXIX measures AP falling from 1.000 to 0.116 between IoU 0.5 and 0.9 on one detector, and two detectors changing places. Being a ranking metric, it is unchanged by any monotone rescaling of the confidences, so it cannot see calibration at all.
Backdoor XXXII §35
Training data corrupted so a model behaves normally except on inputs carrying a trigger the attacker chose. Worse than ordinary poisoning because accuracy is exactly what it preserves, so every ordinary evaluation passes. Inherited by anything downstream of a poisoned dataset or a downloaded checkpoint, and no general detection method exists.
Backdoor criterion XXII §12
A set $Z$ satisfies it for $(X,Y)$ when no member is a descendant of $X$ and it blocks every path from $X$ to $Y$ beginning with an arrow into $X$. Then the interventional distribution equals $\sum_z p(y \mid x,z)p(z)$: note the marginal weights. The first condition is the formal reason adjusting for everything available is wrong.
Backpropagation VII §14
Reverse-mode automatic differentiation applied to a network. Four equations: the output error, its backward recursion through the layers, and the weight and bias gradients at each layer. Not a separate algorithm from reverse mode; the same chain rule, applied to this specific computation graph.
Backpropagation through time XI §6
Ordinary backpropagation applied to the unfolded graph. Costs $O(\tau)$ time (inherently sequential, since $\mathbf{h}^{(t)}$ needs $\mathbf{h}^{(t-1)}$) and $O(\tau)$ memory for the stored states.
Backward stability III §6
An algorithm is backward stable if its computed answer is the exact answer to a slightly perturbed problem. Combined with conditioning it gives the whole accuracy story.
Bagging VIII §18 · XXV §8
Averaging models fitted to bootstrap-resampled data. Ensemble error is $\frac{1}{k}v + \frac{k-1}{k}c$: variance falls like $1/k$ only to the extent the members disagree; bias does not move. This is why ensembling diverse models matters and ensembling identical ones does not. Trees are the family it works best on, because they are the family with the most variance to remove, and a bagged tree is therefore grown deep on purpose, where a lone tree is not.
Basis vector I §4
$\mathbf{e}_i$, with a 1 in slot $i$ and zeros elsewhere. The columns of a matrix are exactly the images of the basis vectors, which is why the column view and the function view are the same fact.
Batch normalisation IX §23
Standardising a layer's activations within each minibatch, then learning scale and shift parameters, and differentiating through the mean and variance. Not an optimiser but a reparametrisation. The differentiating-through is the innovation: it removes the gradient's ability to ask for a scale change that normalisation would immediately undo. Why it actually smooths optimisation remains disputed.
Bayes' rule II §21
$P(y \mid x) = P(x \mid y)P(y) / P(x)$. Converts a likelihood into a posterior. Read as: posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior.
Beam search XXXIII §17
Keep the $k$ best partial solutions at each depth and discard the rest: memory and time linear in the depth, at the cost of completeness and optimality. Widening it maximises the model's score more exactly, which helps only while the model agrees with what you actually want.
Behavioural cloning XXXI §18
Fitting a policy to an expert's demonstrations by supervised learning. The easiest way to train a policy and the way most deployed driving policies are trained, with one structural defect: the learner's own errors take it to states the expert never visited, where it is worse, which takes it further off. More expert data does not repair it; data from the states the learner reaches does, which is why an intervention log is worth disproportionately more than ordinary driving data.
Bellman equation XVIII §5
The consistency condition that value decomposes into one step plus the discounted rest. The expectation form is linear in $V^\pi$ and can be solved directly; the optimality form has a max and is not, which is why the optimal case needs iteration.
Benchmark contamination XIV §22
Test items present in the training corpus, so the reported score measures recall rather than generalisation. Distinct from the two other ways a benchmark decays: saturation, where the remaining headroom is label noise, and community overfitting, where the field collectively tunes against one public test set for years. For a model whose corpus is unpublished, no outside party can check.
Benign overfitting XXIII §26
Interpolating the training data, noise included, without being harmed by it: the noise is absorbed into directions carrying little signal. Characterised precisely for linear regression in terms of the covariance spectrum; for deep networks it remains an analogy rather than a theorem.
Bernoulli distribution II §14
One binary trial with success probability $\phi$. What every binary classifier outputs.
Bernstein's inequality XXIII §2
A concentration bound that uses the variance as well as the range. Tighter than Hoeffding when the variance is small (by four orders of magnitude at $p = 0.05$ in Figure 1 of Part XXIII), and looser when it is large, because it pays a worse constant for the privilege. Neither dominates.
Bias correction III §16
Dividing Adam's accumulators by $1-\beta^t$ to compensate for their being initialised at zero. Without it the first several hundred steps are far too small.
Bias (of an estimator) V §2
$\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta] - \theta$. Being systematically off. Unbiasedness is weaker than it sounds and frequently worth giving up.
Bias–variance decomposition V §20
Expected squared error $=$ bias$^2$ $+$ variance $+$ irreducible noise. An identity, and the organising principle of everything after it.
Bidirectional RNN XI §22
Two recurrences, one from each end, whose states are both read by the output. Gives each position a view of the whole sequence without a fixed window, and cannot be used for generation or streaming, because it must see everything before emitting anything.
Bird's-eye view XXIX §31
A top-down metric representation of a scene, which is what a planner reasons in. Reachable from cameras by a ground-plane homography (exact only if the world is flat), by predicting depth and projecting features into 3-D, or by giving each top-down cell a query that attends to the images projecting into it, which is where multi-camera systems have converged.
Boosting XXV §16
Building a model additively, each term fitted to what the previous ones left behind. Attacks bias rather than variance, so it wants shallow base learners, and it can overfit: Figure 4 of Part XXV shows test error turning after 46 trees at a learning rate of 1.
Bootstrap V §10
Resampling your own data with replacement to approximate an estimator's sampling distribution. Needs no formula and works on any statistic you can compute.
Branch and bound XXXIII §19
Depth-first search that computes a bound on the best achievable in each subtree and discards the subtree when the bound cannot beat the incumbent. Exact, and its performance is entirely the bound's tightness: validity makes it correct, tightness makes it an algorithm.
Broadcasting I §6
Silently expanding a smaller array to match a larger one during an elementwise operation. Convenient, and a common source of silent shape bugs.
Buffer IV §8
A tensor a Module owns, saves and moves with itself, but that the optimiser must not update, such as a batch-norm running average. "Part of the model" and "learned by gradient descent" are different questions.
Bundle adjustment XXIX §30
Recovering camera poses and 3-D points together by minimising total reprojection error. A large sparse nonlinear least-squares problem whose sparsity (each point seen in few views) is what makes it tractable. It is maximum likelihood under a Gaussian noise model, and what it needs from the front end is correct correspondences rather than precise ones.
Byte-pair encoding XX §2
Start from bytes, repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair. Frequent words become single tokens, rare words split, and nothing is ever unrepresentable, which is why no unknown token is needed. Merges may not cross a pre-tokenization boundary, and training is done on unique word types weighted by frequency.
Calibration XIV §5
Whether a predicted probability matches the observed frequency: among the cases a model calls 0.8, about 80% should be correct. Independent of accuracy: a model can be accurate and wildly overconfident, which modern networks generally are. Matters wherever the probability is used rather than the argmax: refusal thresholds, expected-cost decisions, downstream ranking.
Canary XIII §33
A constructed record inserted into training at a known frequency so that memorisation can be measured by trying to extract it. The only way to establish a memorisation threshold for your model and your corpus.
Capacity VI §6
The range of functions a model can represent. Raising it lowers bias and raises variance; the useful amount depends on how much data you have. Classical theory treats this as the whole story; double descent showed the story continues past the interpolation threshold.
Catastrophic cancellation III §3
Subtracting two nearly equal numbers, destroying significant digits. Silent: the result looks ordinary and is mostly noise.
Catastrophic forgetting XX §47
The collapse of an earlier capability when a model is trained on something new. Measured here as a fall to chance with half the loss in the first few per cent of the new task's training, and correlating at 0.97 with distance moved in parameter space, so it is a distance rather than a schedule, which is why a small learning rate, a KL penalty and a low-rank adapter are three spellings of one remedy. What is lost is access rather than content: re-exposure recovers it far faster than learning it did.
Categorical distribution II §14
One trial with $k$ possible outcomes. What a multi-class classifier outputs, produced by a softmax. Called multinoulli in some texts.
Causal mask XII §21
Setting the scores for future positions to $-\infty$ before the softmax, so position $i$ sees only positions $\leq i$. It is what makes every position a training example computed in one parallel pass, the property that displaced recurrence. Test it by consequence: changing token $j$ must leave outputs before $j$ bit-identical.
Central limit theorem II §15
Sums of many independent finite-variance quantities tend toward a Gaussian, whatever their individual shapes. One of three reasons the Gaussian is ubiquitous.
Chain of thought XX §21
Producing intermediate steps before the answer. Not a prompting trick: a transformer does fixed computation per token, so generated tokens are additional forward passes and depth in time substitutes for depth in layers. The benefit grows with the number of steps required, and the written chain is not a faithful transcript of the computation.
Chain rule 0 §17
$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}$. The derivative of a composition is the product of the derivatives. Rates through a chain multiply. The single most important result in this volume, and the whole of backpropagation.
Checkerboard artefact X §27
A repeating lattice in the output of a transposed convolution, produced when the kernel size is not divisible by the stride so that output positions receive different numbers of overlapping contributions. Arithmetic, not a tendency; avoided by choosing $k \bmod s = 0$ or by upsampling then convolving.
Checkpoint IV §15
A saved state_dict. Resuming exactly needs the optimiser's state too, since momentum and the squared-gradient average are carried between steps.
Chinchilla scaling XVII §12
The corrected compute-optimal allocation: parameters and tokens should grow together, at roughly 20 tokens per parameter. Replaced an earlier answer that favoured much larger models, which was wrong because its shorter runs used a learning-rate schedule sized for the longest budget. See [[compute-optimal-allocation]].
Circuit XXI §11
A subgraph of a network (specific heads, layers and directions) together with a claim about what each part contributes and how they compose. Falsifiable in a way a feature description is not: it owes you sufficiency, necessity, faithfulness of the parts, and generality beyond the inputs it was found on.
Classifier-free guidance XVI §30
Combining a conditional and unconditional noise prediction from one network as $\epsilon_u + w(\epsilon_c - \epsilon_u)$. At $w=1$ it is the conditional model; above 1 it extrapolates, and samples from something proportional to $p(\mathbf{x})p(c \mid \mathbf{x})^w$ (a different distribution, chosen because it looks better). Past a peak the samples leave the distribution entirely rather than merely becoming less varied.
Cliff IX §9
A near-vertical region of the loss surface produced when repeated matrix multiplications through time amplify the gradient exponentially. One ordinary step from a cliff face can raise the cost by orders of magnitude. Gradient clipping survives this by rescaling the gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold, preserving the direction and bounding only the distance.
Co-adaptation VIII §19
Units learning to depend on each other, so neither is useful alone. Fragile, and a good way to fit noise. Dropout's second explanation is that it prevents this.
Cohen's kappa XIII §5
Chance-corrected agreement, $(p_o - p_e)/(1 - p_e)$. High values establish that the task is well specified and the guideline is being followed, not that the guideline is right, since shared training produces shared systematic error. Report it per class; the aggregate hides the rare one.
Collider XXII §4 · XXVIII §4
A common effect of two variables. Unlike a confounder it blocks the path between them until you condition on it, or on any of its descendants, at which point it opens and manufactures an association. Measured in Figure 2 of Part XXII: two independent variables reach a correlation of −0.519 purely by conditioning.
Colour refinement XXVI §16
Repeatedly replacing each node's colour with a hash of its own colour and the multiset of its neighbours'. Also called one-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman. A message-passing network is exactly as powerful as this at distinguishing graphs, which is why the limit is a theorem rather than a tendency: Figure 3 of Part XXVI shows two graphs it cannot separate receiving representations that differ by exactly zero.
Column space I §12
The span of a matrix's columns: everything $A\mathbf{x}$ can possibly equal. $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution if and only if $\mathbf{b}$ lies in it.
Complex-step derivative XIV §9
$f'(x) = \text{Im}\big(f(x + i\epsilon)\big)/\epsilon + O(\epsilon^2)$, for analytic $f$ evaluated on complex inputs. No subtraction appears, so the cancellation that gives finite differences their U-curve is absent and $\epsilon$ may be $10^{-150}$. Requires analyticity, which excludes abs, max, ReLU and any branch on the value.
Composition 0 §7
$f(g(x))$: feeding one function's output into another. A deep network is a composition of functions; that is what "deep" means.
Computational graph VII §12
An expression drawn as a directed acyclic graph of elementary operations. Nodes are values; edges record which operation produced each value from which inputs. The object a backward pass walks, accumulating gradients by the chain rule at each node.
Compute-optimal allocation XVII §9
Choosing $N$ and $D$ to minimise loss subject to $6ND = C$. With the additive law it has a closed form, $N \propto C^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}$. Optimal for training compute only; once a model serves traffic the lifetime objective moves the answer to a smaller model trained longer.
Concept drift XIV §26
The relationship $p(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ changes: the same input now warrants a different answer. The hardest of the three shifts to detect, because inputs and label frequencies can both look unchanged. Only labelled data from the current distribution reveals it.
Condition number III §5
$\kappa(A) = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$. The factor by which input error is amplified. A property of the problem, not the algorithm: $\kappa = 10^k$ costs about $k$ digits.
Conditional statistic VII §10
Which summary of $p(y\mid\mathbf{x})$ a loss recovers. Squared error gives the conditional mean, absolute error the median, which is why squared-error generators produce blurry averages of plausible outputs.
Confidence interval V §8–9
An interval produced by a procedure that captures the truth a stated fraction of the time. The guarantee attaches to the procedure, not to any interval you hold.
Configuration space XXXIII §23
One dimension per degree of freedom, so a whole robot in a whole pose is one point. Obstacles become the set of configurations that collide, whose shape depends on the robot's geometry as much as the obstacle's, and which nobody computes: what is available instead is a collision check.
Conformal prediction XXIV §24
A wrapper giving exact finite-sample marginal coverage for any model and any distribution, requiring only exchangeability between calibration and test. Figure 4 of Part XXIV hits nominal to within 0.002 for a good model and for a predictor that ignores the input; the model controls the width, not the validity.
Confounder XXII §3
A common cause of treatment and outcome. Creates association without causation, and the resulting error is bias rather than variance: it does not shrink with sample size and is invisible to every diagnostic in Part XIV.
Confused deputy XXXII §37
A privileged component persuaded to act on behalf of someone who lacks the privilege. A model that reads untrusted content and takes actions is one by construction: it has a single channel, so text in a document can be read as a command and executed with the user's authority. SQL solved this structurally with parameterised queries and that fix is unavailable here, which leaves least privilege, isolation and confirmation.
Conjugate directions IX §20
$\mathbf{d}_t^\top H \mathbf{d}_{t-1} = 0$: orthogonality in the geometry the Hessian defines. Descending along one does not undo the minimisation along the other, which is what steepest descent with a line search cannot avoid doing.
Connectionist temporal classification XXX §11
Training a sequence model without an alignment by summing over every frame labelling that collapses to the target, using an HMM's forward recursion. Its cost is a conditional independence assumption: given the input there is no term coupling one output symbol to another. Figure 4 of Part XXX measures the consequence: asked for an equal mixture of two readings, the best CTC model puts 0.25 on each and leaks half the mass onto sequences the target never contains, at every input length. An external language model is restoring that term rather than refining the estimate.
Consistency V §5
Converging to the truth as the sample grows without bound. Weaker than unbiasedness at any finite $n$, and more important.
Consistent heuristic XXXIII §12
Satisfies the triangle inequality $h(n)\le c(n,n')+h(n')$ on every edge. Strictly stronger than admissible: it makes $f$ non-decreasing along a path, so the first expansion of a state already has its cheapest route and closing it is safe. Learned heuristics are almost never consistent.
Contamination XIII §13
Evaluation material present in the training data. Inflates the reported score and lowers the true one, because leaked copies displace real training signal: two errors in opposite directions, which is why nothing looks wrong.
Context vector XI §27
The fixed-size summary an encoder passes to a decoder. Every fact the decoder needs must fit through it, and it is computed before the encoder knows what will be asked, which is the bottleneck attention removes.
Continuation method IX §28
Solving a sequence of increasingly difficult objectives, each initialised from the solution of the previous one. The first objective in the sequence is easy, often constructed by blurring or adding noise. Fails when the easy objective never convexifies, when the minimum tracks to a local rather than global one, or when so many stages are needed that the total cost exceeds just solving the hard problem directly.
Continuous batching XIX §38
Scheduling a serving batch at the granularity of a decode step rather than a request, so a finished sequence's slot is refilled immediately. Several times the throughput at the same latency, from scheduling alone.
Contractive autoencoder XV §13
An autoencoder penalising the squared Frobenius norm of the encoder's Jacobian, so the code resists infinitesimal changes of the input. The infinitesimal counterpart of denoising. Its surviving large singular directions estimate the tangent space of the data manifold. Requires tied weights, or the penalty is defeated by scaling the encoder down and the decoder up.
Contrastive divergence XVI §39
Approximating an energy-based model's negative phase by running the Markov chain a few steps from the data rather than to convergence. What made Boltzmann machines trainable, at the cost of a biased gradient.
Contrastive learning XV §17
Learning a representation by requiring two altered views of the same input to agree and views of different inputs to disagree. No labels, no reconstruction. What the representation keeps is decided entirely by which differences the two views are allowed to have; see [[augmentation-as-specification]].
Contrastive temperature XXX §27
The scale dividing similarities inside a contrastive loss. Described as a scale on the logits and better understood as a choice of how many negatives you are training against: Figure 6 of Part XXX measures 508 effective negatives of 512 at $\tau = 1$ and 1.1 at $\tau = 0.01$, where the single hardest negative takes 98.5% of the gradient. So a low temperature is implicit hard-negative mining, and below some value a larger batch buys nothing.
Control task XV §32
A probing diagnostic: give every example identity a fixed random label, consistent between train and test. The mapping is perfectly learnable and completely meaningless, so a probe that scores well on it was memorising identities. Introduced by Hewitt and Liang, and the reason probe accuracy alone establishes nothing.
Convexity 0 §20
A function is convex if the line segment between any two points on its graph lies above or on it, or equivalently, it curves upward everywhere. There is then at most one minimum, and it is global: any local minimum is the global one. Neural network losses are not convex; the field traded this guarantee for the expressive power that non-convexity allows.
Convolution X §2
$S(i,j) = \sum_{m,n} I(m,n)K(i-m,j-n)$: a weighted sum of a neighbourhood, applied identically at every position. Linear, so it is a matrix multiplication with two constraints: sparse, and with its nonzero entries repeated.
Convolution theorem XXVII §14
Convolution in one domain is pointwise multiplication in the other. Equivalently, the complex sinusoids are the eigenvectors of every convolution and the frequency response is the list of eigenvalues. This is the result the whole of Part X rests on: it is why a filter has a one-line description, why a 3×3 kernel cannot be frequency-selective, and why stacking kernels can be.
Coordinate check XXIII §51
The diagnostic for a parameterisation: take one optimiser step at several widths and plot the change in each layer's activations, and in the output, on log axes. Flat lines mean the update size does not depend on width. Measure the change in the function rather than in an activation: the latter falls with width under every scheme including the unstable one, so it shows no instability anywhere.
Coordinate descent IX §24
Minimising with respect to one variable or block at a time, cycling. Fast when the coordinates are nearly independent and arbitrarily slow as they become coupled, measured at 6,912 sweeps against Newton's one.
Covariance II §12
Whether two variables tend to move together. Detects only linear association: zero covariance does not imply independence.
Covariance matrix II §13
All pairwise covariances, with variances on the diagonal. Always symmetric and positive semidefinite, which is exactly why PCA works.
Covariate shift XIV §26 · XXIII §39
The input distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$ changes while $p(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ does not. Detectable without labels, which is why input monitoring catches it first. Correctable by importance weighting, though Figure 6 of Part XXIII measures the correction achieving nothing when the model is correctly specified: with the conditional unchanged and the model able to represent it, there is no compromise to relocate.
Coverage XIV §4
The fraction of cases a system answers, having been permitted to refuse the rest. Turns an unreachable accuracy target into a reachable optimisation: hold accuracy at the required level and maximise how often the system is willing to respond. Refusing everything is perfectly accurate at zero coverage, which is why the pair must always be reported together.
Coverage (of an interval) XXIV §1
The fraction of the time an interval contains the truth (a different quantity from Part XIV §4's coverage, which is the fraction of cases a system chooses to answer). An interval of nominal coverage $1-\alpha$ promises this over repeated draws, and measuring it is the only thing separating an uncertainty estimate from a decoration: Figure 3 of Part XXIV puts four standard methods between 0.790 and 0.993 against a nominal 0.95.
Credible interval V §9
The Bayesian counterpart, about which you may legitimately say "95% probability the value lies here". The price is a prior.
Critical batch size XVII §33
The batch beyond which a less noisy gradient no longer reduces the number of steps needed. Below it, doubling the batch roughly halves the steps; above it, the extra compute is nearly wasted. Estimated by the gradient noise scale, and it grows during training.
Critical initialisation XXIII §53
The initialisation variance at which the per-layer gradient multiplier equals one, separating an ordered phase where representations of distinct inputs collapse together from a chaotic one where nearby inputs are driven apart. Correlations decay slowest there, so information about the input survives the most layers.
Critical point 0 §19
Where the derivative is zero. Every minimum and maximum is one, but so are saddles; the first derivative alone cannot tell them apart.
Cross-attention XII §6
Queries from one sequence, keys and values from another. The 2014 mechanism, which removed the encoder–decoder bottleneck while leaving the recurrence (and therefore the sequential training) in place.
Cross-correlation X §3
Convolution without the kernel flip. Loses commutativity, which nothing in a network needs, and is what every library computes while calling it convolution. Matters when loading weights across frameworks.
Cross-entropy II §25
$-\sum_x P(x)\log Q(x)$. Minimising it is exactly minimising KL divergence, and for a one-hot label it collapses to $-\log$ of the probability assigned to the correct answer. Your loss function.
Cross-validation VI §10
Rotating the held-out block through $k$ folds so every point is validated exactly once. A good model-selection procedure, but a biased estimate of the performance of what it selects, because the model chosen is the one that looked best on those same folds.
Curriculum learning IX §28
A continuation method that raises the influence of simple examples early. The stochastic form (always a mix, with the proportion of hard examples rising) works; the obvious deterministic easy-to-hard ordering has been measured not to.
Curse of dimensionality VI §21
The number of examples needed to cover a space grows exponentially with dimension, and distances lose their meaning. Named by Bellman.
Damped Newton IX §19
$[H + \alpha I]^{-1}\nabla J$, adding $\alpha$ to every eigenvalue so that saddle directions become descent directions. The same operator as ridge regression, and it interpolates to gradient descent with rate $1/\alpha$ as $\alpha$ grows.
Data leakage XIII §11
Information about the target reaching the model that will not exist at prediction time. Five kinds: target, train–test, temporal, preprocessing, group. The preprocessing case (fitting a scaler before splitting) is a two-line ordering mistake that survives review because it inflates by about the margin most results claim.
DataLoader IV §11
Wraps a Dataset and yields shuffled batches. shuffle=True is Part II §17's empirical distribution implemented; without it the gradient estimate is no longer unbiased.
Dataset augmentation VIII §10
Transforming training examples in ways that should not change the label. The only regulariser carrying real domain knowledge, and useless or harmful when the invariance is false.
Dead ReLU VII §19
A unit whose pre-activation is negative for every input, so its output and gradient are permanently zero. Usually caused by too large a learning rate.
Dead unit XIV §12
A rectifier whose pre-activation is negative on essentially every input, so its gradient is zero and it never recovers. A rising dead fraction is one of the cheapest instruments in training: it reports capacity being silently lost, usually to a learning rate that was briefly too large.
Deadly triad XVIII §21
Function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy sampling. Each is individually reasonable and necessary; together the iteration can have no fixed point and the weights diverge. Measured here at $\|\mathbf{w}\|$ going from 10 to 1,084, with any one removal restoring convergence. Not fixed by a smaller step size.
Decision threshold XXIV §33
The probability above which acting has lower expected cost, equal to $c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}})$. It depends only on the cost ratio, never on the base rate or the model, and 0.5 is correct only when the two mistakes cost the same.
Decision tree VI §16
Recursive axis-aligned splits producing constant predictions in boxes. Assumes axis-aligned structure matters, which tabular data often has and images do not. Part XXV §2 builds it from the splitting rule upward: its capacity is the leaf count, and unlike a network it behaves exactly as the classical bias–variance theory says.
Deep ensemble XXIV §19
Several networks trained from different initialisations, averaged. Not Bayesian in derivation and consistently the best-measured practical uncertainty method, because different initialisations find genuinely different modes: diversity of solution beating fidelity to one.
Denoising autoencoder XV §11
An autoencoder given a corrupted input and required to output the clean one. Copying is defeated because the thing to be copied is not what was presented. Its reconstruction-minus-input field estimates the score of the data density convolved with the corruption noise, which makes it density estimation in disguise and is what Part XVI is built on.
Denoising score matching XVI §25
Estimating the score of a noise-smoothed density by training a network to predict the clean point from a corrupted one. Removes score matching's second-derivative cost and its lack of coverage in one move. The identity diffusion is built on: see [[score]] and [[diffusion-model]].
Depthwise separable convolution X §19
A $k\times k$ convolution per channel, then a $1\times1$ across channels. Costs $1/c_{\rm out} + 1/k^2$ of a standard convolution (exactly, not approximately), which is about ninefold for $k=3$. The latency saving is much smaller, because it is memory-bound.
Derivative 0 §15
The slope of a curve at a point. Practically: if I nudge the input slightly, how much does the output move?
Detach IV §7
Returns a copy of a tensor with its parent link cut, so gradients stop there. Scoped to one tensor, unlike no_grad, which is scoped to a block.
Determinant I §22
The factor by which a transformation scales volume; negative if orientation flips. Zero exactly when the matrix is singular. A fine conceptual tool and a poor numerical one.
Device IV §12
Where a tensor's bytes live. Operations require matching devices and never transfer silently, because a hidden transfer inside a loop would destroy performance invisibly.
Diagonal matrix I §16
Zero off the main diagonal. Scales each axis independently, and is cheap to store, multiply and invert, which is why so much effort goes into making matrices diagonal.
Dictionary learning XXI §5
Recovering the set of directions whose sparse combinations produce a set of activations: the decomposition that undoes superposition. Implemented with a sparse autoencoder. Scored against known features it recovers substantially more than any linear method and still not all of them.
Difference-in-differences XXII §27
Comparing the change in a treated group against the change in an untreated one, which removes any confounder fixed over time. Assumes parallel trends: a claim about a counterfactual, so untestable, though pre-treatment parallelism can be checked.
Diffusion model XVI §29
A generative model trained to reverse a fixed noising process, by predicting the noise added to a data point at a randomly chosen level. Training is a regression with no adversary, no bound and no sampling from the model. Sampling cost is set by the number of denoising steps, a choice, rather than by the size of the output, which is why it displaced autoregressive models for images.
Dilated convolution X §29
A kernel with gaps between its taps, spanning $1+2d$ pixels with the parameters of a $3\times3$. Buys receptive field at no cost in parameters or compute. A constant dilation across layers grids: the residue classes never mix.
Dirac delta II §17
A generalised function that places all probability mass at a single point: zero everywhere except that point, with total integral one. Used to express the empirical distribution as a sum of one Dirac delta per training example.
Discount factor XVIII §3
$\gamma \in [0,1)$. Makes the return finite, expresses a preference for sooner, and (the load-bearing reason) makes the Bellman operator a contraction. The effective horizon is about $1/(1-\gamma)$, and $\gamma$ changes what the optimal policy is, so it should be reported.
Discrete Fourier transform XXVII §10
The change of basis from samples to frequencies, as an $n \times n$ matrix product costing $O(n^2)$. It treats the record as one period of a periodic signal, which is where spectral leakage comes from and why windows exist.
Disentanglement XV §39
A representation whose separate coordinates correspond to separate underlying factors of variation. Unidentifiable without supervision: any invertible map to a different set of independent factors induces the identical distribution over observations, so no data-derived objective can prefer one to the other. Weak supervision, temporal structure or interventions restore it.
Distillation XIX §24
Training a small student to match a large teacher's full output distribution rather than its answer. The information is in the relative probabilities of the wrong classes, raised to visibility by the temperature, which is why a student can be trained on unlabelled data.
Distributed representation XV §4
A code describing an input by many independently settable features, as against a symbolic code assigning one symbol per input. Its advantage is usually misstated: $n$ linear-threshold features in $\mathbb{R}^d$ distinguish $\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{n}{j} = O(n^d)$ regions, polynomial in $n$ and exponential in $d$. The deeper point is that concepts share entries, so what is learned about one transfers to another.
Distribution shift VI §5
Test or deployment data drawn from a different distribution than training data. Because generalisation bounds assume the two distributions are identical, a validated model can fail badly in production without contradicting any theoretical guarantee. The most common reason a published result does not reproduce in deployment.
Dominance (heuristics) XXXIII §14
$h_2$ dominates $h_1$ if it is everywhere at least as large and both are admissible; the dominant one never expands more nodes. Hence the elementwise maximum of admissible heuristics is free to use, while their sum generally is not.
Do-operator XXII §11
$\mathrm{do}(X=x)$: delete the equation that produced $X$, substitute the constant, leave every other equation alone. Deleting the equation is what severs the confounding paths, which is what randomisation achieves physically.
Double descent V §20 · XXIII §25
Test error peaks where capacity equals the number of training points, then falls again past it. The bias–variance identity still holds; what fails is the assumption that variance rises monotonically with capacity. Measured in Figure 4 of Part XXIII: the peak reaches 4.708 at $p = 115$ against $n = 120$, and the second descent falls to 0.224, below the best underparameterised fit at 0.541.
Double Q-learning XVIII §19
Keep two value estimates; use one to select the maximising action and the other to evaluate it. The selection is still noisy, the evaluation is independent of that noise, and the [[maximisation-bias]] disappears.
Doubly robust estimator XXII §21
Combines an outcome model and a propensity model so that the estimate is consistent if either is correct. Measured in Figure 4 of Part XXII: exact when one holds, biased by half the effect when neither does. Clipping the weights to control its variance reintroduces bias.
Dropout VIII §19
Deleting units at random during training, with rescaling. Simultaneously bagging over $2^n$ weight-sharing subnetworks and a prohibition on co-adaptation.
d-separation XXII §10 · XXVIII §4
Two nodes are d-separated by a set when every path between them is blocked given that set, which implies conditional independence. Chains and forks are open and close under conditioning; colliders are closed and open under it.
dtype IV §2
A tensor's numeric type. PyTorch defaults to float32 and NumPy to float64, which is the most common dtype error; losses want integer labels and floating-point features.
$e$ 0 §9
$\approx 2.71828$. The unique base for which $\frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x$: the function whose rate of change equals its own value.
Early stopping VIII §13
Halting when validation error stops improving, keeping the best parameters. Self-tuning and nearly free. Traverses the same solution family as $L^2$, in the same order.
Echo state network XI §25
Fix the recurrent weights at random with spectral radius near one, train only a linear readout. A kernel machine with a random temporal feature map, and the training objective is convex. Worth knowing as the baseline any claim about learned temporal representations should beat.
Effective rank XV §20
$\exp$ of the entropy of a representation's normalised covariance spectrum. Returns approximately $d$ for isotropic data in $d$ dimensions and approximately 1 for data on a line. The instrument for detecting [[representation-collapse]], and cheaper and more reliable for that purpose than a probe.
Effective receptive field X §10
The region actually carrying the gradient, as opposed to the region that could. Grows like $\sqrt{L}$ where the theoretical field grows like $L$, because a pixel at the edge is reachable by one path and one at the centre by many. At depth 48 it is about a third of the theoretical field.
Effective sample size XXVIII §30
How many independent draws a correlated or weighted sample is worth. For a chain, $n/(1 + 2\sum_k \rho_k)$; for importance weights, $(\sum w)^2/\sum w^2$. The Monte Carlo standard error must be computed from this and not from the number of draws, which can be larger by orders of magnitude.
Eigenvector / eigenvalue I §17
A direction the matrix only stretches rather than turns, and the factor by which. The matrix's natural axes. A zero eigenvalue means a direction was annihilated.
ELBO XVI §15
The evidence lower bound, $\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})] - D_{KL}(q \parallel p(\mathbf{z}))$. Always at or below $\log p(\mathbf{x})$, with the gap equal to the KL from the approximate posterior to the true one, which cannot be measured, because the true posterior is what $q$ was introduced to avoid. Measured here to vary by a factor of nine across a sweep, enough to rank two models backwards.
Elicitation XXI §31
The effort spent trying to make a model exhibit a capability: prompting, scaffolding, tools, fine-tuning. Every capability evaluation is a lower bound at the elicitation used, so a negative result without a stated elicitation budget cannot be interpreted.
Emergence XVII §7
The claim that some capabilities appear discontinuously with scale. Substantially a measurement artefact: a discontinuous metric such as exact match on a $k$-step task behaves like $p^k$, which looks flat and then rises sharply while the underlying per-step accuracy improved smoothly. Checking with a continuous metric costs nothing.
Empirical distribution II §17
Your dataset, treated as a distribution: equal mass on each observed point. The gap between it and the true distribution is the generalisation problem.
Empirical risk VI §3
Average loss on the training data you have. Computable, unlike true risk, which averages over the whole distribution you will never fully observe. Minimising empirical risk is what every training loop does; the gap between it and true risk is the generalisation problem.
Empirical risk minimisation IX §2
Minimising average loss on the training set as a stand-in for the risk. Rarely done in deep learning, because it overfits and because the loss you care about usually has no usable derivative.
Encoder–decoder XI §27
A sequence-to-vector model (the encoder) composed with a vector-to-sequence model (the decoder), so the input and output lengths need not match. Also called sequence-to-sequence. The architecture that made neural machine translation viable. Its flaw (every fact the decoder needs must be compressed into a single fixed-size context vector, computed before the encoder knows what the decoder will ask) is the bottleneck that attention addresses.
Energy-based model XVI §3
$p(\mathbf{x}) = \exp(-E(\mathbf{x}))/Z$ for any computable $E$. Completely general and therefore not free: the difficulty is entirely in $Z$. Its gradient needs samples from the model at every step, which is the loop the family never escaped.
Entropy II §23
Average surprise: $-\sum P(x)\log P(x)$. Operationally, the minimum average bits needed to encode outcomes from this distribution with an optimal code. Maximised by the uniform distribution, where nothing is predictable; zero for a certain outcome, where no communication is needed.
Epipolar constraint XXIX §28
A point in one image constrains its match in another to a line, because the point, its two projections and the two camera centres are coplanar. Reduces matching from a two-dimensional search to a one-dimensional one, which is why stereo is tractable. Note that the algebraic residual is scale-dependent and only the point-to-line distance in pixels is interpretable.
Epistemic uncertainty XXIV §2
Uncertainty from not having seen enough to know which function is right. Reducible: it falls toward zero with more data, and where it is large is where collecting more is worth most. Active learning is the strategy of sampling where it is largest.
Equivariance X §9 · XXVI §2
A transformation of the input produces the corresponding transformation of the output. Distinct from invariance, where the output does not move at all: a node classifier needs equivariance, a molecule's energy needs invariance. Part XXVI §35 builds an equivariant vector update as a scalar-weighted sum of relative positions, which is what lets a network output a force rather than only a number.
Estimand XXII §40
The quantity you intend to estimate: whose effect, of what, on what, averaged over which population. Naming it precedes choosing an estimator, and most flawed causal work is technically correct about a quantity nobody wanted.
Estimator V §1
Any function of the data. Because the data is random, the estimator is a random variable with a distribution, and every question about its quality concerns that distribution.
Eval mode IV §14
model.eval() disables dropout and switches batch norm to stored statistics. Forgetting it makes evaluation a random draw and biases the score downward, silently.
Evaluation validity XXI §30
Whether an instrument measures the construct it claims to, as distinct from whether it is contaminated or decayed. A refusal-rate benchmark measures refusals; that this is safety is a separate empirical claim the score does not establish.
Evidence lower bound XXVIII §18
$\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x,z)] + \mathbb{H}[q]$, a lower bound on the log evidence whose gap is exactly the KL from the approximation to the true posterior. Maximising it is minimising that KL. One inequality underlies EM, the VAE and every variational method in the literature.
Exchangeability XXIV §29
That the calibration and test points are interchangeable in distribution: weaker than i.i.d. and the single assumption conformal prediction needs. It fails silently: Figure 5 of Part XXIV measures coverage falling to 0.496 against a promised 0.90 with no signal from the method.
Expectation II §10
The probability-weighted average, the balance point of a distribution. Linear always, which makes it the most useful identity in probability.
Expectation–maximisation XXVIII §15
Alternating between inferring the latent variables and re-fitting the parameters as though they were observed. Guarantees the observed-data likelihood never decreases, and guarantees nothing about the optimum reached: Figure 3 of Part XXVIII measures the failure-to-recover rate rising with component separation, because separated components form basins a badly-initialised restart cannot leave.
Expected calibration error XIV §5
Bin predictions by confidence and average $|\text{accuracy} - \text{confidence}|$ over bins, weighted by bin population. A single number for how far a model's stated confidence is from its observed accuracy. Sensitive to the number of bins, so report it.
Expected value of information XXIV §36
The gain in expected utility from making an observation before deciding. Never negative, and zero whenever the observation would not change the action, which is why feature importance is the wrong instrument for deciding what to collect.
Explicit memory XI §26
A set of addressable cells beside the network, with a controller that learns where to read and write. Addresses cannot be optimised directly, so the read is a softmax-weighted average over every cell, which is the same operation as attention, reached from computer architecture rather than from translation.
Exploding gradient VII §18
Gradient magnitude growing exponentially with depth, because per-layer factors exceed 1. Remedied bluntly by clipping.
Exploration XVIII §33 · XXXIII §29
The problem that you only observe the outcome of what you did. Acting greedily is self-confirming: an action underestimated early is never retried, so the error is never corrected. Undirected methods have linear regret; methods that explore in proportion to uncertainty do not.
Exposure bias XI §10 · XVI §12
The mismatch between training (where the model always receives a correct prefix) and generation (where it receives its own previous output). The error compounds: one wrong token shifts the prefix off the training manifold, making the next token worse, and so on. Explains why free-running generation degrades after a few dozen steps even for models with low training loss.
Extensive form XXXIII §37
A game as a tree of sequential moves. With imperfect information the states a player cannot distinguish form an information set, the search must reason over possible worlds rather than a position, and optimal play requires randomising, which breaks every algorithm that returns a single best move.
Fast Fourier transform XXVII §12
Cooley and Tukey's $O(n \log n)$ algorithm: split the sum into even and odd terms, each a transform of half the length, and combine in linear time. Figure 2 of Part XXVII measures a from-scratch implementation agreeing with the definition to 4.35×10−13 and pulling away from it without bound, and measures the gap between an asymptotic operation count and a wall clock.
Feature map X §2
The two-dimensional output produced by convolving one kernel across an entire input: a spatial map of where that feature was detected. A layer with $k$ kernels produces $k$ feature maps stacked into a three-dimensional output.
Feature pyramid XXIX §15
Detecting from several backbone depths at once, with a top-down path carrying deep semantics back to shallow high-resolution features. It exists because object area in a feature map falls as the square of the stride: Figure 4 of Part XXIX measures a 16 px object covering one feature pixel after four stride-2 stages, so small objects are not hard to detect at that depth; they are absent.
Feedback XXXI §3
Measuring the error and driving the input in proportion to it. The closed-loop response depends on the plant's gain only through $k_p g/(1+k_p g)$, which approaches one whatever the gain is, so feedback substitutes for knowledge, which is why a thermostat needs no thermodynamics. It buys robustness rather than accuracy: Figure 1 of Part XXXI measures a cautious gain giving 0.455 against open loop's 0.167, because the residual offset exceeds the model error it was meant to suppress.
Feedback loop XIV §28
A deployed system influences the data it will later be trained on: a recommender shapes what is clicked, so tomorrow's training set records its own choices. The result is a model that appears to improve while progressively narrowing what it can see. The only reliable defence is a randomised holdout: a small fraction of traffic served without the model's influence.
Feedforward network VII §5
A chain of affine maps and nonlinearities with no cycles. The base model family; every later architecture is a specialisation.
Fertility XX §3
Tokens per character. A tokenizer trained mostly on one language represents it efficiently and everything else in pieces, so the same content costs several times more in money, in context, and in dependency length. A property of the tokenizer's training corpus, not of the languages.
FID XVI §43
Fréchet inception distance: embed real and generated samples with a fixed network, fit a Gaussian to each, report the Fréchet distance. The field's default, and biased by sample count, dependent on the exact embedding network, and a single number over two opposite failure modes, which is the one thing it most needs to distinguish.
FlashAttention XII §29
Computing attention in tiles that fit in fast on-chip memory, keeping a running softmax, so the $n \times n$ score matrix is never written out. Memory falls from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n)$ and it computes the same function. It changes no asymptotics in compute and it is what made long context practical.
Floating point 0 §26
How computers approximate real numbers. Spacing grows with magnitude; too large gives inf, too small gives zero. The reason every numerical trick in this book exists.
Flow matching XVI §32
Training a velocity field along a chosen path from noise to data, usually the straight line. No Markov chain and no noise schedule; with particular choices it coincides with diffusion. Its value is that the path becomes an explicit design choice.
Focal loss XXIX §14
Down-weighting each example's loss by a factor that shrinks as the prediction becomes confident and correct. The change that closed the accuracy gap between one-stage and two-stage detectors, and it works because the problem was never that negatives were hard but that there were so many easy ones that their sum dominated. Figure 3 of Part XXIX measures the ratio at 672 : 1 at 512 px and 10,752 : 1 at 2,048 px.
Forget gate XI §18
The LSTM's conditional self-loop weight: a leaky unit's $\alpha$, computed by the network at every step. Systematic comparison found it the crucial component, and initialising its bias to 1 rather than 0 is worth more than most architecture variants.
Fourier features XXVII §36
Lifting a coordinate into a bank of sines and cosines before feeding it to a network, so that high-frequency functions are linear combinations of the inputs rather than something the network must synthesise. The standard remedy for spectral bias and the reason coordinate networks work for images and radiance fields. It does not remove the bias; it moves the cutoff into a hyperparameter you are now responsible for.
Frame XIII §2
The part of the population you were able to reach. Statistics addresses the gap between frame and sample; the gap between population and frame is a modelling assumption that no amount of data closes.
Frobenius norm I §14
The $L^2$ norm of a matrix, flattened. The standard way to measure a matrix's size or the distance between two matrices.
Frontier XXXIII §6
The generated-but-not-expanded nodes, ordered by whatever the algorithm sorts by. Usually the memory bottleneck, since it holds a whole level while the closed list holds only states: a distinction that vanishes in asymptotic notation and dominates in practice.
F-score XIV §3
The harmonic mean of precision and recall, $F_1 = 2pr/(p+r)$. Harmonic rather than arithmetic so that a degenerate classifier cannot score well: precision 1.0 with recall 0.02 gives $F_1$ near 0.04, where the arithmetic mean would report 0.51.
Fusion XXXII §9
Combining a chain of elementwise operations into one pass over the data, so it is read and written once rather than once per operation. Identical arithmetic, traffic reduced by roughly the chain length. Figure 2 of Part XXXII measures 7.52× on a chain of eight: bounded by the chain length, because you cannot make fewer than one pass.
Gated recurrent unit XI §21
An LSTM with one gate fewer: a single update gate controls forgetting and admitting together, and there is no output gate. Fewer parameters, usually the same accuracy, and no variant of either has been shown to beat both across a range of tasks.
Gaussian distribution II §15
The bell curve, parameterised by mean and variance. Ubiquitous for three separate reasons: the central limit theorem, maximum entropy, and mathematical convenience.
Gaussian process XXIV §8
A distribution over functions in which any finite set of values is jointly Gaussian, specified by a mean and a covariance function. Gives an exact posterior in closed form whose variance depends on where you observed rather than what you saw, so the interval widens away from the data without anyone specifying that.
Gelman–Rubin statistic XXVIII §31
Compares between-chain variance with within-chain variance to test whether chains agree. Necessary for convergence and not sufficient: Figure 6 of Part XXVIII measures R̂ = 1.0000 on chains that all sat in one of two equal modes and found 100% of their mass there, and 10.47 on the run that recovered the target correctly.
Generalisation gap VI §4
True risk minus training error: how much worse a model does on new data than on the data it trained on. Controlling it, rather than minimising training error, is the actual objective. A model with zero training error and a large generalisation gap has memorised rather than learnt.
Generalised advantage estimation XVIII §29
An exponentially weighted average over $n$-step advantages, controlled by $\lambda$. $\lambda = 0$ is the one-step advantage and $\lambda = 1$ the Monte Carlo one; it is TD($\lambda$) applied to advantages, computed in one backward pass.
Gini impurity XXV §3
A node purity measure, $1 - \sum_k p_k^2$, used to score classification splits. Interchangeable with entropy in practice; the choice between them matters far less than the depth.
Global average pooling X §15
Average each channel over all positions, giving one number per channel regardless of image size. Replaced the flatten-then-dense head of early architectures and removed most of their parameters.
Glorot initialisation IX §16
Weight variance $s^2 = 2/(m+n)$, the arithmetic-mean compromise between the scale that preserves forward activation variance ($1/m$, where $m$ is fan-in) and the scale that preserves backward gradient variance ($1/n$, where $n$ is fan-out). Both cannot hold simultaneously unless $m = n$; Glorot is wrong in both directions and by less than either specialist choice. For ReLU, multiply by 2 to account for the half of activations set to zero.
Goodhart's law XIV §33
Any measure used as a target stops measuring what it measured. Not cynicism about metrics but a structural consequence of optimising a proxy, and it recurs at every scale: an individual overfitting a validation set, a field overfitting a benchmark, a product optimising engagement as a stand-in for value. The defence is to keep one measurement you never optimise against.
grad_fn IV §5
The node recording which operation produced a tensor. It is the _backward closure of Part VII §21 wearing a class name, MulBackward0 and the rest.
Gradient 0 §23
$\nabla f$, the vector of all partial derivatives. Points in the direction of steepest ascent, perpendicular to the contours.
Gradient accumulation IV §13
Calling backward() over several small batches before one step(), to simulate a larger batch. The loss must be divided by the number of batches, since each is already a mean.
Gradient boosting XXV §17
Boosting where each term is fitted to the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the predictions: gradient descent with the function itself as the parameter. The tree is how a step defined at the training points generalises off them.
Gradient checking 0 drill C16
Verifying an analytic gradient against a numerical finite-difference estimate: compute $(f(x+h) - f(x-h))/(2h)$ for small $h$ and compare to the analytical value. The most reliable debugging technique for hand-written differentiation code, because a discrepancy pinpoints exactly which operation has a wrong derivative.
Gradient checkpointing VII §17
Storing only some forward activations and recomputing the rest during the backward pass. Trades roughly one extra forward pass for $O(\sqrt{L})$ memory instead of $O(L)$.
Gradient clipping I §15, IX §9
Rescaling the gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold. Preserves the direction exactly and bounds only the distance, which is the quantity a gradient never claimed to specify.
Gradient descent 0 §24
$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\nabla f(\theta)$. Go downhill, repeat. How every neural network is trained.
Gradient noise scale XVII §33
The ratio of a gradient's variance to its squared norm, estimable from two batch sizes during a real run. Approximates the [[critical-batch-size]].
Graph neural network XXVI §8
A network whose layer aggregates each node's neighbours and updates the node from the result. Almost every architecture in the family is that one loop with different choices of message, aggregator and update. Permutation-equivariant by construction, bounded above by colour refinement, and subject to over-smoothing with depth.
Greedy supervised pretraining IX §26
Training each layer as part of a shallow supervised model on the previous layer's output, then fine-tuning jointly. Not competing with joint optimisation; choosing where it starts.
Griffin–Lim XXX §7
Estimating a waveform from a magnitude spectrogram by alternately projecting onto the set of consistent spectrograms and the set of realisable signals. Figure 2 of Part XXX measures it converging monotonically in spectrogram error (0.666 to 0.048), while the waveform correlation dips before rising to only 0.53. It converges in spectrogram space and only incidentally in waveform space, which is what the characteristic metallic reconstruction is.
Group normalisation X §24
Normalising over spatial positions and a group of channels, so the statistics do not involve the batch. Exists because batch norm's estimates degrade like $1/\sqrt{N}$, and detection and segmentation run at $N=2$.
Grouped convolution X §19
Splitting the channels into $g$ groups, each output group seeing only its own input group. Divides the parameter count by exactly $g$. Introduced in AlexNet to fit two GPUs; rediscovered as a design axis, with depthwise as its limit.
Grouped-query attention XII §30
Several query heads sharing one key–value head, so the KV cache divides by the sharing factor. An architectural decision taken because of a deployment constraint.
Growth function XXIII §9
The largest number of distinct labellings a hypothesis class can realise on any $m$ points. Finite even for infinite classes, which is what lets the union-bound argument extend beyond finite hypothesis sets.
Guidance weight XVI §30
The scalar $w$ in classifier-free guidance. Adherence to the condition saturates early; typicality peaks and then collapses. A sample metric computed at $w \neq 1$ is not a measurement of the model.
Hadamard product I §6
Elementwise multiplication, $A \odot B$. A completely different operation from matrix multiplication, sharing only the word "product".
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo XXVIII §29
A sampler that adds a momentum variable and simulates the resulting dynamics, so proposals travel far while staying in high density. Figure 5 of Part XXVIII measures its advantage over random-walk Metropolis growing from 1.6× to 7.0× as the target's condition number rises.
Hessian 0 §19, I §18
The matrix of second derivatives. Its definiteness classifies critical points, which is the multivariable second derivative test.
Hessian-vector product VII §16
$H\mathbf{v} = \nabla(\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{v})$: the Hessian's action on one direction, for about two backward passes and never $n^2$ memory. The answer to Part III §12's objection that curvature is unaffordable.
Heterophily XXVI §39
The property of a graph whose edges tend to join nodes with different labels. Message passing assumes the opposite, so on a heterophilous graph the aggregation averages away the signal and a features-only model wins. One number, computed in a line, and it predicts whether the architecture suits the graph.
Hidden layer VII §2
A layer whose output is neither the input nor the prediction. It learns a representation in which the remaining problem is easier.
Hierarchical reconciliation XXV §42
Projecting independently produced forecasts onto the space of ones that add up across a hierarchy. Typically improves accuracy at every level rather than only fixing the arithmetic, because the levels' errors are informative about each other.
HiPPO XI §34
An initialisation for a state space model's transition matrix chosen so the state approximates a basis of the input history rather than decaying arbitrarily. Turns the vanishing-gradient problem into an explicit choice of where to put the timescales.
Histogram splitting XXV §4
Binning each feature into a fixed number of buckets before growing, so a split search is a cumulative sum over a bincount rather than a sort over rows. Figure 5 of Part XXV measures it evaluating 32 candidate thresholds where exact search evaluates 4,000, and improving accuracy up to a point, because fewer thresholds means fewer chances to fit noise.
Hoeffding's inequality XXIII §2
A bound on the probability that an average of bounded independent variables deviates from its mean, using only the range. Distribution-free, which is exactly why it gives the same answer for a fair coin and a one-in-twenty coin.
Homophily XXVI §27
The fraction of edges joining nodes with the same label. High homophily is what makes a random node split leak (a test node's neighbours are labelled training nodes), and it is why label propagation with no learning at all is a hard baseline to beat.
Hubness XV §26
In high dimensions, a few points appear in a great many nearest-neighbour lists while many appear in none, although the mean count is fixed by construction. The mechanism is that points nearer the centre of the cloud are nearer to everything. A property of the geometry, not of any model, and a cause of degenerate retrieval that is often misread as popularity bias.
Hybrid stack XI §37
Interleaving a small number of full-attention layers among mostly recurrent or linear-state ones. Cache and per-token cost scale with the attention layers rather than the depth, so a few of them recover most of the retrieval ability cheaply: capacity accounting rather than taste.
Hyperband XIV §17
Successive halving with its one free parameter hedged, by running several halving schedules of different starting widths. ASHA makes the same idea asynchronous, promoting a configuration as soon as it qualifies rather than at the end of a round.
Hyperparameter VI §9
A setting you choose rather than fit, such as capacity, learning rate, or $\lambda$. Chosen on a validation set, never on training or test data.
Hyperparameter transfer XXIII §52
Tuning on a small model and using the result on a large one. Valid under μP, where the optimal learning rate does not move with width; invalid under standard parameterisation, where it tracks $1/m$ and the small-scale answer is wrong by the width ratio. Part XVII §34 relies on this.
Hypothesis test V §11
Assume the null hypothesis is true, compute how surprising the observed data would be under that assumption, and reject the null if sufficiently surprising. Proof by contradiction with a probability threshold in place of logical certainty. The test's validity rests entirely on the null being a well-specified model, not on the alternative being plausible.
IDA* XXXIII §21
Iterative deepening with an $f$-cost limit instead of a depth limit. Memory linear in the depth, optimal with an admissible heuristic, and it re-expands everything each round. The right answer when nodes are cheap to regenerate and expensive to store; poor when costs are real-valued.
Identifiability IX §7
A model is identifiable if no two distinct parameter settings produce the same probability distribution over outputs, or equivalently, if a large enough dataset can rule out all but one setting. Neural networks are not identifiable: weight-space symmetry and non-linearity produce astronomically many equivalent parameter settings. This sounds alarming and is not, because the many minima are equivalent precisely because they represent the same function.
Identifiability (causal) XXII §14
Whether a causal quantity can be written as a function of the observational distribution given a graph (a different question from Part IX §7’s model identifiability, which is about parameters). Decidable by the do-calculus, and for some graphs the answer is provably no, at which point no estimator and no sample size helps and the problem is the design.
Identity matrix I §10
$I$, ones on the diagonal. The transformation that does nothing.
i.i.d. assumption VI §5
Examples drawn independently from one distribution. What makes learning from a sample possible, and routinely false.
im2col X §17
Gathering every patch the kernel will see into a matrix, turning the whole convolution into one matrix multiply. Four to thirteen times faster than looping, and the buffer holds every pixel $k^2$ times, measured at 8 to 18 times the input.
Implicit model XVI §34
A generative model defined by a sampling procedure with no density anywhere: a network mapping noise to samples. Gives samples and no way to score anything, including itself.
Implicit regularization XXIII §27
The bias an optimisation procedure introduces when the objective has many minimisers. Gradient descent from zero on underdetermined least squares converges to the minimum-norm solution (measured to fifteen digits in Figure 5 of Part XXIII), and on separable data with logistic loss it converges to the maximum-margin direction. Uncharacterised for deep networks.
Importance sampling XXVIII §26
Estimating an expectation under one distribution using samples from another, reweighted by the density ratio. Unbiased for any covering proposal, and frequently useless: in high dimensions mismatch compounds per dimension and the effective sample size collapses, so unbiasedness is not the property to check.
Importance-weighted bound XVI §17
$\log \frac{1}{k}\sum_i p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}^{(i)})/q(\mathbf{z}^{(i)}\mid\mathbf{x})$ with $\mathbf{z}^{(i)} \sim q$. Also a lower bound, and it tightens as $k$ grows. If a likelihood is to be reported for a latent variable model, this is the one to report, with $k$ stated.
In-context learning XII §38 · XX §17
Performing a task from examples in the prompt with no weight changing. Not a separate capability: predicting the later parts of a structured document requires inferring the pattern from its earlier parts, so next-token prediction selected for it. Established by permuting the demonstrations' labels on a mapping the model cannot have memorised.
Independence II §9
$P(x,y) = P(x)P(y)$ for every pair. Strictly stronger than zero covariance.
Induction head XII §37
A two-layer circuit implementing find the earlier occurrence of the current token and copy what followed it. Layer 1 writes "what preceded me" into each position; layer 2 queries on that. The best-understood circuit in any transformer, and it needs the composition, as one layer cannot do it.
Inductive and transductive XXVI §26
Whether the test nodes were absent from the training graph or merely unlabelled in it. Different problems; the transductive one is easier; and reporting a transductive number for a system that will face new nodes is the most common overstatement in graph learning. Any method needing the full graph at training time is transductive by construction.
Infinitely strong prior X §11
A prior placing zero probability on some parameters, forbidding them whatever the data says. A convolutional layer is a dense layer under one: weights must be shared across positions and zero outside a small receptive field. It is why architecture and regularisation are the same kind of thing.
InfoNCE XV §17
The standard contrastive loss: softmax cross-entropy over a batch where the correct class is the other view of the same example. Its denominator is the term that pushes different examples apart, without which the objective's global optimum is to map every input to the same vector.
Information bottleneck XV §45
Finding a representation $Z$ minimising $I(X;Z) - \beta I(Z;Y)$: keep as few bits about the input as possible while retaining as many as possible about the label. States what a representation is for, and separates compressing for prediction from compressing for reconstruction: a direction can hold most of the variance and none of the signal. Its use as an explanation of training dynamics is contested, because mutual information in a deterministic network is not well defined without noise or binning.
Initialisation VII §11
Starting weights drawn from a distribution with variance $\sqrt{2/n_{\text{in}}}$ so that activation variance neither vanishes nor explodes through depth. Zero is not permitted: identical weights produce identical gradients and the network never breaks symmetry to learn different features.
Instruction tuning XX §25
Fine-tuning on instruction–response pairs so that a request is followed by a compliant answer. Takes thousands of examples against trillions of pretraining tokens, which is the evidence that it selects an interface to existing capability rather than teaching one.
Instrumental variable XXII §26
A variable that moves the treatment, is unconfounded with the outcome, and affects the outcome only through the treatment. Recovers an effect with the confounder never measured; only the first condition is testable, and it estimates the effect on compliers rather than the population.
Intersection over union XXIX §4
The overlap of two regions divided by their union. Scale-invariant, bounded, and zero for any disjoint pair, which makes it acceptable as a metric and poor as a loss, since it has no gradient exactly where a detector starts. The generalised variants add a term that keeps pulling in that case, which is the same defect and the same repair as Part XXVIII §41's transport distance replacing a divergence that is infinite on disjoint supports.
Invariance X §9 · XXVI §2
The output does not change when the input is transformed. An architecture is a declaration of which group it is invariant to: convolution declares translation, message passing declares permutation, a molecular network declares both permutation and rigid motion. Figure 1 of Part XXVI measures the built-in version at 1.000 on relabelled data against 0.951 for augmentation.
Inverse kinematics XXXI §26
Solving for the joint angles that place an end effector at a desired pose. Unlike forward kinematics it may have no solution, one, several or infinitely many. Near a singularity the Jacobian loses rank and a small commanded motion demands an enormous joint velocity, which is why production code uses a damped pseudo-inverse rather than a true one.
Inverse-probability weighting XXII §19
Weighting each unit by the reciprocal of its probability of the treatment it received, reconstructing the population that would have existed under random assignment. Efficient and fragile: variance rises sharply as overlap degrades.
Irreducible error V §20
The noise variance $\sigma^2$ in the data itself. A property of the world; no model, however good, removes it.
Isotonic regression XXV §26
A non-decreasing step function fitted by pool-adjacent-violators, used to repair probabilities. Because it is monotone it changes no ranking (measured at 0.0001 of AUC in Figure 6 of Part XXV), so it can be applied to a deployed ranker safely.
Iterative deepening XXXIII §4
Repeated depth-first search to depth 1, 2, 3, … Breadth-first's completeness with depth-first's memory, at an overhead of $b/(b-1)$: about 11% at branching 10. Repeating work to save memory is a good trade far more often than it sounds.
Jaccard index XIII §15
$|A \cap B| / |A \cup B|$ over the shingle sets of two documents. The standard measure of near-duplication, and what MinHash estimates.
Jacobian 0 §22
The matrix of all partial derivatives of a vector-valued function: the gradient's generalisation when the output has several components.
Jensen–Shannon divergence XVI §34
$\tfrac12 D_{KL}(p\|m) + \tfrac12 D_{KL}(q\|m)$ with $m$ the average. Symmetric and bounded by $\log 2$. Substituting the optimal discriminator into the GAN objective yields it, up to a constant, so the discriminator is estimating a divergence the loss cannot compute.
Kalman filter XXVIII §13
Exact inference in a linear-Gaussian state space model, as a recursion on a mean and a covariance. Not an approximation: Figure 2 of Part XXVIII measures it against the analytic posterior at 10−11. The Kalman gain is what conditioning produces, not a tuning parameter.
Kernel trick VI §15
Computing inner products in a high-dimensional feature space without constructing the coordinates. Makes linear methods nonlinear, at a cost quadratic in dataset size.
KKT conditions III §19
Stationarity, feasibility, multiplier non-negativity, and complementary slackness: the requirements at a solution of an inequality-constrained problem.
KL divergence II §24
$D_{KL}(P \parallel Q)$, the extra bits paid on average for assuming $Q$ when the true distribution is $P$. Non-negative, zero only when $P = Q$ almost everywhere, and asymmetric: $D_{KL}(P\|Q) \neq D_{KL}(Q\|P)$ in general, which is why it is not a distance. Minimising it over $Q$ is equivalent to maximum likelihood.
k-means VI §18
Alternate assigning points to nearest centres and moving centres to cluster means. Always returns $k$ clusters, whether or not any exist.
k-nearest neighbours VI §16
Predict by voting among the $k$ closest training points. No training; $k$ is the capacity dial, running backwards.
Knowledge editing XX §51
Changing one stored association directly in the weights, typically by a rank-one update to a layer read as a key–value store. A single edit is exact. The cost is an exchange rate rather than a budget: each edit damages the store by roughly one fact's worth, so it survives about as many edits as it holds facts, far below its dimensional capacity, and the arithmetic case for putting changeable knowledge in retrieval.
Knuth–Moore count XXXIII §36
The leaves alpha–beta evaluates under perfect move ordering: $b^{\lceil d/2\rceil}+b^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}-1$. Roughly twice the square root of the full tree, so ordering is worth a doubling of search depth: more than any evaluation-function improvement has ever been worth.
KV cache XII §28 · XIX §4
Stored keys and values for every previous position, so attention need not recompute them. Its size is linear in both context and batch, so it, not the weights, limits how many sequences fit. It equals the weights at a few tens of thousands of tokens, and grouped-query attention exists mostly to divide it.
Label shift XIV §26 · XXIII §41
The class frequencies $p(y)$ change while $p(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$ does not: the classes look the same, they simply arrive in different proportions. Correctable by reweighting if the new proportions can be estimated. The cheapest shift to correct: the fix is a vector of class-prior ratios, estimable from unlabelled target data by inverting a confusion matrix.
Label smoothing VIII §12
Replacing a hard one-hot target with a soft distribution: the correct class gets $1-\varepsilon$ and the rest share $\varepsilon$. Exists because cross-entropy with a hard target drives the corresponding logit to $+\infty$ chasing a probability it can only approach. Smoothing removes the incentive, regularises the output distribution, and improves calibration.
Lagrange multiplier III §19
The scalar relating the objective's gradient to a constraint's gradient at a constrained optimum, where the two are parallel.
Langevin dynamics XVI §24
Sampling by following the score uphill with injected noise. Requires a score that is accurate where the walk goes, which plain score matching does not provide away from the data, and even with an exact score it recovers separated modes with the wrong relative weights.
Laplace approximation XXIV §18
A Gaussian fitted at the mode of the posterior with covariance the inverse Hessian, so curvature becomes uncertainty. Attractive because it is post hoc (one Hessian approximation on an already-trained model), and limited by which Hessian approximation is affordable.
Latent variable II §18
A variable that participates in generating the data but is never observed. The hidden cause behind a mixture, and the core idea behind autoencoders and generative models.
Layer normalisation IX §23
Normalising across the feature dimension for each example independently, so the statistics depend on one example and not on the batch. Consistent behaviour at batch size 1 makes it the natural choice for transformers, which typically process one sequence at a time during inference and cannot rely on stable batch statistics.
Lazy regime XXIII §49
Training in which the parameters move so little that the network is well approximated by its linearisation, so it behaves as kernel regression with features fixed at initialisation. Reached by scaling the output up, or implicitly by width. It fits the training data as well as the rich regime and does so without finding the structure the data depends on.
Leaf tensor IV §5
A tracked tensor created directly rather than computed. Parameters are leaves; activations are not. Only leaves receive a .grad, because you never update an activation.
Leakage (spectral) XXVII §21
Energy spread across every frequency bin by the discontinuity between the ends of a record, which the transform's periodicity assumption turns into a real feature of the signal. A tone that does not complete a whole number of cycles shows as a peak with skirts, and the skirts can bury a genuine weak component. Repaired by windowing.
Leaky unit XI §17
A hidden unit with a linear self-connection, $\mu \leftarrow \alpha\mu + (1-\alpha)v$. Remembers about $1/(1-\alpha)$ steps. The same geometric series as momentum, RMSProp, batch norm's running statistics and Polyak averaging.
Learning curve XIV §14
Test error against training-set size, plotted on log–log axes with points doubling rather than increasing linearly. Approximately a power law over a useful range, which converts "should we collect more data" into an estimate. The extrapolation is a claim about a regime you have not measured and can be wrong in either direction.
Learning rate 0 §24
$\alpha$, the step size in gradient descent. Too small crawls, too large diverges. The single most consequential hyperparameter.
Likelihood II §21, §26
$P(\text{data} \mid \text{parameters})$: how probable the observed data would be under a given hypothesis. Maximising it is the organising principle of model fitting.
Limit 0 §14
What a function approaches as its input approaches some value: a statement about the journey, not the destination, saying nothing about the value at that point. The derivative is defined as a limit of a ratio, which is why it describes instantaneous rate of change without requiring the denominator to actually reach zero.
Line search III §13
Choosing the step size at each iteration by trying rather than fixing it in advance. Standard in classical optimisation, rare in deep learning because noisy objectives defeat it.
Linear attention XII §32 · XI §31
Replacing the softmax with a kernel so the product can be reassociated as $\phi(Q)(\phi(K)^\top V)$, giving $O(nd^2)$. The middle factor is a fixed-size state updated additively, so it is a recurrent network, and Part XI §28's bottleneck returns. What the substitution costs is measurable: it cannot concentrate weight on one position, the gap behind softmax widens with context length, and raising the dimension does not close it: the limitation is the missing exponential rather than the resolution.
Linear independence I §11
No vector in a set is a combination of the others. Each contributes reach the rest cannot supply.
Linear mode connectivity XX §49
Two trained models being joined by a straight path in weight space along which performance does not drop. It holds between models that share an ancestor and fails between independently initialised ones, which is the precondition for averaging weights at all. The barrier shrinks with width, so a negative merging result from a small model does not transfer upward.
Linear probe XV §31
A linear classifier fitted on a frozen representation to test whether a property is encoded. The restriction to linear is the point: it establishes the property is linearly available, which is what a downstream model can actually use. Its accuracy means nothing without a [[control-task]].
Linear-quadratic regulator XXXI §10
The optimal controller for linear dynamics under quadratic cost, obtained by iterating a Riccati recursion. Optimal by construction, so nothing tuned can beat it: Figure 3 of Part XXXI measures a hand-tuned PD within 0.1% on a double integrator, 78× worse on a triple, and unable to stabilise five at all. It is the dual of the Kalman filter, which is the same recursion for estimation.
Local average treatment effect XXII §26
The estimand two-stage least squares returns with heterogeneous effects: the effect among units whose treatment the instrument actually changed. Not identifiable individually, and often different from the population average.
Local search XXXIII §15
Holding one complete candidate and moving to a neighbour, with no frontier and no path. Constant memory, and its failure modes (local optima, plateaus, ridges) are where those shapes were first named, before gradient methods met the same ones with derivatives available.
Locality-sensitive hashing XIII §15
Splitting MinHash signatures into bands and comparing only documents that collide in some band, which removes the $O(n^2)$ pairwise comparison. The band structure is an explicit precision–recall knob; the quoted threshold $(1/b)^{1/r}$ approximates the steepest point of the curve, not its half-height.
Locally connected layer X §8
Convolution's connectivity without its sharing: a separate weight at every position. The middle of a spectrum whose other end is convolution, and useful when features should be local but not the same everywhere.
Logarithm 0 §10
An exponent, retrieved. Its essential property is turning multiplication into addition, which is why every likelihood is wrapped in one.
Log-sum-exp 0 §26, II §20
A numerically stable way to compute $\log\sum e^{z}$ without overflowing. The denominator of every softmax.
Long short-term memory XI §18
A recurrent cell whose state has a self-loop with a gated weight, so the time constant of integration is an output of the model rather than a hyperparameter. The gradient along the self-loop is a product of numbers the network chooses.
LoRA XIX §32
Freezing $W_0$ and learning the update as a product of two thin matrices, $W = W_0 + BA$ with $B$ initialised at zero. Trains a fraction of a per cent of the parameters, merges into the base for zero inference cost, and (kept unmerged) lets one resident model serve many adaptations in a single batch.
Loss scaling XVII §19
Multiplying the loss by a large constant before the backward pass so that small gradients land inside fp16's representable range, then dividing it out before the update. Unnecessary in bf16, which keeps fp32's exponent field and therefore its range.
$L^p$ norm I §14
A measure of size. $L^2$ is ordinary length; $L^1$ is the sum of magnitudes and produces sparsity; $L^\infty$ is the largest entry.
Machine epsilon III §1
The gap between 1 and the next representable float; $2^{-52} \approx 2.2\times10^{-16}$ in float64. Anything smaller, added to 1, vanishes.
Manifold hypothesis VI §23
Real high-dimensional data (images, audio, text) concentrates near a low-dimensional curved surface (a manifold) embedded in its ambient space. The reason learning in high dimensions is merely hard rather than impossible: the effective dimension of the problem is the dimension of the manifold, not the dimension of the raw data.
MAP estimation V §17
Maximising the posterior rather than the likelihood, i.e., likelihood times prior. The prior enters the objective exactly where a regularisation penalty sits.
Marginal coverage XXIV §26
Coverage averaged over inputs as well as outcomes: what conformal prediction guarantees. Consistent with covering 99% of easy inputs and 60% of hard ones, which is the worst-group problem arriving inside a method that looks like it has a guarantee. Exact conditional coverage is impossible without assumptions.
Marginal likelihood XXIV §11
The probability of the data with the parameters integrated out. Used to select kernel hyperparameters without a validation set: its log-determinant term penalises a model that could have explained many datasets, which is Occam's razor arriving as a determinant.
Marginalisation II §6
Summing or integrating a joint distribution over a variable to remove it. Deliberate forgetting, done so the total still sums to 1.
Markov decision process XVIII §2
States, actions, a transition kernel, a reward function and a discount. The Markov property is a claim about the state representation rather than about the world: satisfied by construction if the state contains everything relevant, and otherwise relocated into the problem of building such a state.
Masked modelling XV §22
Hiding part of the input and training the model to reconstruct it: a denoising autoencoder with masking as the corruption. The mask ratio measures the data's redundancy: about 15% for text, about 75% for images, because a patch is well predicted by its neighbours and a token is not.
Matrix I §2
A rectangular grid of numbers, indexed row first. Readable as data, as a set of column ingredients, or as a transformation.
Maximal update parameterisation XXIII §50
μP: output layer initialised at $1/m$, output learning rate falling like $1/m$, and input learning rate growing like $m$. Chosen so activations and their updates stay $\Theta(1)$ in width, which is what makes a hyperparameter tuned at small width correct at large width. All three parts are needed: omitting the input-layer scaling makes it lazier than NTK parameterisation.
Maximisation bias XVIII §19
$\mathbb{E}[\max_a \hat{Q}(a)] \geq \max_a \mathbb{E}[\hat{Q}(a)]$, so Q-learning's target is systematically optimistic and the optimism propagates through bootstrapping. Measured at +0.73 with 32 actions whose true values are all zero. [[double-q-learning]] removes it.
Maximum likelihood II §26
Choosing parameters that make the observed data most probable. Identical to minimising cross-entropy and to minimising KL divergence.
Mean-field approximation XXVIII §19
A variational family in which the latent variables are independent. Cheap, standard, and wrong in a specific direction: for a Gaussian posterior with correlation $\rho$ the fitted marginal standard deviation is exactly $\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$, so the reported uncertainty at $\rho = 0.9$ is 44% of the truth. A property of the objective, not of the optimiser.
Mediator XXII §5
A variable on the causal path from treatment to outcome. Adjusting for it removes the part of the effect travelling through it, leaving the direct rather than the total effect: right for some questions and a silent error for most.
Mel scale XXX §4
A frequency warping spacing bands uniformly in perceived pitch, roughly logarithmic above 500 Hz. Pooling a spectrum into mel bands asserts that frequency resolution should degrade with frequency the way human hearing's does: right for speech intended for people, wrong for machine, animal or radio signals. Figure 1 of Part XXX measures the highest band pooling 14× as many FFT bins as the lowest, with a 200 Hz separation falling inside a single band above about 4,560 Hz.
Message passing XXVI §8
The loop at the centre of graph learning: form a message on each edge, aggregate a node's incoming messages with a permutation-invariant operator, update the node from its own state and the aggregate. Sum is injective on multisets and mean and max are not, which makes that choice an expressivity question.
MinHash XIII §15
A signature of $k$ minima under $k$ random permutations, whose match rate estimates the Jaccard index, because the minimum over $A \cup B$ lands in the intersection exactly $J$ of the time. Error falls like $1/\sqrt{k}$ and does not depend on corpus size.
Minibatch III §14
A random sample used to estimate the gradient. Unbiased, with noise falling as $1/\sqrt{b}$ against cost rising as $b$.
Minimax XXXIII §34
The value of a position under optimal play by both sides: maximise on your move, minimise on theirs. Costs $b^d$, so the entire subject is buying depth: by cutting off with an evaluation function, and by proving branches cannot matter.
Minimum description length XV §44
Choosing the model that minimises bits for the model plus bits for the residuals under it. A more complex model costs more to send and leaves less to explain, so the total has an interior minimum, reached without any held-out data. It is not parameter-free: the choice of parameter code is a prior, moved somewhere interpretable.
Mixture II §18
Pick a component at random, then sample from it. Buys multimodality cheaply; the component index is a latent variable.
Mixture of experts XII §33 · XVII §30
Replacing a feed-forward block with many copies and a router that sends each token to a few of them. Multiplies parameters without multiplying compute per token. Costs: memory does not shrink, an all-to-all per layer, load imbalance as a runtime tax, and harder fine-tuning.
Modality gap XV §36 · XXX §35
In a two-tower multimodal model, the two modalities occupy separate regions with a measurable distance between their centroids, despite training that aligns matched pairs. The objective constrains only relative similarities, so translating one modality's embeddings changes nothing it measures. Cross-modal and within-modal similarities are therefore not comparable.
Mode collapse XVI §36
A generative model producing only part of the distribution. Every sample is genuine, so the failure is invisible in any individual sample and visible only in the distribution. Measured here at 1 of 8 modes, and caused by the balance between generator and discriminator rather than by the loss form, which is the standard explanation.
Mode covering and mode seeking XVI §4
The two behaviours of the two KL directions. Forward KL, which maximum likelihood minimises, is infinite where the data has mass and the model has none, so the model spreads: blur. Reverse KL is infinite the other way, so the model may abandon regions: collapse. Knowing which a method optimises predicts how it will fail.
Model collapse XIII §28
Degradation from training on generated data. Naive resampling barely causes it, measured at 0.99 of the original spread after twelve generations. What does: filtering the outputs for quality, which resamples the middle every time, and model mismatch, which destroys structure at the first generation while the variance is unchanged.
Model extraction XXXII §36
Cloning a model through its query interface. Figure 8 of Part XXXII measures 95% agreement in 40 queries against a victim with 30 parameters that had been fitted on thousands of examples: the cost scales with what is being recovered, not with the victim's training set. Restricting the output to a label raises it 16× and reaches 97% anyway, so query access is disclosure at a price rather than a door that can be closed.
Model soup XX §49
Averaging the weights of several fine-tunes of the same pretrained model. Sound because they share an ancestor and are therefore linearly connected; averaging across pretraining runs is not the same operation and destroys both models.
Model-as-judge XIV §24
Grading open-ended output with a language model. Cheap and correlated with human preference, and carrying measurable biases: position, verbosity, self-preference, and a tendency to reward surface form. Usable only once its agreement with your own labels has been measured on your own task, which converts it from an oracle into an instrument with a known error rate.
Model-predictive control XXXI §14
Optimising over a horizon subject to constraints, applying only the first input, and re-solving at the next instant. The re-solving is where the feedback is: fix the plan and it becomes open-loop planning. It exists because a constraint cannot be added to an optimal controller by clipping its output: the controller keeps planning as though the limit were absent, so it never chooses the differently shaped trajectory that stays inside.
Module IV §8
An object that owns tensors and knows which are learned. Attribute assignment is intercepted and registers submodules, which is what makes parameters() (and therefore the optimiser) possible.
Momentum III §15
Accumulating a velocity across gradient descent steps, which damps the zigzag caused by unequal curvature.
Monte Carlo II project
Estimating a quantity by averaging over random samples. The key property is that error falls as $1/\sqrt{n}$ regardless of the dimension of the integration domain, in stark contrast to deterministic grid methods, whose cost grows exponentially with dimension. This is why Monte Carlo survives where everything else does not.
Monte Carlo tree search XXXIII §31
Selection, expansion, simulation, backpropagation, repeated until the budget runs out. Grows the tree asymmetrically, needs no evaluation function, and is anytime. Applying a bandit rule at every node (UCT) is what turns the Monte Carlo average into a search.
Move ordering XXXIII §36
The order children are tried in a pruning search. Worth more than the pruning rule itself, and most of its value is available to cheap approximations (the best move from a shallower search, moves that caused a cutoff elsewhere), because the curve between random and perfect ordering is steep at the random end.
Multi-head attention XII §12
Splitting the model dimension into $h$ independent attention operations and mixing the results with $W^O$. The parameter count is unchanged; heads cost only reshaping.
Multiple comparisons V §14
Running many statistical tests inflates the chance of at least one false positive. With $k$ tests each at level $\alpha$, the family-wise false-positive rate approaches $1-(1-\alpha)^k$, which reaches 0.4 at twenty tests. Hyperparameter search is the most common instance in machine learning, and is almost never corrected for, which is one reason reported results are routinely optimistic.
Multi-task learning VIII §16
One model, several tasks, a shared trunk. Each task's data constrains the shared parameters, so the representation is pushed toward factors that underlie all of them. Fails as negative transfer when the tasks do not in fact share structure.
Mutual information II drill S1
$I(X;Y) = D_{KL}(P(X,Y)\|P(X)P(Y))$: how far the joint distribution is from the product of its marginals. Unlike covariance, which detects only linear association, mutual information is zero if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are truly independent. Hard to estimate in high dimensions.
Nash equilibrium XXXIII §37
A strategy profile in which no player gains by deviating alone. Always exists in mixed strategies. Unique in value for two-player zero-sum games, which is why that case feels like optimisation; in general-sum games equilibria multiply and the best outcome for everybody need not be one.
Negative transfer VIII §16
Multi-task learning making every task worse at once, because the shared trunk is pulled in conflicting directions. The uniformity across tasks is the diagnostic.
Nesterov momentum IX §15
Evaluating the gradient at $\boldsymbol{\theta} + \alpha\mathbf{v}$ rather than at $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, looking where you are going. Improves the convex batch rate from $O(1/k)$ to $O(1/k^2)$ and does not improve the stochastic rate at all.
Neural codec XXX §22
An autoencoder with a residual vector quantiser in the middle, trained with reconstruction, adversarial and commitment terms, producing a sequence of codebook indices at perhaps 75 per second. Those indices are tokens, so a language model can be trained on audio with no audio-specific architecture. The stages are coarse-to-fine, so capacity is best spent on the first.
Neural tangent kernel XXIII §47
The Gram matrix of the network's Jacobian with respect to its parameters at initialisation. In the infinite-width limit under NTK parameterisation it is deterministic and constant during training, so gradient descent on the network becomes kernel regression. At any width anyone trains it is neither: it moves by several per cent and it moves toward the data.
Newton's method III §11
$\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - H^{-1}\nabla f$. Jumps to the minimum of the local quadratic. Conditioning-independent, and unusable at scale.
No free lunch VI §7
Averaged over all possible target functions, every learner performs identically, at chance. No algorithm has a general advantage. The theorem does not say inductive bias is worthless; it says that any bias that helps on one class of problems hurts equally on the complementary class. The useful version of the claim is that every learning algorithm is a set of assumptions, and the right question is which assumptions fit this problem.
no_grad IV §7
A block inside which no graph is recorded. Used for evaluation, where the memory spent saving activations for a backward pass that will never happen is pure waste.
Noise floor XIV §19
The seed-to-seed spread of a fixed configuration. Any reported difference smaller than it is not a result. Measuring it costs five runs of the baseline, and it is the number that determines the smallest improvement an experimental setup can honestly detect.
Nonconformity score XXIV §24
A measure of how unusual an observation is under a fitted model, typically the absolute residual. Conformal calibrates a quantile of these on held-out data; normalising by a predicted difficulty is what makes the resulting interval adapt in width.
Non-maximum suppression XXIX §12
Deleting lower-scoring detections that overlap a kept one, to remove duplicates. Greedy, non-differentiable, not part of the model, usually untuned, and it decides the output: Figure 2 of Part XXIX measures crowded recall at 0.328 at a threshold of 0.3 and 1.000 at 0.7, with precision moving the other way. It cannot distinguish a duplicate from two genuinely overlapping objects, because overlap is the only evidence it uses.
No-regret XXIII §31
An online algorithm whose regret grows sublinearly in the horizon, so its average loss approaches that of the best fixed decision. The guarantee needs no distributional assumption at all, and the comparator being a fixed decision is what makes that possible.
Norm I §14
Any function measuring size that is zero only at zero, scales absolutely, and obeys the triangle inequality.
Normal equations III §20
$A^\top A\mathbf{x} = A^\top\mathbf{b}$. The textbook least squares formula, and the numerically worst way to compute it: forming $A^\top A$ squares the conditioning.
Normalising constant XVI §2
$Z(\theta) = \int \tilde{p}(\mathbf{x};\theta)\,d\mathbf{x}$, the integral that turns a positive function into a distribution. Intractable in high dimensions and dependent on $\theta$, so it changes every gradient step. Every family of generative model is a different answer to what to do about it.
NTK parameterisation XXIII §50
Unit-variance initialisation with an explicit $1/\sqrt{m}$ in the forward pass, constructed so the tangent kernel has a finite limit. It achieves stability by letting the features stop moving, at the same rate standard parameterisation does: stable and lazy, where μP is stable and still learning.
Nyquist frequency XXVII §2
Half the sampling rate: the highest frequency a given rate can represent. Below it, sampling loses nothing and the continuous signal is exactly recoverable; above it, content aliases. Named alongside Shannon in the sampling theorem.
Occupancy XXXII §6
How many threads an accelerator has in flight to hide memory latency. A means rather than an end: a kernel at low occupancy that already saturates memory is finished, and raising occupancy usually costs registers, which can push values back to memory. Optimise the time and read occupancy only to explain it.
Off-policy XVIII §18
Learning about one policy from data generated by another. Enables replay, learning from demonstrations, and reuse of old data, and is one leg of the [[deadly-triad]]. Q-learning is off-policy; SARSA is not, and the two targets differ by one symbol.
Off-policy evaluation XXII §32
Estimating what a policy would have earned from logs generated by a different one, by reweighting with the ratio of action probabilities. Requires the logging policy's probabilities to have been recorded, which cannot be reconstructed afterwards.
Operational design domain XXXI §41
The conditions under which a safety claim applies: where, when, in what weather, at what speeds. A claim without one is not falsifiable, and it is the first of the three things (domain, failure mode, rate with confidence) that turn "safer than a human" into a statement.
Optimal transport XXVIII §41
The cost of the cheapest plan for moving one distribution onto another; the Wasserstein distances are its values. Unlike a density-ratio divergence it uses the geometry of the space, so it stays finite and informative on disjoint supports where KL is infinite: Figure 8 of Part XXVIII measures $W_1$ growing linearly with slope 1.0000 exactly where KL supplies no gradient at all.
Orthogonal matrix I §16
Orthonormal columns, so $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$. A rotation or reflection: preserves all lengths and angles. The inverse is free.
Out-of-bag estimate XXV §13
Scoring each row with the bagged trees whose bootstrap sample omitted it, about 37% of them. A held-out estimate for free, and invalid whenever the rows are not exchangeable, which includes every series with a time index.
Overflow / underflow III §2
Exceeding the largest representable value, giving inf; or falling below the smallest, giving exactly zero. Both lead to nan through a later operation.
Over-smoothing XXVI §13
The convergence of node representations toward each other under repeated aggregation. Mean aggregation is row-stochastic, so its repeated application is a random walk converging to a distribution independent of where it started; Figure 2 of Part XXVI measures mean pairwise distance falling from 1.351 to 0.000 with accuracy following it down. Under the symmetric normalisation the rows converge to a common direction scaled by the square root of degree instead: the same loss of information, stated exactly.
Over-squashing XXVI §20
The dilution of distant information as an exponentially growing receptive field is compressed into a fixed-width vector. Figure 4 of Part XXVI measures a source's share of a target's representation falling by a factor of 6,561 along a path. The opposite constraint from over-smoothing, and together they are why long-range dependence on graphs is hard.
μP XVII §34
A parameterisation whose initialisation, learning-rate and multiplier scalings make the optimal learning rate independent of width, so it can be tuned on a small model and transferred to a much larger one. Follows from requiring activations and updates to stay the same order of magnitude as width grows.
PAC learning XXIII §8
Probably approximately correct: an algorithm that, from enough samples, returns a hypothesis within $\varepsilon$ of the best in its class with probability $1-\delta$, for every distribution. The universal quantifier over distributions is what makes the resulting bounds pessimistic.
Padding X §5
Implicit zeros around the input. Valid adds none and shrinks by $k-1$ per layer; same preserves the size and under-represents the border; full visits every pixel $k$ times. The best setting is usually between the first two.
Paged attention XIX §39
Storing the KV cache in fixed-size blocks with an index rather than contiguously, so a sequence reserves what it uses instead of its maximum possible length. Waste falls from the great majority to a few per cent, and shared prefixes become shared blocks.
Panoptic segmentation XXIX §19
Assigning every pixel exactly once, with countable things receiving identities and uncountable stuff receiving classes. The distinction from semantic segmentation is operational: a planner needs to know there are two cars rather than one car-shaped region, and a semantic model cannot tell it.
Parameter sharing VIII §15
Forcing weights to be identical rather than merely similar. Removes parameters outright, and encodes a real invariance: it is what convolution and recurrence are.
Partial derivative 0 §22
The derivative with respect to one variable, holding the others fixed. The slope of a slice.
Pass@k XXXIII §39
The fraction of problems where at least one of $k$ samples is correct. Not a method (there is no selection rule, it needs an oracle) but the ceiling any selector could reach, and the correct upper bound to plot beside a measured rate. Reporting it as a capability number is the standard measurement error here.
Pattern database XXXIII §13
Project a state onto a subset of its features, enumerate that smaller space exhaustively backwards from the goal, and store exact distances. Lookup is constant time and the values are admissible. Two such databases may be added only if no single action contributes to both.
PCA I §23
Principal components analysis: project data onto the directions of greatest variance, which are the top eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.
PDF / PMF II §4–5
Probability density function (continuous variables) and probability mass function (discrete variables). A density assigns probability per unit length; it may exceed 1 at a point, but its integral over any interval is capped at 1. A mass function assigns probability directly to each outcome; values are always in $[0,1]$.
Permutation importance XXV §14
The increase in error when one column is shuffled. Unbiased with respect to cardinality, unlike impurity importance, and it reports two correlated features carrying the same signal as both unimportant. Neither measure answers a causal question.
Permutation invariance XXVI §2
The requirement that relabelling a set's or a graph's elements leaves the answer unchanged. Not learnable by augmentation at any realistic scale (a 30-node graph has 2.7 × 1032 orderings), which is the argument for building it into the architecture instead.
Phase XXVII §18
The argument of a Fourier coefficient: where a frequency's crests fall, as against how much of it is present. An edge is many frequencies whose crests align, so phase carries the structure: Figure 4 of Part XXVII measures a reconstruction correlating +0.736 with its phase donor and −0.125 with its magnitude donor. Any method keeping magnitude and discarding phase, including a mel spectrogram, has discarded the content.
Pinball loss XXV §41
The loss minimised by a given quantile, and therefore the proper scoring rule for a quantile forecast. Reporting only an error on the median discards the part of a forecast a decision actually uses.
Pipeline bubble XVII §24
The idle fraction of pipeline-parallel time, exactly $(P-1)/(M+P-1)$ for $P$ stages and $M$ microbatches. Falls with more microbatches and never reaches zero, and every microbatch in flight holds its own activations, so the cure competes with memory.
Pointwise convolution X §19
A $1\times1$ convolution: a dense layer applied independently at every position, mixing channels and not space. The channel-mixing operation, and the reason Inception's reductions, ResNet's bottlenecks and separable convolution all look alike.
Policy gradient theorem XVIII §25
$\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}[\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) G_t]$. Derived from $\nabla p = p \nabla \log p$. No derivative of the environment appears, so no model is needed, which is why this estimator is used here despite the variance that made Part XVI set it aside.
Polyak averaging IX §25
Averaging the points the optimiser visited rather than taking the last one. The plain mean is right for convex stationary problems; non-convex training uses an exponentially decaying average, which discards regions the optimiser has left.
Polysemanticity XV §33
A single unit responding to several unrelated things. Expected rather than anomalous: if a layer stores more features than it has dimensions, features must share directions, and a neuron is a projection of several. See [[superposition]].
Pooling X §12
Replacing a neighbourhood by a summary, usually its maximum. Buys approximate invariance to translation, with a range set by the window, and downsamples. Largely displaced by strided convolution except at the very end of a network.
Position effect XX §12
Information at the beginning and end of a long context is used well and information in the middle worse. Means an advertised context window is not long-context ability, and the measurement that separates them is a retrieval probe at controlled depths.
Positive definite I §18
$\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x} > 0$ for all non-zero $\mathbf{x}$; equivalently all eigenvalues positive. The quadratic form is a bowl, so the critical point is a minimum.
Positivity XXII §20
That every unit has a genuine chance of either treatment. One of the three assumptions any adjustment rests on and the only one that can be checked: plot the propensity distribution by arm. It fails loudly, through variance, rather than silently.
Posterior / prior II §21
Belief about a parameter before seeing any data (the prior) and after updating on the observed data (the posterior). Bayes' rule performs the update: $P(\theta\mid x) \propto P(x\mid\theta)P(\theta)$. The posterior is the prior multiplied by the likelihood, then renormalised.
Posterior collapse XVI §19
A latent variable model whose encoder ignores its input, so the code carries no information and the decoder models the data alone. Exactly optimal for the KL term, and worst with the most powerful decoders. Detected by the KL per latent dimension, not by the loss.
Potential-based shaping XVIII §39
Adding $\gamma\Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$ to the reward for any $\Phi$. Provably leaves the optimal policy unchanged, because the terms telescope along any trajectory. The only shaping form with that guarantee: an arbitrary bonus can and does change the answer.
Power V §13
The probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when the alternative is true, i.e., the probability of detecting a real effect. An underpowered study that finds nothing has not shown the effect is absent; it has shown only that the study was too small to find it. In machine learning, benchmark comparisons with high seed variance are frequently underpowered without knowing it.
Power spectrum XXVII §27
The distribution of a signal's energy across frequency, licensed as a decomposition by Parseval's identity. Natural images follow a steep power law (Figure 6 of Part XXVII measures $f^{-2.91}$ on 26 tiles from three sources, against $f^{0.00}$ for white noise), which is the measurement every smoothness prior in this book is betting on.
PPO XVIII §31
A clipped surrogate objective that removes the reward for stepping outside a trust region, so the gradient vanishes there rather than being penalised. Won on simplicity rather than theory, and replication work found much of its measured advantage comes from implementation details absent from its equations.
Precision XIV §3
Of the cases flagged positive, the fraction that are. Trades against recall as the threshold moves: flagging nobody gives precision 1.0 and recall 0. Reporting either alone is compatible with a useless classifier.
Precision and recall for distributions XVI §43
Precision is the fraction of generated samples inside the data's support; recall is the fraction of real samples inside the model's. A mode-dropping model has high precision and low recall, a model producing garbage the reverse. The pair separates what a single distance conflates.
Prediction set XXIV §28
The classification form of a conformal interval: a set of labels guaranteed to contain the truth with probability $1-\alpha$. Its size is an interpretable difficulty signal, and it can be empty, which is a system able to say it has nothing.
Prefill and decode XIX §2
The two phases of serving. Prefill processes the whole prompt in one parallel pass and is compute-bound; decode produces one token at a time and is memory-bound. Different bottlenecks, different optimisations, different costs, and conflating them is the most common error in reasoning about inference.
Pre-norm XII §17
Normalising the input to each sublayer rather than the sum after it, leaving the residual path unmodified. Widens the range of learning rates from which training can recover; the claim that it removes the need for warmup is too strong.
Pretext task XV §16
A supervised problem whose labels are manufactured from the data: predicting a rotation, a tile permutation, a removed colour. Plateaued because it specifies what must be predicted where what you want to specify is what may be ignored, leaving each task carrying an accidental bias determined by whichever shortcut was available.
Probabilistic completeness XXXIII §25
The guarantee sampling-based planners offer: if a solution exists, the probability of finding it tends to one with more samples. If none exists the algorithm runs forever and never says so: an asymmetry that is why deployed planners carry a timeout and a fallback.
Probabilistic roadmap XXXIII §26
Sample free configurations, connect nearby pairs whose straight segment is collision free, then answer queries by graph search on the result. The expensive phase is paid once, which makes it right for a static environment with many queries and wrong for a changing one.
Probability-flow ODE XXVIII §37
A deterministic equation whose marginal distribution at every time matches a given diffusion's. Sampling becomes deterministic given the starting noise, the map is invertible, and an exact likelihood becomes available. Figure 7 of Part XXVIII measures the honest cost: with a first-order solver it is less accurate than the stochastic sampler at every step count, and its advantage comes from admitting higher-order solvers.
Prompt injection XX §38
Instructions placed in data that the model treats as instructions. The model has no channel separating the two, so a retrieved document containing a directive is indistinguishable from a directive. Cannot be fixed inside the model; the separation must be enforced by the system around it.
Propensity score XXII §18
The probability of treatment given the covariates. A balancing score: conditioning on it alone suffices wherever conditioning on all the covariates would, collapsing a high-dimensional adjustment to one dimension.
Proper scoring rule XXV §25
A loss whose minimiser is the true conditional distribution. Fitting one is why gradient boosting with the logistic loss comes out calibrated while a random forest's vote fraction, fitted to nothing, does not.
Proxy label XIII §6
Measuring $z$ because you cannot measure the $y$ you wanted. Often necessary; dangerous because the substitution is invisible downstream: every metric afterwards is about $z$ and nothing records that.
Pseudoinverse I §20
$A^+$, defined through the SVD, existing for every matrix. Gives the least-squares solution when overdetermined and the minimum-norm solution when underdetermined.
p-value V §11–12
The probability, computed under the null hypothesis, of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one obtained. A small p-value says the data would be surprising if nothing were happening; it does not say the alternative is true, or probable, or important. The probability that the null is true is not a p-value and cannot be computed from one without a prior.
QR factorisation III §20
$A = QR$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $R$ triangular. The stable route to least squares, because it never forms $A^\top A$.
Quadratic form I §18
$\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}$, a scalar. Its graph is a bowl, a dome, or a saddle depending on the definiteness of $A$.
Quantization XIX §8
Storing weights as integers with a shared scale. The dominant compression technique because it reduces bytes moved, which [[arithmetic-intensity]] says is what binds during generation. Its accuracy depends far more on granularity and on outlier handling than on the rounding method.
Quasi-Newton III §12
Building an approximation to $H^{-1}$ from successive gradients. L-BFGS is the standard member; it assumes exact gradients, so it is rare in deep learning.
Query, key, value XII §3
The three roles in an attention lookup. The key is what a position is found by, the value is what it returns. Separating the address from the contents is what makes a lookup a lookup.
Rademacher complexity XXIII §16
How well a class can correlate with random signs on the sample you have. Data-dependent, unlike VC dimension, and computable: the computation is the randomised-label experiment, so the bound and the diagnostic are the same procedure.
Radial distortion XXIX §27
A lens's departure from the pinhole model, growing with distance from the principal point. Figure 6 of Part XXIX measures exactly zero error at the centre and 164 px at the corner of a 1,024 px wide-angle image, two orders of magnitude beyond any detector's localisation accuracy. Because it vanishes at the centre, a system developed on centred objects looks calibrated and fails at the edge of frame, which is why the residual must be reported against radius rather than averaged.
Random forest XXV §11
Bagging plus a random subset of features offered at each split. The subsetting lowers the correlation between trees monotonically; whether that buys lower ensemble error depends on how many columns carry signal, since a tree denied its best feature is also a worse tree.
Random search XIV §16
Sampling hyperparameter configurations independently rather than gridding them. Better than a grid at matched budget, and not because of the exponential cost: a grid spends its trials on repeats of every dimension, so 64 grid points in two dimensions test 8 distinct values of the axis that matters, where 64 random points test 64. Sample on a log scale for scale-free quantities, and do not discretise.
Random variable II §3
A quantity whose value is determined by the outcome of a random process. Formally a function from the underlying sample space to real numbers, which is why two random variables defined on the same sample space can be dependent: they are functions of the same underlying randomness.
Rank I §11
The number of linearly independent columns, i.e., genuinely distinct directions, not column count. Rank deficiency is information destroyed.
Rank collapse XII §26
Stacked self-attention converging to a representation in which every position holds the same vector, measured at effective rank 1.00 by depth 32. Attention outputs are convex combinations and repeated averaging converges; and the residual connection is what prevents it, which is a second job it does beyond shortening the gradient path.
Rapidly-exploring random tree XXXIII §27
Grow a tree by sampling a configuration, finding the nearest node, and stepping toward it. Extension probability is proportional to a node's Voronoi volume, so the tree pulls toward unexplored space without being told to. Single-query, and the paths need smoothing before anything executes them.
Rate–distortion function XV §43
$R(D)$: the least rate that reconstructs a source within average distortion $D$. A property of the source and the distortion measure rather than of any codec, so every real quantiser sits above it. For a Gaussian under squared error, $R(D)=\tfrac12\log_2(\sigma^2/D)$: half a bit per halving of distortion.
Readout XXVI §29
The operation collapsing a graph's node representations into one vector for a graph-level prediction. Where the invariance is finally imposed, and the sum-against-mean choice is a claim about whether the number of nodes is a fact about the object or an artefact of how the graph was built.
Recall XIV §3
Of the cases that are positive, the fraction flagged. The counterpart to precision: flagging everybody gives recall 1.0 and precision equal to the base rate.
Receptive field X §10
The set of input pixels that can affect one output unit. For $L$ stacked $3\times3$ layers it is $1+2L$, which is the region that can matter, not the region that does.
Recurrent neural network XI §4
A network applying the same transition to a state at every step: $\mathbf{h}^{(t)} = f(\mathbf{h}^{(t-1)}, \mathbf{x}^{(t)};\boldsymbol{\theta})$. Shares parameters across time, accepts any length, and pays with a path between distant positions that grows with the sequence.
Recursive network XI §24
The same shared parameters applied over a tree rather than a chain, giving depth $O(\log\tau)$ instead of $O(\tau)$. The obstacle is that somebody has to supply the tree, and inferring it is discrete.
Red-teaming XXI §26
Adversarial search for inputs producing unacceptable outputs. Produces existence proofs, which are strong, and not rates, which is what people usually read from it: the inputs were chosen adversarially, so the success fraction measures the team rather than the system.
Regression discontinuity XXII §28
Using a threshold rule to compare units just above and just below a cutoff, identified by assuming everything else varies smoothly through it. Unusually credible, and it estimates the effect at the cutoff only.
Regret XVIII §34 · XXIII §31 · XXXIII §29
Cumulative reward lost against always having played the best action. Its growth is what matters: linear means a fixed loss per step forever, logarithmic means the loss per step goes to zero. It can be negative, because the comparator is the best fixed action rather than the best sequence, which is why a no-regret guarantee is weaker than the name suggests.
Regularization VIII §1
Any modification intended to reduce generalisation error without reducing training error, or equivalently, a preference among the functions that fit the training data equally well. Stated before you know which functions will fit. Includes penalties, architecture constraints, data augmentation, noise injection and early stopping: five very different mechanisms that express the same underlying intent.
Rehearsal XX §48
Mixing a fraction of earlier training data into a new training run. The cheapest remedy for [[catastrophic-forgetting]] by a wide margin: a few per cent recovers most of what would be lost at no measurable cost to the new task. Its binding constraint is organisational rather than algorithmic: it presumes you still have the earlier data in a form you can sample.
Relaxation (heuristics) XXXIII §13
Delete a constraint, solve the easier problem exactly, and use its cost as a heuristic. Admissible automatically, because every solution to the original is still a solution to the relaxed one. Manhattan distance is the 8-puzzle without the rule that tiles block each other.
Reliability XX §44
Whether a model is correct under every paraphrase of a request, as against average accuracy. Always at most the mean and usually far below it. Benchmarks report accuracy; deployments need this, because at 95% per step a twenty-step chain succeeds about a third of the time.
Reliability diagram XIV §5
Observed accuracy plotted against predicted confidence, bin by bin. The diagonal is perfect calibration; a curve below it is overconfidence. Expected calibration error is this plot reduced to one number, and the plot is worth keeping because the reduction hides where the miscalibration lives.
ReLU 0 §18
$\max(0, x)$. Derivative is 1 where active and 0 elsewhere, which is why it does not suffer the vanishing gradient of the sigmoid.
Reparameterisation trick XVI §16
Writing $\mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\sigma}\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ so that the randomness sits in a variable that does not depend on the parameters, making the sampling step differentiable. Low variance, and restricted to continuous latents, which is why VAEs use Gaussian codes essentially always.
Representation collapse XV §20
A learned representation shrinking to a single point or direction, carrying no information. The global optimum of any objective that rewards agreement between views without a term pushing different inputs apart. Detected with [[effective-rank]]; a probe may or may not notice, depending on the task and on the probe's own regularization.
Reprojection error XXIX §27
The distance in pixels between where a 3-D point projects under an estimated camera model and where it was observed. The objective of calibration and of bundle adjustment, and the quantity to check against radius when diagnosing a geometry problem.
Reservoir computing XI §25
The family containing echo state networks and liquid state machines: a fixed random recurrence providing temporal features, with only the readout trained.
Residual connection VII §18
Adding a layer's input directly to its output: $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x} + F(\mathbf{x})$. The gradient backpropagates through the addition without any multiplicative factor, so information reaches early layers even at great depth. The structural change that made training networks of hundreds of layers practical, and whose benefit can be understood as giving optimisation a shortcut path to the identity function.
Residual quantisation XXX §20
Applying several small codebooks in sequence, each encoding what the previous stages left. Avoids a codebook exponential in the bitrate: Figure 5 of Part XXX measures it landing within 1.4 to 1.5× of full vector quantisation with a codebook of 16 rather than 256.
Residual stream XII §16
The reading of a transformer in which the representation at depth $L$ is the embedding plus the sum of every sublayer's output: a channel each component reads from and adds to, rather than a pipeline of stages.
Reverse mode VII §13
Propagating derivatives of one output backward through a graph. One pass yields the full gradient, which is why training is affordable.
Reverse-time SDE XXVIII §36
A diffusion run backwards is another diffusion, with a drift involving $\nabla \log p_t$: the score. Learn that one quantity at every noise level and generation is simulation. This is the result the whole of diffusion modelling rests on, and Part XVI's discrete noise ladder is its discretisation.
Reward hacking XVIII §39
A policy that maximises its reward while failing the intention behind it. Not a malfunction: the reward is a specification and the optimiser is paid to find what it literally says. Measured here at a policy earning 13.9× the proxy return of the correct policy while never reaching the goal.
Rewiring XXVI §23
Adding, removing or replacing edges to shorten the paths message passing must travel: a virtual node connected to everything, or full attention. It reduces over-squashing and changes the object being modelled, so whether it is legitimate is a question about the problem rather than about the metric.
Rich regime XXIII §49
Training in which the features themselves change, so the network is not described by its linearisation. The regime in which representation learning happens, and (measurably) the one that generalises better at equal training loss and reaches it in fewer steps. See [[lazy-regime]].
Ridge point XIX §3
A machine's peak compute divided by its memory bandwidth, in FLOP per byte. Operations below it are memory-bound and their arithmetic units idle.
Ring all-reduce XXXII §18
Summing a buffer across workers by passing slices around a ring: reduce-scatter, then all-gather. Each worker moves $2(N-1)/N$ buffers, bounded by two whatever the worker count, against a naive scheme's linear growth: Figure 4 of Part XXXII measures a factor of 32 at 64 workers. Optimal in volume and not in latency, which is why libraries switch algorithm by message size.
Risk VI §3
Expected loss on new draws from the true data distribution: what you actually want to minimise. Cannot be computed because the true distribution is never fully observed. Empirical risk is the computable approximation; the generalisation gap is what the approximation costs.
RMSProp III §16
Per-parameter step sizes scaled by a running average of squared gradients. AdaGrad with forgetting, which is what makes it work on non-convex problems.
Rolling-origin validation XXV §37
Repeatedly training on everything up to a point and testing on what follows. The honest split for ordered rows: Figure 8 of Part XXV measures a random split reporting an error 18.8 times better than the chronological one, with every conventional diagnostic passing.
Roofline XXXII §4
A performance ceiling with two pieces (intensity times bandwidth, or peak arithmetic, whichever is smaller) meeting at the ridge point. Placing a kernel on it takes ten minutes and decides which remedies apply, since the fixes for memory-bound and compute-bound work are disjoint sets.
RoPE XII §11
Rotating queries and keys by an angle proportional to position, so that $(R_m\mathbf{q})^\top(R_n\mathbf{k}) = \mathbf{q}^\top R_{n-m}\mathbf{k}$ and absolute position cancels. Verified at $2.1\times10^{-12}$. Applies at every layer and leaves the values untouched.
Rotation equivariance XXVI §35
Rotating the input rotates the output. Achieved by keeping scalars and vectors separately at each node and updating vectors only as scalar-weighted sums of relative positions, so every term transforms the same way. Required whenever the prediction has a direction, which invariant distance-only models cannot produce.
RRT* XXXIII §28
RRT with two additions (connect a new node to the cheapest nearby parent, then rewire neighbours through it when cheaper), which makes the cost converge to the optimum. Requires a rewiring radius shrinking like $(\log n/n)^{1/d}$, where the dimension reappears.
Rule of three XXI §27 · XXXI §42
After $n$ trials with zero failures, the 95% upper bound on the failure rate is about $3/n$. Equivalently, seeing a failure of rate $p$ at least once costs about $3/p$ attempts. The single most useful piece of arithmetic in safety evaluation.
Saddle point I §18, III §18
A critical point that is a minimum in some directions and a maximum in others. The dominant obstacle in high-dimensional optimisation, far more common than bad local minima.
Safety case XXI §32
A structured argument that a system is acceptably safe in a stated context: claim, evidence, assumptions, defeaters. Borrowed from aviation and nuclear engineering, and adopted for the same reason: no single test establishes safety, so the object of review has to be the argument.
Sampling distribution V §1, §6
The distribution of a statistic across possible samples. For the mean it is approximately Gaussian with spread $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
Sampling frame XIII §2
See frame.
Sampling theorem XXVII §2
A signal with no content above $B$ is determined completely by samples taken at a rate above $2B$, and can be reconstructed exactly. The reconstruction is explicit, which is what makes this a theorem rather than a rule of thumb, and what makes violating it a definite loss rather than a degradation.
SARSA XVIII §18
On-policy control: the target uses the action actually taken next rather than the greedy one. Learns the value of the policy being run, exploration included, so it avoids cliff edges that Q-learning walks along and occasionally falls off.
Sauer's lemma XXIII §11
If the VC dimension is $d$, the growth function is at most $(em/d)^d$. Establishes a dichotomy with nothing in between: a class realises every labelling at every size, or polynomially many. The polynomial is what produces a vanishing rate.
Scalable oversight XXI §21
The problem of supervising a system you cannot directly evaluate, because it is faster or more expert than its overseer. The bottleneck that binds first as capability rises, currently unsolved, and the thing debate, amplification and weak-to-strong all attack.
Scaled dot-product attention XII §4
$\text{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})V$. The $\sqrt{d_k}$ is forced: $\text{Var}[\mathbf{q}^\top\mathbf{k}] = d$, so without it the logits grow like $\sqrt{d}$, the softmax saturates, and its Jacobian (and therefore the layer's learning) goes to zero.
Scaling law XVII §2
$L = A x^{-\alpha} + E$: loss falls as a power of parameters, data or compute, above an irreducible floor. Empirical, with no accepted derivation. The exponent and the floor are fitted together and trade off, so two quoted exponents may differ only in how each handled $E$.
Schedule III §17
A rule for changing the learning rate over training, typically decaying it. Required for convergence under stochastic noise: a fixed rate causes the optimiser to circulate around rather than converge to the optimum. The Robbins–Monro conditions ($\sum\alpha_t = \infty$, $\sum\alpha_t^2 < \infty$) are the theoretical requirement; warmup then cosine decay is the common practical form.
Score XV §12
$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \log p(\mathbf{x})$, the gradient of the log density with respect to the input: a vector field pointing uphill in probability. Independent of the normalising constant, because the gradient of a log annihilates any constant factor, which is why it can be learned when the density cannot.
Score matching XVI §23
Fitting a model's score to the data's, which removes $Z$ by differentiation rather than approximation. Its integration-by-parts form eliminates the unknown data score too, at the cost of a second derivative per input dimension, which made it unusable at scale until [[denoising-score-matching]] removed that cost.
Seasonal naive XXV §35
Predicting each point from one season earlier. A baseline that is frequently hard to beat, and one a forecasting result should always be reported against.
Seed variance XIV §19
The variation in final performance across random seeds of an otherwise identical configuration. Arises from initialisation, data order, dropout masks, augmentation draws and kernel nondeterminism. On many benchmarks it exceeds the improvements being claimed.
Selection bias XIII §7
The frame differing from the population by a mechanism related to the outcome. Survivorship, self-selection and feedback. The lending case is the sharp one: no statistic computed from the observed data detects it, because the missing records are missing.
Selective copying XI §36
Recovering the value of the last marked token from a stream of noise. The task selectivity exists to solve: no fixed decay does better than chance-adjacent because it must average in everything it sees, while an input-dependent gate is exact, with the same one number of state in both.
Selective state space model XI §36
Making a state space model's decay and input matrices functions of the token, so it can hold through irrelevant spans and overwrite on relevant ones. What separates a modern state space model from a classical linear time-invariant one. It costs time invariance, so the FFT route is gone and the associative scan is what remains.
Selectivity XV §32
Probe accuracy on the real task minus probe accuracy on a [[control-task]]. The quantity that carries a claim about a representation, where accuracy alone does not. A property of the representation and the probe: a powerful enough probe drives selectivity toward zero on any representation that retains example identity.
Self-attention XII §7
Attention with queries, keys and values all from the same sequence. Path length 1 between any two positions, and no sequential dependency, at a cost of $O(n^2d)$ compute and $O(n^2)$ memory.
Self-consistency XXXIII §39
Sampling several answers and returning the most common one. Needs no verifier, and works because independent errors scatter while a correct derivation concentrates. Fails on a systematic misconception, which voting amplifies: removing variance and never bias.
Self-supervised learning VI §2 · XV §15
Learning a representation from unlabelled data by manufacturing a training signal from the data itself. Its first form, greedy layer-wise pretraining, was abandoned when the optimisation problem it solved was fixed by other means. Its modern forms, contrastive and masked objectives, differ in stating what may be ignored rather than what must be predicted.
Semi-supervised learning VIII §17
Using unlabelled data, which carries information about $p(\mathbf{x})$ and none about $p(y\mid\mathbf{x})$. Helps only if the structure of the first is informative about the second, e.g., clusters sharing labels, boundaries in low-density regions.
Set prediction XXIX §11
Emitting a fixed number of queries and matching them to ground truth by solving an assignment problem, so the loss is permutation-invariant by construction. Removes the suppression step entirely, which is its strongest argument, and trains slowly because the assignment changes between epochs.
SGD III §14
Gradient descent on minibatch estimates. Noisy per step, enormously cheaper per step, and the noise helps escape saddles.
Shattering XXIII §10
A set of points is shattered by a class when the class realises all $2^m$ labellings of it. Checkable by enumeration: Figure 2 of Part XXIII decides each labelling with a linear program and recovers $d+1$ for linear classifiers without invoking the theorem.
Shielding XXXI §35
Keeping a verified fallback controller and a region from which it can recover, and letting a learned policy act only inside that region. The only one of the three standard safety approaches that yields a statement for a safety case: penalties trade safety against reward at an exchange rate, and constraints bound an expectation, which says nothing about the tail.
Shingle XIII §15
An overlapping $k$-gram of a document. The set of a document's shingles is what similarity is computed over.
Shortcut learning XIII §8
A model using a feature that predicts the label in your dataset and will not in deployment. Not a malfunction; the shortcut genuinely has lower loss on the data supplied. Held-out data cannot detect it, because the shortcut is in the test set too.
Shrinkage VIII §4
What a penalty does per eigen-direction: $L^2$ keeps a fraction $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\alpha)$, so sharply curved directions survive and flat ones are erased.
Sigmoid 0 §18, II §19
$\sigma(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})$, squashing the real line into $(0,1)$. Its derivative is $\sigma(1-\sigma)$, which peaks at 0.25, the number behind vanishing gradients.
Signal propagation XXIII §53
How the correlation between two inputs' representations evolves with depth in a randomly initialised network. What initialisation actually chooses is not a variance but a depth scale: the number of layers over which information about the input survives.
Simpson's paradox XXII §3
When an association within every stratum reverses when the strata are pooled. Not a paradox (two correct answers to two different questions), and which one is wanted is decided by what you intend to do, not by the data.
Sim-to-real gap XXXI §23
The discrepancy between a simulator a policy was trained in and the world it is deployed in. Crossed by randomising the simulator's parameters, identifying the real ones, learning a residual correction, or (cheapest and most overlooked) closing a fast stabilising loop, which corrects errors before they compound and extends how far any learned model can be trusted.
Simulated annealing XXXIII §16
Local search that accepts a worsening move of size $\Delta$ with probability $e^{-\Delta/T}$, cooling $T$ over time. The acceptance rule is Metropolis–Hastings, so at fixed temperature the visit distribution is Boltzmann; the schedule that guarantees convergence is too slow to use.
Singular matrix I §12, §22
Not invertible. Equivalently: zero determinant, a zero eigenvalue, rank-deficient, collapses space onto a lower-dimensional set. Six statements, one condition.
Singularity (kinematic) XXXI §26
A configuration at which a manipulator's Jacobian loses rank, so motion in some direction becomes unavailable and the joint velocity required for a small end-effector motion diverges. Full extension of a two-link arm is the simplest example.
Sliding-window attention XI §37
Exact attention within a fixed window and nothing beyond it. A fixed cache like a recurrence, trading lossy recall everywhere for exact recall inside the window. The same trade as a linear-state layer at a different point.
Soft addressing XI §26
Reading every memory cell and taking a softmax-weighted average, because an exact integer address has no gradient. Identical in form to attention, which is how two separate problems converged on one operation.
Softmax II §20
Exponentiate, then normalise, turning arbitrary logits into a categorical distribution. Unchanged by adding a constant to every logit, which is the basis of the stable implementation.
Softmax saturation XII §4
Logits spread widely enough that the softmax is effectively an argmax. Its Jacobian $a_i(\delta_{ij}-a_j)$ is then near zero, so the layer stops learning: Part VII §7's saturation argument, arriving in an attention weight rather than an activation.
Softplus II §19
$\log(1+e^x)$, a smooth always-positive approximation to ReLU. Its derivative is exactly the sigmoid.
Space-filling loss XV §43
The fixed overhead a quantiser pays for coding one sample at a time rather than long blocks: $\tfrac12\log_2(\pi e/6) \approx 0.2546$ bits per sample for an entropy-coded uniform scalar quantiser. It does not shrink with rate, and going from one dimension to two recovers only about a twelfth of it.
Span I §11
The set of all vectors reachable by forming linear combinations of a given set: all possible scalings and sums. The span of the columns of a matrix $A$ is exactly the set of vectors $A\mathbf{x}$ can equal, which determines when $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution.
Sparse autoencoder XV §10
An autoencoder with an L1 penalty on its code. Two distinct uses: learning features from data, where the penalty is the log-prior of a Laplace-distributed latent variable rather than a regularizer in the usual sense; and, applied to a trained network's activations with a much wider code, recovering interpretable directions from [[superposition]].
Sparse coding XV §10
Inferring a sparse code by optimisation rather than by a learned encoder: minimise $\|\mathbf{x} - D^\top\mathbf{h}\|^2 + \lambda\|\mathbf{h}\|_1$ over $\mathbf{h}$. No encoder generalisation error, at the cost of running an iterative solver for every input. Atoms are recovered only up to sign and permutation.
Sparse initialisation IX §16
Giving each unit exactly $k$ nonzero incoming weights of ordinary size, so the total input does not depend on width and individual weights do not shrink. Imposes a strong prior on which weights are large.
Sparse representations VIII §6
Most activations zero for any given input, as opposed to sparse parameters, where most weights are zero. A rich vocabulary used sparingly, rather than a small one.
Specification problem XXI §17
That the objective written down is an approximation to what was wanted, and optimisation exploits the difference. Requires no assumption of agency or deception (a misfitted regression has the same structure), and becomes dangerous only as optimisation pressure grows.
Spectral bias XXVII §29
The tendency of a network trained by gradient descent to fit low-frequency components of its target first and fastest. Figure 7 of Part XXVII measures 90% of target reached at step 50 for the slowest component and never, within 20,000 steps, for the fastest, with the ordering set by frequency and not by amplitude. Much of what this book calls implicit regularisation is this one fact, and it is why early stopping keeps the smooth explanation.
Spectral radius XI §11
The largest absolute eigenvalue of a matrix. Because a recurrence applies the same matrix at every step, $\mathbf{h}^{(t)} = Q\Lambda^t Q^\top\mathbf{h}^{(0)}$ and the spectral radius decides whether the state and the gradient vanish or explode. Set near 1 deliberately in a reservoir; suffered everywhere else.
Spectral theorem I §17
Every real symmetric matrix factors as $Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors. The reason covariance matrices and Hessians are tractable.
Spectrogram XXVII §23
The magnitude of the transform of successive overlapping windowed segments. Every one commits to a window length, a hop and a window function, and the first of those is a point on a resolution trade that cannot be escaped: Figure 5 of Part XXVII measures the product of time and frequency resolution constant at 1.875 while each factor moves sixteen-fold.
Speculative decoding XIX §40
A small draft model proposes several tokens; the large model scores them all in one pass (nearly free, because that pass was memory-bound), and a rejection rule accepts a prefix. The rule makes the output distribution exactly the large model's, so it is not an approximation.
Standard error V §3
The standard deviation of an estimator, not of the data. For the mean, $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
State dict IV §15
A model's parameters and buffers as a plain dictionary of names to tensors. Portable, unlike pickling the model object, which embeds a reference to your class definition.
State space model XI §34
A recurrence designed so that a sequential form is available at inference and a parallel form for training. The live descendant of the recurrent line, and an attempt to keep constant memory per step while removing the training bottleneck.
State space (search) XXXIII §2
States, an initial state, actions, a transition function and a goal test, plus step costs. Almost never written down: it is generated as you go, which is the only reason search is feasible. Choosing it is the modelling; choosing the algorithm afterwards is a short menu.
Stationarity XI §8
The assumption that the relation between consecutive steps does not depend on $t$. What sharing parameters across time asserts, and false for regime changes, scene cuts and diurnal cycles, with no way for the model to say so. Part XXV §33 covers what differencing costs to obtain it, and why the classical forecasting models require it.
Steering vector XXI §13
A direction added to activations at inference to push behaviour toward a property, usually built as a difference in means between paired inputs. A causal test when swept over its coefficient and checked against properties you did not intend to change; a curiosity otherwise.
Stop-gradient XV §21
Blocking the gradient through one branch of a two-branch objective. With a predictor head on the other branch it prevents collapse without any repulsive term, by making the collapsed solution unreachable by the optimiser rather than suboptimal. A guarantee about the trajectory rather than about the objective, and correspondingly more fragile.
Straggler XXXII §21
The slowest worker in a synchronous step, which sets the pace for the whole job. At scale the tail of the timing distribution matters rather than its mean, so one thermally-throttled machine is a fleet-wide throughput problem.
Straight-through estimator XIX §12
Passing the gradient through a rounding operation as though it were the identity. A deliberate lie about the derivative that works because the quantity being optimised is the weights, for which the identity gradient points approximately correctly.
Stride X §5
Evaluating the kernel every $s$ positions. Identical to convolving with stride one and discarding all but every $s$-th output, and much cheaper than doing that.
Structural causal model XXII §8
A set of assignments $V_i := f_i(\mathrm{pa}_i, \varepsilon_i)$ with independent noise. Its content beyond a joint distribution is modularity: one equation can be replaced while the others keep working, which is what makes intervention computable.
Structure tensor XXIX §22
The summed outer product of image gradients over a patch. Its smaller eigenvalue decides whether optical flow is determined there (near zero on an edge, large on a corner), and the same quantity decides where a corner detector fires. Good features to track and solvable flow are the same condition seen from either side.
Structured sparsity XIX §20
Zeroing weights in a regular pattern (whole rows, channels, heads, or two of every four) so that hardware can exploit it. Delivers real speedups at 50% where unstructured sparsity delivers none at 90%, because a zero in a dense array costs the same as any other number.
Student's $t$ V §7
The correct distribution for a mean when $\sigma$ is estimated from the same data. Heavier-tailed than the Gaussian, and converging to it as $n$ grows.
Successive halving XIV §17
Start many configurations with a small budget, keep the best half, double the budget, repeat. Costs roughly $n\log n$ epoch-equivalents rather than $n$ full runs, so the same budget starts far more configurations, which is the mechanism, not better evaluation. Rests on early performance predicting final performance, and at small budgets it is not better than random but identical, because there is no room to eliminate.
Superposition XV §33
A layer representing more features than it has dimensions by assigning them non-orthogonal directions. Worthwhile when features are sparse, because two features sharing a direction rarely collide. Measured here at sixteen features stored in five dimensions at 99% sparsity, against exactly five when features are dense.
Support vector machine VI §14
The maximum-margin separating boundary. Depends only on the few points at the margin, which are the support vectors.
Surrogate loss IX §3
A differentiable proxy for the loss that actually matters. The 0–1 classification error has no useful gradient; negative log-likelihood is the differentiable surrogate. Minimising the surrogate does not guarantee minimising the target, but it can leave you strictly better off, because the surrogate keeps extracting information from the data even after the classifier's accuracy is saturated.
SVD I §19
$A = U\Sigma V^\top$ for every matrix: rotate, stretch, rotate. Truncating it gives the provably best low-rank approximation.
Sycophancy XX §31
A model changing its answer to agree with a position the user stated. A direct consequence of preference data: annotators preferred agreement, so the objective rewarded it. Measurable by asking neutrally and then again after stating an opinion.
Symbol-to-symbol differentiation VII §16
Returning the gradient as a graph rather than as numbers, so it can be differentiated again. What makes higher-order derivatives possible without separate machinery.
Symmetric matrix I §16
$A = A^\top$. Buys real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors, which is not true of matrices in general.
Synthetic data XIII §27
Examples produced by a model, a simulator or a program. Can transfer and reorganise information; cannot create it. The legitimate uses all name a source (a teacher model, a program, a verifier), and if you cannot name one, §28 is what happens.
Tangent propagation VIII §11
Penalising the derivative of the output along directions the label is invariant to. Imposes an invariance by differentiation where augmentation imposes it by example, and those directions are the tangent space of Part VI §23's manifold.
Tangent space XV §5
The set of directions in which a point can move while remaining on a manifold: for an image, small translations, rotations and changes of pose. Its dimension is the manifold's, far below the ambient dimension. A good representation is a coordinate system along it and insensitive off it.
Target encoding XXV §20
Replacing a category with a statistic of the target within it. The best-performing categorical encoding and a leak unless the statistic is computed out-of-fold, because the row being encoded contributes to its own feature.
Task vector XX §50
The displacement a fine-tune produced, $\tau = \theta_{\text{ft}} - \theta_0$, treated as an object that can be added and subtracted. Addition composes capabilities and loses about a quarter of each; negation removes a behaviour and damages unrelated ones. How well it works is predicted by the angle between the vectors: near-orthogonal composes, aligned interferes.
Teacher forcing XI §9
Using the true previous token as input during training, rather than the model's own prediction. Not a hack: the correct maximum likelihood objective conditions on the observed sequence, not on the model's output. A side benefit is that steps are decoupled, so the loss can be computed for all positions in parallel rather than sequentially.
Temperature scaling XIV §5
Dividing logits by a single scalar $T$ fitted on validation data, then re-applying the softmax. Because a positive $T$ preserves the order of the logits, no prediction changes and accuracy is exactly unaltered; only the confidences move. The strongest calibration method relative to its cost, with one parameter.
Temporal difference learning XVIII §16
Updating a value estimate toward $r + \gamma V(s')$: a bootstrapped target using the current estimate. Biased while the estimate is wrong, and far lower variance than a full return; the bias is transient and the variance is not, which is why TD usually wins.
Tensor I §2
In deep learning, simply an array with any number of axes. Carries none of the transformation-law meaning it has in physics.
Tensor parallelism XVII §23
Splitting individual weight matrices across devices, with an all-reduce per block. Communicates twice per layer on the critical path and cannot be overlapped, so it lives within a node, typically across the eight devices sharing a board.
Test-time compute XX §37 · XXXIII §42
Spending more at inference instead of at training: sampling several answers, best-of-$n$ against a verifier, longer reasoning chains, or a model trained to use the budget. Makes performance a function of two compute quantities that trade against each other, and best-of-$n$ inherits Part XIV §20's selection bias.
Threat model XXXII §33
A statement of what an adversary can do, wants, and knows. A defence without one is not a claim, and the assumption that fails is always the same: that the adversary does not know the defence. A large fraction of published defences were broken by an attack adapted to them.
Toeplitz matrix X §4
A matrix in which each row is the row above, shifted. The structure of convolution written as a matrix; in two dimensions the shift happens at two scales and the result is doubly block circulant.
Trace I §21
Sum of the diagonal. Equals the sum of the eigenvalues, and is invariant under cyclic permutation of a product.
Transfer learning IX §26
Initialising from a model trained on a different, usually larger task. The same move as pretraining, across tasks rather than across depth.
Transpose I §6
$A^\top$, flipping rows and columns. Reverses order in a product: $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$.
Transposed convolution X §27
Multiplication by $M^\top$ used forwards, taking a small feature map to a large one. It is the backward pass of a convolution, run in the other direction. Cannot infer its output size, which is what output_padding is for.
Treewidth XXVIII §11
The size of the largest intermediate factor created by the best elimination order. Exact inference costs exponential time in it, so it decides tractability before any data arrives: 1 for a tree, small for a chain, and growing with the side length of a grid.
Triangulation XXIX §29
Recovering depth from disparity between two views: $Z = fb/d$. A fixed disparity error becomes a depth error growing as $Z^2/(fb)$: Figure 7 of Part XXIX measures the exponent at 2.000 and 10.4 m of error at 100 m for a 12 cm baseline. Halving the disparity error is worth exactly as much as doubling the baseline, and is usually cheaper.
Truncated BPTT XI §6
Propagating the gradient back only $k$ steps. Unlike minibatch noise this is bias (whole terms are deleted, and averaging does not recover them), so a dependency longer than $k$ cannot be learned at all.
Trust region XVIII §31
A constraint on how far a policy may move in one update, measured in policy space rather than parameter space. Necessary because a policy that steps too far collects worse data, from which it learns a worse policy: a collapse with no fixed dataset to recover against.
Two-part code XV §44
The decomposition description length rests on: transmit a model, then transmit the data given the model. Dropping the first term turns a selection criterion into a fitting criterion, which is where "it compresses the data better" usually goes wrong. See [[minimum-description-length]].
Two-tower model XXX §25
Encoding two modalities separately and comparing them with a single dot product, trained so matched pairs beat every other item in the batch. Fast, because each item can be embedded once and indexed, and structurally unable to express anything needing detailed comparison between the inputs: which object a phrase refers to, whether a relation holds, counting. The standard resolution is to retrieve with two towers and rerank with a cross-encoder.
UCT XXXIII §31
Applying an upper confidence bound at every internal node of a Monte Carlo tree search. What makes the value estimate converge to the value under optimal play rather than to the value of a random player: the exploration constant has an interior optimum that moves with the budget, so one tuned at a small budget is wrong at a large one.
Unfolding XI §2
Substituting a recurrence into itself until the self-reference is gone, converting a loop into an ordinary directed acyclic computational graph. The result has a fixed architecture (inputs at each time step, shared weights at each step, outputs at each step) and can be differentiated by standard backpropagation. The sharing across time is what allows generalisation to sequence lengths not seen during training.
Uniform convergence XXIII §12
A bound holding simultaneously for every hypothesis in a class, which is what licenses choosing one after seeing the data. Its failure for modern networks is a theorem rather than a gap: they fit random labels, so the class capacity is maximal and no distribution-free bound over it can say anything.
Uniform-cost search XXXIII §4
Expand the node of least path cost. Optimal for any non-negative costs, and identical to Dijkstra's algorithm arrived at from the search side rather than the graph side. A* with $h=0$.
Universal approximation VII §3
A single hidden layer with sufficiently many units can approximate any continuous function on a compact set to arbitrary precision. An existence result only: it says nothing about how many units are needed, whether gradient descent will find the approximating weights, or whether the solution found will generalise. Wide networks that satisfy it are not better in practice than deep ones that do not need it.
Update-to-parameter ratio XIV §12
The norm of a step divided by the norm of the parameters it updates, per layer. Around $10^{-3}$ is healthy. Better than watching the learning rate because it is comparable across layers, architectures and optimisers, and it reports what the learning rate actually did rather than what it was set to.
Uplift XXI §28
How much access to a system changes what a specific actor could accomplish, relative to what they could already do. The decision-relevant quantity in a dangerous-capability evaluation, and a difference between two capabilities, so it requires a control group and is an experiment, not a benchmark.
Upper confidence bound XXXIII §30
$\hat\mu_i + c\sqrt{\ln t/n_i}$: act as though each option is as good as its evidence permits. The bonus shrinks with that arm's pulls and grows with total time, so nothing is abandoned permanently. Gives logarithmic rather than linear regret.
Vacuous bound XXIII §23
A bound permitting values the quantity cannot take: a generalisation bound above 1, satisfied by a random classifier. Figure 3 of Part XXIII evaluates the standard VC bound at 1.1, 4.5 and 10.0 for ordinary network sizes.
Validation set VI §9
Data held out from training and used for every modelling decision: selecting hyperparameters, stopping training, choosing between architectures. Kept strictly separate from the test set, which must remain untouched until the final evaluation. The validation set wears out with repeated use, because each decision made by looking at it is implicitly a test on it.
Value iteration XVIII §11
Repeatedly applying the Bellman optimality operator. Converges to $V^*$ from any starting point at rate exactly $\gamma$ per sweep, so accuracy $\epsilon$ takes about $\log\epsilon/\log\gamma$ sweeps: 132 at $\gamma=0.9$ and 1,375 at $0.99$.
Vanishing gradient VII §18
Gradient magnitude shrinking exponentially with depth. With sigmoids each layer multiplies by at most 0.25, so early layers receive almost nothing.
Variable elimination XXVIII §10
Computing a marginal by summing out variables one at a time, exploiting the fact that sums distribute over products so factors not mentioning the summed variable stay outside. Forward–backward and the junction tree algorithm are this with structure.
Variance II §11
Average squared distance from the mean. Its square root, the standard deviation, carries the same units as the data and is usually easier to interpret.
Variational autoencoder XVI §18
An encoder producing an approximate posterior, a reparameterised sample, and a decoder, trained jointly on the [[elbo]]. Gives one-pass sampling and a manipulable latent, at the price of a bound whose looseness is unknown. Its samples are blurry for three separable reasons, only one of which is specific to the method.
VC dimension XXIII §10
The largest number of points some arrangement of which a class shatters. Finite VC dimension is necessary and sufficient for PAC learnability, with matching upper and lower sample-complexity bounds: an exact characterisation, which is why its numerical failure on deep networks cannot be argued away.
Vector quantisation XXX §21
Replacing a whole vector with the nearest of K codebook entries, rather than rounding each dimension independently. Beats scalar quantisation at matched bitrate by 6.19× on strongly correlated data, and still by 1.51× when the dimensions are independent, because a hypercube is a poor cell whatever the data looks like. So decorrelating the data first does not make scalar quantisation equivalent.
Verifier XXXIII §40
Anything that scores a candidate solution. Sound means it never accepts a wrong answer, complete that it never rejects a right one; soundness is what makes search over its outputs safe. An outcome verifier scores finished answers, a process verifier scores each step, and only the second can guide a search over partial solutions.
Vocoder XXX §19
A model turning a spectrogram back into a waveform. It is not recovering the phase that was there but generating one consistent with the magnitude and with what it has learned about the signal, which is why a learned vocoder beats the iterative estimate of Figure 2, a different task with a different failure mode.
Warmup III §17
Ramping the learning rate up from a small value over the first few hundred to few thousand steps, before switching to the main schedule. In adaptive optimisers such as Adam, the squared-gradient accumulators start at zero and their estimates are unreliable early in training; a large initial step can produce a destructively large parameter update. Warmup avoids this while the accumulators fill in.
Weight decay I §15
Adding $\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$ to the loss to discourage large weights. Equivalent to a Gaussian prior on the weights.
Weighted A* XXXIII §11
A* with $f=g+w\,h$ for $w\ge1$. Inadmissible by construction, so optimality is replaced by a guarantee of at most $w$ times optimal: a bound that in practice sits far above what happens, so the weight should be chosen by measuring rather than by deciding what suboptimality you can tolerate.
Weight-space symmetry IX §7
Permuting units within a layer, or scaling a ReLU unit's input weights by $\alpha$ and its output weights by $1/\alpha$, leaves the function unchanged. Produces $n!^{\,m}$ arrangements and a continuum of exactly equivalent minima.
Windowing XXVII §21
Multiplying a record by a function tapering to zero at both ends, so that the transform's implied periodic extension has no discontinuity. Hann, Hamming and Blackman trade main-lobe width against skirt height. Not optional in a careful spectral measurement: the unwindowed default is a rectangular window, which is the worst of them.
Worst-group risk XXIII §43
The largest risk over a set of predefined groups, rather than the average over the population. Figure 7 of Part XXIII measures an average-minimising model at 0.215 on the conflicting group (below chance, so systematically inverted), while the group-robust model reaches 0.795 and a better overall score.
XOR VII §1
The function that is 1 when its two binary inputs differ and 0 when they agree: four points arranged so no straight line separates the positives from the negatives. The example that ended the first connectionist era: Minsky and Papert showed a single-layer perceptron cannot compute it, which was read, incorrectly, as a verdict on neural networks in general. A single hidden layer solves it exactly.
ZeRO XVII §18
Sharding the redundant per-device copies in data-parallel training: stage 1 the optimiser states, stage 2 also the gradients, stage 3 also the parameters. FSDP is stage 3. Activations do not shard, which is why the memory saving flattens well short of the device count.
