Part DCoverage without assumptions

Every method in movement C produces an interval whose coverage is an empirical question. Conformal prediction produces one whose coverage is a theorem (for any model, any distribution, any sample size) requiring a single assumption. This movement measures that it holds, and measures what happens when the assumption does not.

§24 The construction

Go and read

Angelopoulos & Bates (2023), A gentle introduction to conformal prediction and distribution-free uncertainty quantification. The clearest available entry point: §2 states the coverage proof in a single paragraph (essentially the exchangeability-and-ranks argument below with the bookkeeping made explicit) and later sections cover the classification sets of §28 and the adaptive variants beyond what this movement develops. Read it once and implement it the same afternoon; the algorithm is short enough that reading and coding it take about the same time, which is the paper's own argument for why the method is trusted.

Split conformal, in four steps

1. Fit any model on a training split. It can be anything; nothing about it is assumed.
2. On a held-out calibration set, compute a nonconformity score for each point: for regression, $s_i = |y_i - \hat{f}(x_i)|$.
3. Take $\hat{q}$ as the $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil / n$ empirical quantile of those scores.
4. Predict the interval $\hat{f}(x) \pm \hat{q}$.

Then $P(y_{n+1} \in C(x_{n+1})) \geq 1 - \alpha$, in finite samples, with no asymptotics.

Why it works, in one sentence

If the calibration scores and the test score are exchangeable, the test score is equally likely to land in any position among them; so it falls below the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile with probability at least $1-\alpha$. That is the whole proof, and it is about ranks rather than about the model, which is why the model is unconstrained.

The small correction $(n+1)$ rather than $n$ is what makes it exact in finite samples rather than approximate.

§25 The guarantee, measured

What "distribution-free" buys here that it did not in Part XXIII

Part XXIII's distribution-free bounds were vacuous at realistic sizes. These are not, and the difference is what is being bounded: there, the risk of every hypothesis in a class, simultaneously; here, the coverage of one interval for one new point.

The weaker claim is achievable. That is the lesson worth carrying: a guarantee is worthless or valuable depending on how much it tries to cover, and the useful ones are usually narrower than the ones people wish for.

§26 Marginal, not conditional

Pitfall: the guarantee is averaged over inputs

Coverage is $P(y \in C(x)) \geq 1-\alpha$ where the probability is over both the test point and its input. It is not $P(y \in C(x) \mid x) \geq 1-\alpha$ for every $x$.

So a constant-width interval can cover 90% overall while covering 99% of easy inputs and 60% of hard ones, which is Part XXIII §43's worst-group problem, arriving in a method that looks like it has a guarantee. The guarantee is real and it is marginal.

Exact conditional coverage is impossible without assumptions, which is a theorem rather than an open problem. §27's repairs are approximations to it.

§27 Making the width adapt

Three ways to stop the interval being constant

Normalised scores. Divide by a predicted difficulty: $s_i = |y_i - \hat{f}(x_i)| / \hat{\sigma}(x_i)$, with $\hat{\sigma}$ from §5's heteroscedastic head. The interval becomes $\hat{f}(x) \pm \hat{q}\,\hat{\sigma}(x)$, and the marginal guarantee survives however bad $\hat{\sigma}$ is.

Conformalised quantile regression. Fit models of the $\alpha/2$ and $1-\alpha/2$ quantiles directly, then conformalise the residual. Adapts well and is the usual recommendation for regression.

Group-conditional. Calibrate separately within each of a set of predefined groups, giving the guarantee within each. This is the direct repair for §26's problem when you know which groups you care about; and Part XXIII §43's caveat applies: you need the group labels.

Not developed here: jackknife+ and CV+ get the same finite-sample guarantee without giving up half the data to a calibration split, by leaving out one point (or one fold) at a time. Worth knowing exists if a held-out calibration set is the expensive part.

§28 Classification, and sets rather than intervals

Prediction sets

For classification the output is a set of labels guaranteed to contain the truth with probability $1-\alpha$. Score each class by $1 - \hat{p}(y \mid x)$ and include every class scoring below the calibrated threshold.

The set's size is then an interpretable difficulty signal: one label means confident, five means the model has narrowed it to five, and the empty set (possible at large $\alpha$, that is, at low nominal coverage, where the calibrated inclusion bar is strict enough that even the top-scoring class can fail to clear it) means nothing scored well enough, which is a useful thing for a system to be able to say.

This is the natural implementation of Part XIV §4's option to refuse: refuse when the set is too large to act on.

§29 Where the guarantee ends

Go and read

Tibshirani et al. (2019), Conformal prediction under covariate shift. The source of the weighted-conformal repair below: reweight each calibration score by the likelihood ratio between the training and test covariate distributions and the exchangeability argument goes through essentially unchanged. Read it for the positivity condition it states carefully (every test input needs nonzero density under the calibration distribution, or no weighting recovers it), which is the same requirement Part XXIII §40 gives for importance weighting generally, arriving here as the reason a moved distribution cannot always be repaired.

Reading this next to Figure 4

Figure 4 says the guarantee survives an arbitrarily bad model. Figure 5 says it does not survive a moved distribution. Those are the two halves of what conformal prediction is: enormous robustness to model misspecification, no robustness at all to the exchangeability assumption.

Which means the assumption to monitor is not "is my model still good" (that is handled) but "is the data still like the calibration set", which is Part XXIII §44's detection problem and Part XXI §35's monitoring.

What is available when it is broken

Weighted conformal. If the shift is covariate shift and you can estimate the likelihood ratio, reweight the calibration scores and the guarantee is restored: Part XXIII §40's importance weighting, with the same positivity requirement.

Recalibrate continuously. With labels arriving, roll the calibration set forward. This is the practical answer and it needs labels.

Report the shift. If neither is available, the honest move is to state that the coverage guarantee is conditional on an assumption you are no longer able to check.

§30 What conformal does not give you

Four things, stated so they are not assumed

Conditional coverage: §26. Marginal only, unless you group-condition.

A good model: Figure 4's constant predictor is valid and useless. Width is the model's job.

Robustness to shift: Figure 5.

The two kinds of uncertainty separately: the interval is a total, and movement A's decomposition is not recoverable from it.

§31 What movement D establishes

Three things

A finite-sample coverage guarantee is available for any model. Figure 4 hits its nominal rate to within 0.002 at five levels, for a sixth-degree fit and for a predictor that ignores the input.

The model decides the width, not the validity. Which relocates the modelling question cleanly: a better model gives a narrower interval, and never a wrong one.

Exchangeability is the entire assumption, and it fails silently: Figure 5's coverage reaches 0.496 against a promised 0.90 with no signal from the method itself.
