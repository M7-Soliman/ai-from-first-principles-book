§5 The feedforward network

In 60 seconds

A chain of layers, each an affine map followed by a nonlinearity: $\mathbf{h}^{(k)} = g\big(W^{(k)}\mathbf{h}^{(k-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(k)}\big)$. Information flows one way, input to output, with no cycles, hence feedforward. The whole network is one composed function, and Part 0 §17 already told you how to differentiate those.

Watch the shapes

With a batch of $n$ examples and layer widths $d_0 \to d_1 \to d_2$:

$X$ is $n \times d_0$.   $W^{(1)}$ is $d_0 \times d_1$.   $XW^{(1)}$ is $n \times d_1$, and $\mathbf{b}^{(1)}$ broadcasts across the rows.

Every activation carries the batch dimension first; every weight matrix maps one width to the next. If a shape ever fails to chain, the fault is almost always a transposed weight matrix or a forgotten batch axis.

§6 Output units: match the distribution

The last layer is not a free choice. It is determined by what kind of thing you are predicting, and Part II supplies the answer in every case.

Why this matters: the table is derived, not conventional

Every row follows from Part II §26. Pick the distribution your target actually has, write down its negative log-likelihood, and both the output activation and the loss fall out together.

A Bernoulli needs a parameter in $(0,1)$, so you need a squashing function: that is the sigmoid. Its negative log-likelihood is cross-entropy. Neither was chosen; both were implied. The same derivation gives softmax and its loss for $k$ classes, and squared error for a Gaussian.

Pitfall: never apply softmax then take the log

Frameworks provide a fused cross_entropy_with_logits and it expects raw logits, not probabilities. Applying softmax yourself and passing the result applies it twice, flattening your distribution toward uniform.

The fusion exists for the numerical reason in Part III §4: computing $\log(\text{softmax}(z))$ directly avoids taking the log of a probability that may have underflowed to exactly zero. This is the single most common implementation bug in classification code.

§7 Hidden units

Go and read

Glorot, Bordes & Bengio (2011), Deep sparse rectifier neural networks. The case for the rectifier, argued at the moment it was still contentious, when replacing a smooth saturating nonlinearity with something that is not differentiable at the origin looked reckless. Then Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton (2012) on ImageNet, best read as an artefact: rectifiers, dropout, two GPUs and scale, combined, and the argument about whether depth worked ended in a single result.

Output units are determined. Hidden units are a genuine design choice, and the modern answer is short: use ReLU unless you have a reason not to.

ReLU, $\max(0, x)$
The default. Derivative is exactly 1 when active, so it neither shrinks nor amplifies the gradient. Cheap, and it produces genuinely sparse activations. Its flaw is §19's dead units.
Leaky ReLU: a small slope for negative inputs
Fixes dead units by ensuring the derivative is never exactly zero. Costs almost nothing and helps sometimes.
tanh: squashes to $(-1, 1)$
Zero-centred, which helps optimisation, but saturating. Reasonable in shallow networks and in recurrent architectures, where its boundedness is a feature.
GELU and relatives: smooth, roughly ReLU-shaped
Common in transformers. The smoothness near zero appears to help, and the improvement over ReLU is real but modest.
History: the simplest function took longest to adopt

ReLU is $\max(0,x)$. It requires no exponentials, has a trivial derivative, and can be written in one line. It was not the default until roughly 2012.

The reasons it was resisted are instructive. It is not differentiable at zero, which looked disqualifying to anyone with a mathematical training. It is unbounded above, which seemed likely to cause instability. And it produces exact zeros, which looked like discarding information. Meanwhile the sigmoid had a smooth derivative, a probabilistic interpretation, and biological analogy on its side.

Every objection was reasonable and every one turned out not to matter. The non-differentiable point is never hit in floating point; the unboundedness is what prevents saturation; the zeros are useful sparsity. What settled it was empirical: deep networks with ReLU simply trained, and deep networks with sigmoids largely did not.

It is a clean case of the pattern this book keeps meeting: mathematical propriety and what works are different criteria, and the field has repeatedly had to be dragged toward the second.

§8 Architecture

Depth, width, and how the layers connect. There is far less theory here than you might expect, and the honest summary is that architecture is chosen empirically.

A few things are known:

  • Depth helps, up to a point that keeps moving. The limits have been practical (gradient flow) rather than representational, and each fix has raised the ceiling.
  • Width is cheaper than depth to train and more expensive in parameters for the same capacity.
  • Residual connections (adding a layer's input to its output) make very deep networks trainable, essentially by giving the gradient a path that skips the multiplication. Section 18 explains why that works.
  • The right architecture encodes what you know about the data. That is Part VI §7: convolution for spatial locality, recurrence or attention for sequences. Architecture is where your prior goes.
Try it

You are predicting a house price from 12 features. What output unit and loss? Now the same network must instead predict which of 5 neighbourhoods a house is in. What changes, and what does not?

Answer. A price is a positive real number. The default choice is a linear output with squared error, the Gaussian likelihood. If you care more about relative than absolute error, predicting $\log(\text{price})$ is usually better, which also enforces positivity automatically.

For five neighbourhoods: a softmax output over 5 units with cross-entropy loss. The final weight matrix becomes $d \times 5$ instead of $d \times 1$.

What does not change: every hidden layer, every activation, the optimiser, the training loop, and the backward pass. Only the last layer and the loss are determined by the target, which is the whole content of §6, and the reason the same code trains both.

What this part does not cover

Goodfellow ch06 discusses several further activation functions (maxout, which learns its own piecewise-linear form; absolute-value rectification; hard-tanh; and radial basis functions) and additional output types including fully parametrised mixture density networks and the softmax overparametrisation argument ($n$ outputs versus $n-1$). On the theoretical side, the formal Borel-measurability version of universal approximation and the tight exponential-regions bounds of Montúfar et al. (2014) are not derived. None of these is needed for what follows; the drills and the project do not use them.
