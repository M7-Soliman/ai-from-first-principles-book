Part BUniform convergence

A class with infinitely many hypotheses can still have finitely much freedom, because what matters is not how many functions it contains but how many distinct labellings it can produce on a finite sample. That observation replaces the count in §4's bound with something that stays finite, and it is the central idea of classical learning theory.

§8 Probably approximately correct

The PAC framework

A class $\mathcal{H}$ is PAC learnable if there is an algorithm and a function $n(\varepsilon, \delta)$ such that for every distribution, given $n(\varepsilon, \delta)$ samples, the algorithm returns $h$ with $R(h) \leq \min_{h'} R(h') + \varepsilon$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$.

Three commitments are worth noticing. Approximately: within $\varepsilon$, not exactly. Probably: with probability $1-\delta$, not always. And for every distribution, which is the demanding one: the guarantee is required to hold for the worst distribution an adversary could choose, which is why the resulting bounds are pessimistic.

§9 Counting labellings instead of functions

The growth function

For a sample of $m$ points, the class can produce some set of labellings. The growth function $\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(m)$ is the largest number of distinct labellings achievable on any $m$ points.

It is at most $2^m$. A class with continuum-many members can have a small growth function: linear classifiers in the plane contain infinitely many functions and produce at most 14 labellings of four points, not 16.

Replace $|\mathcal{H}|$ with $\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(2n)$ in §4's bound and it applies to infinite classes. Everything now turns on how fast the growth function grows.

§10 Shattering, and the VC dimension

VC dimension

A set of $m$ points is shattered if the class realises all $2^m$ labellings of it. The VC dimension is the largest $m$ for which some set of $m$ points is shattered.

The definition is existential in the points and universal in the labellings: some arrangement of $m$ points, every labelling of it.

Why the collapse is total rather than gradual

The transition in the figure is from 1.00 to 0.00 with nothing between. Past $d+1$ points in general position there is always at least one labelling no hyperplane achieves: for four points in the plane, the diagonal pairing of a convex quadrilateral, which is the XOR arrangement of Part VII §1.

So the VC dimension is not a statistical quantity that gets fuzzy at the edges. It is a combinatorial fact about the class, and Part VII's opening problem is the $d = 2, m = 4$ case of it.

§11 Sauer's lemma, and why the bound is polynomial

The dichotomy

If the VC dimension is $d$, then for $m > d$

$$\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(m) \;\leq\; \sum_{i=0}^{d} \binom{m}{i} \;\leq\; \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^{d}$$

This is the load-bearing combinatorial fact of the subject. The growth function is either $2^m$ for all $m$ (infinite VC dimension, no learning guarantee) or it is bounded by a polynomial of degree $d$. There is nothing in between.

Polynomial matters because $\log \Pi$ then grows like $d \log m$ rather than $m$, and $\sqrt{d \log m / m} \to 0$ while $\sqrt{m/m}$ does not.

§12 The generalisation bound

Go and read

Vapnik & Chervonenkis (1971), On the uniform convergence of relative frequencies of events to their probabilities. The paper the subject is built on. Worth reading for the shape of the argument — a combinatorial fact about labellings converted into a statistical rate — which is still the template every later bound follows.

What it says

With probability at least $1-\delta$, for every $h \in \mathcal{H}$ simultaneously:

$$R(h) \;\leq\; \hat{R}_S(h) + \sqrt{\frac{d\left(\log(2n/d) + 1\right) + \log(4/\delta)}{n}}$$

Three properties to notice. It is uniform over the class, so it survives choosing $h$ after seeing the data. It depends on the class only through $d$. And it is distribution-free, which is its strength and the reason §23 finds it numerically useless.

Simplified constant: the textbook (Vapnik/Mohri) statement of this bound carries $\sqrt{8(d\ln(2en/d)+\ln(4/\delta))/n}$ rather than the coefficient-1, denominator-$n$ form above, harmless here, since §23 shows the bound is vacuous regardless of which constant is used.

§13 Sample complexity, and the fundamental theorem

Go and read

Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David (2014), Understanding Machine Learning: From Theory to Algorithms. The standard modern treatment, and the place to go for the fundamental theorem stated properly with both directions. Chapters 3 to 6 cover this part's movements A and B at full rigour.

What the theorem settles

Inverting the bound: $n = O\!\left(\frac{d + \log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^2}\right)$ samples suffice. Roughly, you need on the order of one sample per unit of VC dimension.

The fundamental theorem of statistical learning makes this an equivalence: for binary classification, a class is PAC learnable if and only if its VC dimension is finite, and the sample complexity is $\Theta(d/\varepsilon^2)$. Not a sufficient condition that might be improvable: the matching lower bound says no algorithm does better.

The lower bound is the part that should have been alarming

The upper bound could have been an artefact of a loose proof. The lower bound says that for any algorithm there exists a distribution requiring that many samples.

Which means the vacuity §23 measures is not a defect in the analysis. Uniform convergence genuinely cannot explain why an overparameterised network generalises, because a theorem says no distribution-free argument over that class can. Something has to be assumed about the distribution or the algorithm, and movement D is the search for what.

§14 No free lunch, restated

The same theorem from the other side

Part VI §7 said no learner beats every other across all problems. In this language: a class with infinite VC dimension, one that can label any finite sample any way at all, is not PAC learnable, because fitting the sample tells you nothing about the rest.

Restriction is not a regrettable limitation of a model family. It is the mechanism by which generalisation happens, and Part X §11's "infinitely strong prior" is one instance of buying it deliberately.

§15 What movement B establishes

Three things

Infinite classes can have finite complexity, because only the labellings realisable on a finite sample matter. Figure 2 measures that number collapsing from everything to not-everything at exactly $d+1$.

The growth function is polynomial or maximal, with nothing between. Sauer's lemma is what converts a combinatorial fact into a $\sqrt{d \log n / n}$ rate.

The characterisation is exact and two-sided. Finite VC dimension is necessary and sufficient, with a matching lower bound, which means the theory's later failure on deep networks is a theorem about what distribution-free analysis can do, not a gap waiting to be closed.
