Part EMaking data

Six ways to have more data than you collected. Each adds something and none adds information that was not already there, which is the constraint the whole movement is organised around.

§26 Augmentation, revisited

Part VIII §10 treated augmentation as a regulariser: transform the inputs in ways that should not change the label, and the model learns the invariance. Seen from this part it is something slightly different: a way of injecting knowledge you have and the data does not show.

Why augmentation is the only entry in this movement that adds anything

A rotation-augmented dataset encodes a fact, that this task is rotation-invariant, which came from you, not from the data. That is genuinely new information in the model, and it is why augmentation is the one technique here that reliably improves generalisation rather than merely rearranging what you had.

The condition is that the invariance must be true. Part VIII §10's warning: a vertical flip is a valid augmentation for aerial photography and destroys handwritten digits, because 6 and 9 are not the same character. An augmentation that is false injects false knowledge, confidently.

And it is bounded. Augmentation cannot show the model a category it has never seen, or a condition your frame excluded. It expands each example; it does not extend the frame.

§27 Synthetic data

Generate examples with a model, or a simulator, or a program, and train on them. This is the most-discussed data strategy of the moment and it needs its central limit stated plainly.

What synthetic data can and cannot supply

Can: more examples of structure the generator already knows. Coverage of rare configurations you can specify. Labels that are exact by construction: a simulator knows the ground truth it rendered. Cheap volume where volume is the constraint.

Cannot: information about the world that was not in the generator. A model's outputs are a resampling of what it learned; a simulator's outputs are a resampling of its designer's assumptions.

The clean statement: synthetic data can transfer and reorganise information, and cannot create it. Everything useful and everything dangerous about it follows from that.

Why it nonetheless works, when it works

Given the limit, why is synthetic data useful at all? Three legitimate mechanisms, all of which are transfers rather than creations.

From a bigger model to a smaller one. Distillation (§29). The information came from the teacher's training data.

From a program to a network. A renderer, a physics engine, a solver: the information came from the person who wrote it, and it is often information you genuinely have and cannot otherwise express as examples.

From an expensive process to a cheap one. Verification is often easier than generation: you can generate many candidate solutions and keep the ones a checker validates. The information came from the checker.

Notice that in each case you can name the source. If you cannot name where the information came from, it did not come from anywhere, and §28 is what happens then.

§28 Model collapse

In 60 seconds

Train on your own outputs, repeatedly, and the distribution degrades. The usual explanation, that sampling error compounds, turns out to be much weaker than advertised. The mechanisms that actually do the damage are filtering and model mismatch, and both are things real pipelines do deliberately.

Go and read

Shumailov et al. (2024), AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data. The paper that named it. Read it alongside Figure 5, which finds the sampling-error mechanism much weaker than the popular summary suggests and locates the damage in filtering and model mismatch instead. Then Gerstgrasser et al. (2024), which shows that collapse largely disappears if generated data accumulates alongside the real data rather than replacing it, a difference in pipeline design, not in mathematics.

Why the folk version does not reproduce, and what does

The standard account is that each generation's sample statistics are slightly off, and the errors compound. That is true and the magnitude is tiny: at $n=1000$ the sample standard deviation is biased low by about 0.025%, so twelve generations lose under 1% of the spread, far less than the run-to-run variation. This figure was built expecting a collapse and did not find one.

What does collapse it:

Filtering. Keep the "good" outputs (the typical ones, the ones a reward model scores highly, the ones that pass a check), and you are resampling from the middle of the distribution every time. Twelve generations of keeping the typical half leaves essentially a point mass. And this is not a hypothetical: filtering generated data for quality is exactly what every synthetic pipeline does.

Model mismatch. If the model cannot represent the data's structure, generation 1 already destroys it. The right panel is the important one: the two modes vanish immediately while the variance barely moves, so the summary statistic everybody monitors reports that nothing is wrong.

The practical conclusion is sharper than "avoid synthetic data". It is: the danger is in the selection step, not the generation step, and monitoring variance will not detect it. Watch the tails and the modes.

§29 Distillation

Train a small model on a large model's outputs: the mechanism in full, including the soft-target loss below, is Part XIX §24. Part IX §26's teacher hints did a narrower version of the same thing, treated as an optimisation aid; here it is a data-generation method, and the framing clarifies what it can achieve.

Why soft targets carry more than labels

A hard label says "cat". The teacher's full distribution says "cat 0.7, lynx 0.2, dog 0.05", which encodes the teacher's view of how the classes relate, on this example. That is substantially more information per example than the label, and it is why distillation outperforms training the student on the original labels.

The information budget is unchanged: it all originated in the teacher's training data. What distillation does is transfer it more efficiently, which is a real and useful thing that is not the same as creating anything.

And the student inherits everything: the teacher's biases, its shortcuts (§8), and its memorised training data. A student distilled from a contaminated teacher is contaminated, and no examination of the student's own training data will show it.

§30 Active learning

If labelling is the bottleneck, label the most informative examples first. Train, find the examples the model is least certain about, label those, repeat.

Pitfall: you have built a feedback loop over your own frame

Active learning works, and it introduces the failure of §7 by construction.

The examples selected are chosen by the current model. So the labelled set is no longer a sample from your frame; it is a sample from the region the model found confusing, which depends on the model's initialisation and on everything it has already been shown.

Two consequences. The labelled set is not a valid evaluation set for anything, and it is not a valid training set for a different model architecture, which may find different things confusing. And a category the model is confidently wrong about will never be selected, because confidence is what the selection is keyed on, so active learning systematically under-samples exactly the errors you most want to find.

The standard mitigation is to reserve a fraction of the labelling budget for uniform random selection. It costs a little efficiency and it is what keeps you able to measure anything.

§31 Annotation as a system

The last method of making data is the oldest: pay people to produce it. It is a system with failure modes, and treating it as a black box that emits labels is how §4's systematic noise gets in.

What determines annotation quality
  • The guideline. The actual specification of the task (§6). Ambiguity here becomes irreducible disagreement there.
  • Who annotates. Expertise, incentives, and whether they share the assumptions the task depends on. Annotators recruited from one population share its blind spots.
  • How they are paid. Per item rewards speed. Per hour rewards care. This shows up in the data.
  • Feedback. Annotators who never learn whether they were right cannot improve, and drift.
  • Adjudication. What happens on disagreement, and whether the disagreement is recorded (§5) or discarded.
Why the guideline is a piece of engineering

It is tempting to treat the guideline as documentation and the labels as the artefact. It is the other way round: §6 established that the labels are the task, and the guideline is the specification the labels implement.

Which means it should be versioned, tested against examples, revised when disagreement reveals ambiguity, and, critically, re-run on old data when it changes. A dataset labelled under three successive guidelines and merged is three tasks in a trenchcoat, and nothing downstream will tell you.
