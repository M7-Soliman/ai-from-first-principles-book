BuildThe project

Drills prove you can do the steps. A project proves you can hold the whole thing in your head at once. This one takes most people six to ten hours, and it is the difference between having read Part I and owning it.

P.1 What you are building

tinylinalg, a small linear algebra library written from scratch, then used to compress real images.

The rule that makes this worth doing: you may use NumPy for array storage, indexing, and elementwise arithmetic only. No @, no np.dot, no np.linalg anything, until the final verification step where you compare against them. If a function you want already exists in np.linalg, that is precisely the function you have to write.

P.2 Stage 1: the core operations

Implement, in tinylinalg/core.py:

FunctionNotes
matmul(A, B)Explicit loops. Raise ValueError with a message naming both shapes on mismatch.
matvec(A, x)Write it twice (once row-wise, once column-wise) and assert they agree. This is §4 made executable.
transpose(A)Without .T.
norm(x, p)Support $p = 1, 2, \infty$ and 'fro'.
outer(u, v)Should return a rank-1 matrix. Verify that it does.

Deliverable: every function agrees with its NumPy counterpart to within np.allclose tolerance on 100 random inputs of assorted shapes, including non-square and degenerate ones.

P.3 Stage 2: solving

In tinylinalg/solve.py:

  • gaussian_elimination(A, b): solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ with partial pivoting (at each step, swap in the row with the largest pivot). Write it first without pivoting, find an input that breaks it, then add pivoting and watch that input start working. Keep both versions; the comparison is the lesson.
  • rank(A): via row reduction, with a tolerance parameter. Explain in a comment why an exact == 0 test is wrong here.
  • inverse(A): by solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{e}_i$ for each basis vector. Then write a docstring explaining why nobody should call this function (§13).

Deliverable: a short write-up of the input that broke your unpivoted solver, and an explanation of why pivoting fixes it.

P.4 Stage 3: eigenvalues

In tinylinalg/eigen.py:

  • power_iteration(A, iters): the largest eigenvalue and its eigenvector, using only matvec and norm. Return the convergence history.
  • deflate(A, lam, v): remove a known eigenpair via $A - \lambda \mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^\top$, so power iteration can find the next one.
  • top_k_eigen(A, k): the two combined.

Deliverable: a plot of convergence rate for matrices with eigenvalue ratios $\lambda_2/\lambda_1$ of $0.1$, $0.5$, and $0.99$. Your plot should visibly confirm the prediction from drill S1: convergence speed is governed by that ratio, and the $0.99$ case should be painfully slow.

P.5 Stage 4: the application

Now use what you built. In compress.py:

  1. Load a greyscale image as a matrix (any photo; larger than 256×256).
  2. Compute its top $k$ principal components using your own eigen code.
  3. Reconstruct from $k \in \{1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100\}$.
  4. Produce a single figure: original plus all six reconstructions, each labelled with its compression ratio and reconstruction error.
  5. Plot explained variance against $k$ and mark where it crosses 90%, 95%, 99%.

Deliverable: the figure, and a paragraph answering: at what $k$ does the image become visually acceptable to you, and how does that compare to where the variance curve flattens? They usually do not match, and thinking about why is the point.

P.6 Stretch goals

  • QR via Gram–Schmidt, then use it for a more stable least squares than the normal equations. Compare on an ill-conditioned system.
  • Jacobi eigenvalue algorithm for symmetric matrices; it finds all eigenpairs at once, and is genuinely elegant.
  • Benchmark your matmul against NumPy's. Expect to be 100–1000× slower, then find out why: BLAS, cache blocking, SIMD. This is a worthwhile detour into why the same mathematics can differ by three orders of magnitude in speed.
  • Eigenfaces: run your PCA on a face dataset and visualise the top components. They look unsettling and are historically important.

P.7 How to know you are done

  • Every function is tested against NumPy on random inputs, including edge cases.
  • Shape mismatches raise clear errors naming both shapes, not IndexError.
  • The pivoting write-up identifies a concrete failing input.
  • The convergence plot shows the predicted dependence on eigenvalue ratio.
  • The compression figure is labelled well enough that someone else could read it without your explanation.
  • Your README explains what you built and one thing that surprised you.
Why this project and not another

Every stage forces a concept from the chapter to become concrete. Writing matmul twice makes the row and column views of §4 into two pieces of working code that must agree. Breaking your own solver teaches conditioning (§13) in a way no amount of reading does. Power iteration makes eigenvectors mechanical rather than mystical. And image compression makes low-rank approximation something you can look at: the moment a photograph reappears from 25 numbers per row is when the SVD stops being notation.

AssessCheckpoint

Part II (probability) does not lean on Part I too heavily, but Parts III and IV, and everything after do. Be honest here; a gap you patch now costs an hour; the same gap found in Part VIII costs a week.

Can you do these without notes?

  • Given any two shapes, say instantly whether they can be multiplied and what the result's shape is.
  • Explain $A\mathbf{x}$ in all three views of §4, and say which is best for reasoning about solvability.
  • Compute a $2\times2$ or $3\times3$ matrix-vector product by hand, correctly, first time.
  • Say what rank means without using the word "independent", then again using it.
  • Explain why $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ might have no solution, using the column space.
  • State why the count of solutions is always 0, 1, or infinity.
  • Define $L^1$, $L^2$, $L^\infty$ and explain why $L^1$ produces sparsity.
  • Explain why deep learning prefers $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2$ over $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2$.
  • State what makes a matrix orthogonal and give the two consequences that follow.
  • Define an eigenvector without writing the equation (in words).
  • Explain what $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ does, factor by factor.
  • Connect positive definiteness to the shape of a surface, and to minima.
  • Say what the SVD does geometrically, in one sentence.
  • Explain why $\det(A) = 0$ means non-invertible, using the volume picture.
  • Derive PCA: from "find the direction of maximum variance" to "top eigenvector of the covariance".
