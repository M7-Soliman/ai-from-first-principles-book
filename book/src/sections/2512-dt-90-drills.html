PracticeDrills

Thirty-one problems. Most of this part is about noticing things, so a disproportionate number of these ask you to construct a failure deliberately and watch it not show up in the metrics.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · How large a corpus is

A corpus of 109 tokens, stored as uint16 ids. Write down the size on disk, and roughly how many books that corresponds to.

Answer. 2 GB, and about 10,000 books at 100,000 tokens each. Worth holding on to as a unit: frontier pretraining corpora are measured in trillions of tokens, so they are thousands of times a small library and their curation cannot be manual. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

WarmW1 · Three things called "the data"

Name them, and say which gap statistics can help with.

Answer. Population, frame, sample. Statistics addresses frame-to-sample only. The population-to-frame gap is a modelling assumption and no amount of data closes it.
WarmW2 · The central claim

What does more data improve, and what does it not?

Answer. Precision, not validity. Figure 1: at $n = 100{,}000$ a mis-framed estimate is wrong by 0.09 with an interval of $\pm 0.003$, tens of times its own width, and the interval no longer contains the truth.
WarmW3 · Three kinds of label noise

Name them and say which one models tolerate.

Answer. Random: tolerated well. Systematic: learned as signal, and not fixed by more data or more annotators drawn the same way. Irreducible: your task definition is incomplete.
WarmW4 · What high agreement means

Two annotators reach $\kappa = 0.9$. What does that establish?

Answer. That the task is well specified and the guideline is being followed. Not that the guideline is right: shared training produces shared systematic error, which is exactly the kind $\kappa$ cannot see.
WarmW5 · Noise tolerance

40% of training labels are flipped at random. Roughly what test accuracy is achievable, and what will training accuracy be?

Answer. Test around 0.84 on clean labels; training around 0.58. The boundary survives symmetric noise; what dies is your ability to measure, since a noisy test set caps the reportable score at about $1-p$.
WarmW6 · Five kinds of leakage

Name them.

Answer. Target, train–test contamination, temporal, preprocessing, group. Preprocessing is the one that survives review, because it is a two-line ordering mistake that inflates by a few tenths of a point.
WarmW7 · Imbalance

Give three fixes that are better than resampling, and the condition each addresses.

Answer. Change the metric (the metric was wrong). Move the threshold (the ranking is fine). Weight the loss (the costs are asymmetric). Resampling breaks calibration and, done before splitting, causes leakage.
WarmW8 · MinHash

Why does the match rate of two signatures estimate the Jaccard index?

Answer. Under a random permutation the minimum over $A \cup B$ is equally likely to be any element, so it falls in the intersection with probability exactly $|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|$. Each permutation is a Bernoulli trial; the error falls like $1/\sqrt{k}$.
WarmW9 · The memorisation threshold

How many copies of a sequence does it take?

Answer. Figure 6: nothing at 1–2, 35% at 4, perfect at 8, which is 0.12% of that corpus (the exact numbers at four and eight copies are torch-version and hardware dependent; the order of magnitude is not). Low enough that anything mirrored or syndicated crosses it by default.
WarmW10 · What actually collapses a model

Naive recursive retraining barely degrades. What does the damage?

Answer. Filtering: keeping the "good" outputs resamples the middle every generation. And model mismatch: structure the model cannot represent is gone at generation 1, while the variance barely moves and looks healthy.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Build the frame gap

Simulate a population of two groups with different rates. Estimate the overall rate from frames that under-represent one group by varying amounts, and plot the estimate with its interval against $n$.

Answer. The interval narrows like $1/\sqrt{n}$ in every case and the bias does not move, so past some $n$ the interval stops containing the truth. Find that $n$ for each frame: it is the point at which more data made you wrong more confidently.
CoreC2 · Measure noise tolerance

Sweep the label-flip rate and record training accuracy on noisy labels and test accuracy on clean ones.

Answer. Test accuracy stays high far longer than expected and collapses near 50%. Also sweep model capacity: a high-capacity model fits the noise and the two curves converge, which is Part VIII's story arriving from the data side.
CoreC3 · Asymmetric noise

Repeat C2 but flip labels in one direction only. Compare.

Answer. Much worse, and it does not recover with more data. Symmetric noise leaves the boundary alone; asymmetric noise moves it, and the model learns the shift as signal. This is why §4's distinction matters more than the noise rate.
CoreC4 · Build the contamination

Copy a controlled fraction of your test set into training. Report the score on the whole test set and on the uncontaminated part.

Answer. The reported score rises and the honest score falls: the copies displaced real training signal. Two errors in opposite directions, which is why nothing looks wrong.
CoreC5 · Preprocessing leakage, measured

Fit a scaler and a feature selector on all the data, then split. Compare against fitting inside the training fold.

Answer. A small inflation: a few tenths of a point, larger for feature selection than for scaling. Small is what makes it dangerous: it is the same size as the improvements being claimed.
CoreC6 · Group leakage

Generate data with several records per underlying unit. Compare a random split against a split by unit.

Answer. The random split reports a substantially better number. The gap between the two is the measurement: it quantifies how much of the model is recognising the unit rather than the signal.
CoreC7 · Implement MinHash

Shingle, hash, and estimate Jaccard. Verify against the exact value and measure the error against the number of permutations.

Answer. Mean error about 0.01 at 256 permutations, falling like $1/\sqrt{k}$. Note that the accuracy depends on the signature length only, not on corpus size, which is what makes it usable at any scale.
CoreC8 · Locality-sensitive hashing

Add banding on top of C7. Sweep the band count and plot the probability of a candidate pair against true similarity.

Answer. An S-curve whose threshold is roughly $(1/b)^{1/r}$ for $b$ bands of $r$ rows. The bands are an explicit precision–recall knob, and the point is that you choose the threshold rather than discovering it.
CoreC9 · Chance-corrected agreement

Implement Cohen's $\kappa$. Then construct two annotators who agree 90% of the time and have $\kappa$ near zero.

Answer. Take a 95/5 class distribution and two annotators who both almost always say "majority". High raw agreement, no information. This is why raw agreement is never reported alone.
CoreC10 · Per-class agreement

Construct a dataset where the aggregate $\kappa$ is 0.8 and the rare class's $\kappa$ is below 0.3.

Answer. Easy, and that is the point: the aggregate is dominated by the common class. Always report $\kappa$ per class, because the rare class is usually the one the system exists for.
CoreC11 · Deduplication's effect

Duplicate a subset of your training data by varying amounts and measure held-out performance.

Answer. Performance degrades as duplication rises at a fixed budget: you are spending gradient on a distribution you did not choose. Then check what the duplicated records did to memorisation, which is C13.
CoreC12 · Collapse, all three mechanisms

Fit-sample-refit for many generations. Do it plain, with a filter that keeps the typical half, and with a model class narrower than the data.

Answer. Plain barely collapses: under 1% in twelve generations at $n=1000$. Filtering collapses to a point mass. Mismatch destroys the structure at generation 1 while the variance is unchanged. Report the variance and a mode count, because only the second one sees it.
CoreC13 · Canaries

Insert random sequences at controlled frequencies, train, and try to extract them by prompting with a prefix.

Answer. A sharp threshold in the low single digits of copies. Note the loss at one copy is worse than chance: the model has learned the corpus rule and is confidently wrong, which is not the same as being ignorant.
CoreC14 · A dataset document

Write one for a dataset you already have, following §19. Fill in every section.

Answer. The test is the "known gaps" section: if you cannot fill it, you have described the dataset without documenting it. The second test: could a reader use your document to predict where the model fails?

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · Build a shortcut and watch it pass

Add a feature that correlates with the label in your data and would not in deployment. Train, evaluate normally, then evaluate on data where the correlation is broken.

Answer. Every standard metric reports success. Only the shifted evaluation finds it. Then ablate the feature and see how much accuracy survives: that number is how much of your model was the shortcut.
StretchS2 · The three splits

Take data with a nested structure: records within units within sites. Report accuracy under a random split, a unit split and a site split.

Answer. Three different numbers, all correct, answering three different questions. Only one estimates deployment at a new site. Report all three; the spread is more informative than any of them.
StretchS3 · Selection bias you cannot fix

Simulate the lending problem of §7: outcomes observed only for applicants a previous policy approved. Train and evaluate; then evaluate on the full population you simulated.

Answer. Excellent measured performance, poor true performance, and no statistic computed from the observed data detects it. The only fix is randomised decisions made before you had the problem, which is a design decision rather than an analysis one.
StretchS4 · Filter, then read what you removed

Apply a quality classifier to a corpus you assembled. Sample a hundred rejected documents and read them.

Answer. The exercise is the reading. Categorise what was removed and ask whether each category was intended. This is the only reliable way to discover that a filter is removing something you wanted, and no aggregate statistic substitutes for it.
StretchS5 · The curation frontier

Train at matched compute on a full corpus and on the top decile by some quality score. Compare; then compare again on an evaluation set the quality score's reference does not resemble.

Answer. The filtered model usually wins on the first comparison and may lose on the second. The gap between the two comparisons measures how much of the "quality" gain was the filter narrowing the frame toward the evaluation set.
StretchS6 · Active learning's blind spot

Run uncertainty-based active learning on data with a category the initial model is confidently wrong about. Track whether that category ever gets selected.

Answer. It does not, or barely. Selection is keyed on uncertainty, and confident errors have none, so the method systematically under-samples exactly the failures you most want to find. Reserve part of the budget for uniform random selection and compare.
