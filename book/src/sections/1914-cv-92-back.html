BuildThe project

Ten to fourteen hours. You write the convolution (forward, backward and fast), then use it to establish, by measurement, which of this part's claims are theorems and which are folklore.

P.1 What you are building

conv-from-scratch: a convolutional layer in NumPy with no framework anywhere in the forward or backward path, a working classifier built from it, and an instrument for looking at what the network has actually learned.

The rule: every gradient is checked against finite differences before it is trusted, and every performance claim is timed rather than counted.

P.2 Stage 1: the operation, three ways

Implement the same convolution three times and confirm they agree:

  • By explicit matrix. Build $M$ as in §4 and multiply. Correct, hopeless, and the reference the other two are checked against. Report its size, its nonzero count and its number of distinct values.
  • By loops over output positions.
  • By im2col plus a single matrix multiply.

Deliverable: the three agree to floating-point rounding; a timing table; and the measured size of the im2col buffer as a multiple of the input. Support stride, padding, dilation and groups, and verify the output-size formula of §5 against every combination.

P.3 Stage 2: backward, and the gradient check

Implement §16's other two operations: the gradient with respect to the kernel and the gradient with respect to the input.

  1. Verify that the input gradient equals a full-padding convolution with the flipped kernel, the exact result of §16, so demand exactness rather than a tolerance.
  2. Check both gradients against central finite differences on small random tensors.
  3. Repeat with stride 2, with asymmetric padding, and with groups greater than one. This is where the bugs are.

Deliverable: a gradient check that passes for every combination of stride, padding, dilation and groups you support, and a note on the step size at which the finite difference stops being the more accurate of the two; see Part III §3.

P.4 Stage 3: a network, and the properties it should have

Assemble a classifier and train it on data you generate, so you control the structure. Then measure the four properties this part claims:

  • Equivariance of the convolutional stack, for translations, rotations and scalings, excluding a border wide enough that wrap-around does not contaminate it.
  • Invariance of the pooled representation, with the reference unshifted. Get this distinction wrong once, deliberately, and see what it appears to show.
  • The receptive field, by backpropagating from a single output unit. Both the nonzero support and the 95% region, against depth.
  • The parameter and FLOP counts, against the formulas of §6.

Deliverable: Figures 3, 4 and 5 reproduced from your own code, and the measured log-log slope of the effective receptive field against depth.

P.5 Stage 4: degradation, and what fixes it

The experiment that produced ResNet, at a scale you can afford.

  1. Train plain networks at increasing depth without normalisation. Record the best training loss, and confirm it degrades.
  2. Add residual connections. Find the depth at which the unnormalised residual network diverges, and explain it from §23.
  3. Add normalisation to both. Report all four curves.

Deliverable: the 2×2 comparison, and a paragraph attributing the improvement between normalisation and the residual connection rather than to one of them. If your plain network reaches $\ln C$ for $C$ classes, say so: that is chance, and it is a more striking result than "worse".

P.6 Stage 5: look at what it learned

  1. Display the first-layer kernels. Fit Gabor functions and report the $R^2$ distribution.
  2. Build a Gabor quadrature pair from §31's formula, confirm orthogonality, and measure the complex cell's invariance as a stimulus translates.
  3. Regenerate your data with different structure (bars, then blobs, then something with natural-image statistics) and see how the learned filters change.

Deliverable: a statement of what the first layer learned and what that says about the data rather than about convolution. If your filters are not Gabors, that is a finding rather than a failure, and §31's pitfall says why.

P.7 Stretch

  • Locally connected against convolutional, at several training-set sizes. Find the crossover, or establish that there is none in your range.
  • Permute the pixels and retrain both a convolutional and a dense model. The asymmetry is §11's prior, priced.
  • Checkerboards. Build an encoder–decoder, produce the artefact deliberately by choosing $k \bmod s \neq 0$, then remove it two different ways.
  • Dilation and gridding. Compare dilation schedules $2,2,2,2$ and $1,2,5,9$ by looking at the gradient receptive field, not the accuracy.
  • Winograd for $3\times3$, against direct convolution: correctness, speed, and the precision you lose.

P.8 Done when

  • Three implementations of convolution agree to rounding, and one of them is fast.
  • The input gradient is shown to equal a flipped full convolution exactly.
  • Gradient checks pass with stride, padding, dilation and groups all non-trivial.
  • Translation equivariance measures at float32 rounding; rotation and scale do not.
  • Your effective receptive field grows measurably more slowly than $1+2L$.
  • The invariance measurement and the equivariance measurement are distinguished, in code.
  • You have a plain network at chance on its own training set, and know why.
  • Your README names one thing you believed about convolution that the measurements contradicted.
Why this project

Convolution is the operation people are most likely to have used for years and never implemented. That is usually harmless, and it stops being harmless at exactly the points this part is about: why the memory went where it went, why the receptive field is smaller than the formula, why the transpose needs to be told its output size, and why a deep network stops training.

Stage 2 is the one that changes things. Deriving that the backward pass of a convolution is a convolution takes ten minutes; getting it to agree with finite differences under stride and groups takes an afternoon, and afterwards you will never again be unsure what a framework is doing when you pass it those arguments.

The one thing to carry forward

An architecture is a symmetry you have decided the data has, encoded so firmly that the model cannot consider anything else.

Convolution encodes one (translation) and every property in this part follows: the parameter saving, the data efficiency, the exact equivariance, the invariance pooling buys, and all four of §32's limits.

Carry the question rather than the answer. For every layer you meet from here on: what has it been told, and is that true here? Part XI asks it of sequences, and Part XII of an architecture that was built by answering it with "almost nothing".
