Part BGetting it wrong

Seven failure modes. Each is common, each is silent, and each produces a number that looks fine. They are ordered roughly by how hard they are to notice, which is the reverse of how often they are discussed.

§7 Selection bias

The frame gap of §2, given its proper name and its worst instances.

Three shapes it takes

Survivorship. You observe only what survived. Loan defaults among people who were granted loans; equipment failures among machines still in service; user behaviour among users who did not leave.

Self-selection. Subjects chose to be in the data. Reviews are written by people moved to write; survey respondents differ from non-respondents in exactly the ways surveys are used to measure.

Feedback. The system's own past decisions determined what data exists. This is the one that gets worse over time, and Part XIV returns to it.

Worked: the loan model that cannot be evaluated

You have five years of lending records: applicant features, and whether they repaid. Train a model, hold out a year, measure accuracy. It looks good.

The outcome is only recorded for applicants who were granted a loan. Applicants who were rejected have no repayment outcome, so they are not in your data at all, and they were rejected by the previous policy, which used the very features you are modelling.

So your model is trained and evaluated on the region of input space where the old policy said yes. Its accuracy there may be excellent. Its behaviour on the rejected region is completely unconstrained by data and completely untested, and that region is precisely where a new model would differ from the old one, which is the only reason to build it.

The fix is not statistical. It is to have granted some loans at random, which someone must have decided to do years earlier.

§8 Shortcuts

Go and read

Geirhos et al. (2020), Shortcut learning in deep neural networks. The survey that gave the phenomenon its name and its taxonomy. Read it for the framing: a shortcut is not a bug in the model, it is a correct solution to the problem you posed, and the problem you posed is your dataset, not your intention.

A model minimises the loss. It has no preference for the feature you had in mind, and if something easier predicts the label, it will use that instead.

Shortcut learning

A feature that predicts the label in your dataset and does not correspond to the thing you meant. The model is not malfunctioning: the shortcut genuinely has lower loss on the data you supplied.

Standard instances: images of one class photographed on a different background; scans from the machine at the hospital that treats the sicker patients; a hospital identifier burned into a corner of the image; a document class that happens to be longer.

Why held-out data cannot detect this

The shortcut is in your test set too, because the test set came from the same frame. Every metric you have will report that the model works.

It fails in deployment, when the correlation that was an artefact of collection does not hold. And by then the score has been reported and believed.

Three things do detect it, all requiring deliberate work. Test on data from a different source: a different hospital, a different year, a different photographer. Ablate the suspected shortcut: mask the corner, crop the background, truncate to a fixed length, and see how much accuracy survives. And look at what the model is using, with the caveats Part XII §36 attached to that.

The general principle is Part V's: a number is only evidence about the conditions under which it was measured, and a test set drawn from the training frame measures the frame.

§9 Label noise, and how much a model absorbs

§4 distinguished random from systematic noise. Here is what random noise actually costs, and it is much less than intuition suggests.

Why random noise is survivable and what it destroys instead

Symmetric noise shifts the label distribution without moving the decision boundary: if 40% of labels are flipped, the majority label on each side of the boundary is still the correct one. A model with limited capacity fits the majority, which is the signal.

What noise destroys is your ability to measure. Notice the right panel: at 30% noise the model scores 0.918 on clean labels and 0.672 on its own training labels. If your test set carries the same noise, your reported accuracy is capped at roughly $1-p$ however good the model is, so you cannot tell a 0.92 model from a 0.99 one.

Two consequences worth acting on. Clean the test set even if you cannot clean the training set: relabelling a thousand test items carefully is cheap and buys you back the ability to measure. And be suspicious when training accuracy is far below test accuracy: that gap is a noise signature, not a bug.

None of this applies to systematic noise. A consistent bias moves the boundary, the model learns it as signal, and no amount of capacity or data corrects it.

§10 Imbalance

One class is 99% of the data. Accuracy 99% is achieved by a constant. This is the most discussed problem in the part and, usually, the least real.

Pitfall: resampling is the wrong fix for the usual problem

The standard advice is to oversample the minority or undersample the majority. Before doing either, ask what is actually wrong.

If the metric is wrong (you are reporting accuracy on a 99/1 split), the fix is the metric. Precision, recall, and the precision–recall curve, which Part XIV treats properly. Resampling to make accuracy meaningful is fixing the thermometer.

If the threshold is wrong (the model ranks well but predicts the majority everywhere), the fix is the threshold. A well-calibrated model with a moved threshold is at least as good as a resampled model at its default, and it stays movable: the threshold can be set per deployment, after training, without retraining.

If the cost is asymmetric (a missed fraud costs more than a false alarm), the fix is a weighted loss, which states the asymmetry explicitly rather than smuggling it in by duplicating rows.

If the minority is genuinely under-observed (you have forty examples of it), then resampling does not help either. You have a data problem and duplicating forty rows does not create information.

Oversampling also breaks calibration and, if done before splitting, causes §11's leakage in its most embarrassing form: the same row in train and test.

§11 Leakage

Go and read

Kapoor & Narayanan (2023), Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in machine-learning-based science. Surveys 294 papers across seventeen fields and finds leakage in a large fraction of them, with a taxonomy this section follows. The uncomfortable part is how ordinary the errors are: mostly the preprocessing and temporal cases, in published work that was reviewed.

Information about the target reaching the model that will not be available at prediction time. It is the most common cause of a result that is too good, and it comes in several shapes that are worth separating because they have different fixes.

The taxonomy

Target leakage. A feature computed from the outcome. A "days since account closed" field predicts churn perfectly and does not exist before churn.

Train–test contamination. The same or near-identical records on both sides. §12 and §13.

Temporal leakage. Training on data from after the test period. Any random split of a time series does this, and time series are far more common than people notice: user behaviour, prices, anything with a trend.

Preprocessing leakage. Fitting a scaler, an imputer, a vocabulary or a feature selector on the whole dataset before splitting. The test set's statistics have then informed the training pipeline.

Group leakage. Records that are not independent split across train and test: multiple images of the same patient, multiple sessions from the same user. Each record is distinct; the underlying unit is not.

Why preprocessing leakage is the one that survives review

The others are visible once you look. This one is a two-line ordering mistake that produces no warning and appears in a great deal of published work.

Wrong: scaler.fit(X); X = scaler.transform(X); split(X)

Right: split(X); scaler.fit(X_train); transform both

The effect is usually small, which is worse than if it were large: it inflates by a few tenths of a point, which is exactly the margin most papers are claiming. Fit every transformation inside the training fold, without exception, and let a pipeline object enforce it rather than your memory.

§12 Duplicates

Real corpora contain the same content many times. Web pages are mirrored, boilerplate repeats, articles are syndicated, code is vendored. Measured on large web corpora, a substantial fraction of documents have a near-duplicate elsewhere in the same corpus.

Three kinds, needing three tools

Exact. Byte-identical. A hash set finds them in one pass.

Near. Differing by a template, a date, a boilerplate header. §15's MinHash.

Semantic. The same content expressed differently: a translation, a paraphrase, the same fact restated. Embedding similarity finds some of it, and the notion is not well defined enough to have a complete answer.

Duplicates do two distinct kinds of damage, and it is worth keeping them apart. Within the training set they reweight it: a document repeated a thousand times is a thousand gradient contributions, so you are training on a distribution you did not choose, and §33 shows what that does to memorisation. Across the split they are contamination, which is §13.

§13 Contamination

In 60 seconds

Test material present in the training data. It has always existed; what changed is scale. When a corpus is most of the reachable web, it contains the benchmarks, and every published score on those benchmarks is measuring something other than generalisation, with no way to tell from the number.

Why the falling blue line is the part people miss

Contamination is usually described as inflating a score. Figure 3 shows it is worse than that: at a fixed training budget, every leaked test item is a training slot not spent on something new.

So you get a number that is too high and a model that is genuinely worse than the one you would have had. The two errors point in opposite directions, which is precisely why the problem is invisible: the metric moves the wrong way, so nothing looks wrong.

Pitfall: you cannot check a corpus you cannot see

Detecting contamination requires searching the training data for the test items. For a model whose corpus is not published, which is most of them, this check is impossible for anyone outside, including you if you did not build the corpus.

Three partial substitutes, all imperfect. Test on material created after the training cutoff, which is the strongest available evidence and gets weaker every month. Check whether the model can complete a benchmark item from its first few tokens, which detects memorisation but not paraphrase. And compare performance on a benchmark against a freshly constructed equivalent: a gap between them is the measurement, and it is Part XIV's instrument.
