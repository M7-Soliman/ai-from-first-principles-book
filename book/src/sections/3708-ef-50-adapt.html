Part EAdaptation

Not about making one model faster, but about making it affordable to have many (one per task, per customer, per experiment) without paying for a full copy of each.

§31 What full fine-tuning costs

Three costs, and the third is the one that bites

Memory during training. Part XVII §16's sixteen bytes per parameter, so a 7B model needs well over 100 GiB before activations.

Compute. A training run, though a short one.

Storage and serving, per variant. Every fine-tune is a complete copy of the model. Ten tasks is ten models to store, load and keep resident, and no two of them can share a batch, which §38 says is where the throughput comes from.

That third cost is what motivates this movement. Even where the first two are affordable, serving a hundred full fine-tunes is not.

§32 LoRA

Constrain the update to be low rank

Freeze $W_0$ and learn the update as a product of two thin matrices:

$$W = W_0 + BA, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\; A \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times k},\; r \ll \min(d,k)$$

with $A$ initialised randomly and $B$ at zero, so the model starts exactly at $W_0$ and the adaptation begins from no change at all.

At $r = 8$ on a 4096-wide matrix this is about 0.39% of the parameters: $r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}}) / (d_{\text{in}} d_{\text{out}}) = 2r/d$ for a square matrix. Gradients and optimiser states are needed only for those, so the memory cost of §31's first item nearly vanishes: the frozen base still needs a forward pass, but not the twelve bytes per parameter of Adam state.

Why it costs nothing at inference

$BA$ can be merged into $W_0$ after training, giving an ordinary dense matrix with no extra operations and no extra latency. Unlike an adapter layer (§35), which adds depth and therefore latency, LoRA's cost at serving time is exactly zero once merged.

And unmerged it is better still, which is §36's point: keeping the small matrices separate is what lets one base model serve many adaptations at once.

§33 Is the update actually low rank

Go and read

Hu et al. (2022), LoRA: low-rank adaptation of large language models. The method, and §7's analysis of the update's rank. Read that section against this one's figure and keep the distinction: the empirical claim is that the spectrum decays, and the claim usually made from it, that a rank-8 constraint therefore suffices, is a different and better-supported statement about the constrained optimisation.

Pitfall: "the update is low rank" is doing two jobs

The empirical claim is that the update's singular values decay quickly, which the figure confirms. The claim usually made from it, that a rank-8 approximation is therefore enough, does not follow, and is worth separating.

Energy is not effect. Capturing 90% of the squared magnitude of an update is not capturing 90% of its effect on the loss. A small-magnitude direction can matter a great deal.

And LoRA does not approximate the full update anyway. It constrains the update to be low rank from the start, and the optimiser then finds the best rank-$r$ update rather than the best projection of the unconstrained one. Those are different objects, and the second is better than the SVD analysis suggests.

So the honest version: the spectrum decays, the constraint costs less than it looks like it should, and the reason is not fully explained by the rank argument that is usually given for it.

§34 The variants

Go and read

Dettmers et al. (2023), QLoRA: efficient finetuning of quantized LLMs. Read for the two details a rank-and-scale summary skips: 4-bit NormalFloat, a quantization grid built for weights already known to be roughly Gaussian rather than uniform, and double quantization of the scales themselves. Together they are why a 4-bit frozen base costs noticeably less memory than the naive arithmetic suggests.

What each changes

QLoRA quantizes the frozen base to 4 bits and trains the adapters in 16, which is what makes fine-tuning a large model possible on one device. The base is frozen, so quantization error in it is a fixed offset the adapters can partly compensate for, which is why it costs less than quantizing a model you intend to keep training.

DoRA separates the update into magnitude and direction, which closes part of the gap to full fine-tuning.

Rank and placement are the two hyperparameters that matter. Higher rank helps up to a point; applying LoRA to all linear layers rather than only the attention projections reliably helps more than raising the rank does.

§35 The alternatives

Three other ways to adapt, and why LoRA displaced them

Adapters insert small bottleneck layers between blocks. They work, and they add depth, so they add latency at every forward pass, which cannot be merged away.

Prefix and prompt tuning learn continuous vectors prepended to the input. Extremely cheap and they consume context, and they are harder to optimise.

Prompting with no training at all. Free, immediate, and limited by what the model can be induced to do (the right first thing to try, and §37's point).

LoRA won on one property: zero inference cost when merged, and multi-tenancy when not. The others each pay something at serving time forever.

§36 Serving many adaptations

The property that made this a deployment technique

Keep one copy of the base model resident and hold hundreds of small adapter pairs. A batch can then contain requests for different adaptations at once: the base matrix multiply is shared across the whole batch, and each request's tiny $BA$ correction is applied per-sequence.

This is why the technique matters beyond saving training memory. §38 says throughput comes from batching, and full fine-tunes cannot share a batch: a hundred fine-tuned models is a hundred separately-batched services, each poorly utilised. A hundred adapters is one well-utilised service.

The cost is real and small: the per-sequence correction is a low-intensity operation of exactly the kind §3 warns about, so it is more expensive than its FLOP count suggests. It remains far cheaper than the alternative.

§37 What to choose

In order of cost
  1. Prompting. Free and immediate. Try it and measure it before anything else, because it is frequently sufficient and it establishes the baseline everything else must beat.
  2. Retrieval. If the gap is knowledge rather than behaviour, adding the right context beats changing the weights, and it stays current.
  3. LoRA. When behaviour must change, when you need many variants, or when full fine-tuning does not fit.
  4. Full fine-tuning. When the adaptation is large, you have the data, and you will serve one model rather than many.

The ordering is by cost and the common error is to start at the bottom. Part XIV's discipline applies unchanged: establish what the cheapest option achieves before paying for the expensive one.
