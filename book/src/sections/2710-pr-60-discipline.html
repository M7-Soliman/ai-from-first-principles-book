§32 A case, end to end

The Street View transcription system of §4 and §11, told once in order, because the value of the process is in the sequence rather than in any step.

Worked: from a goal to ten points of coverage

The goal. Add addresses to a map from photographs. A wrong address is worse than none, so accuracy must be human-level: 98%. Stated as classification that is a hard target; stated as §4's coverage it becomes tractable: hold accuracy at 98% and maximise coverage. Coverage became the project's metric.

The baseline. §6: a convolutional network with rectifiers, and the simplest possible output: $n$ independent softmax units, one per character, each trained as if it were its own classification problem.

The first change came from theory. Refusing requires a probability for the whole sequence, and multiplying independent softmax outputs is not one. Replacing it with an output layer computing a genuine sequence log-likelihood made the rejection mechanism work: §5's point, that a refusal threshold is only as good as the number it thresholds.

Then instrumentation. Coverage was still under 90% with no obvious theoretical problem left. §10: train and test error were nearly identical, so it was not overfitting, and with tens of millions of labelled examples, §13 says more data was not the answer either. That leaves underfitting or a data defect.

Then §11. Sorting the training images by the confidence of incorrect transcriptions showed the defect: addresses cropped too tightly by the upstream detector, with digits missing. "1849" arriving as "849".

The fix was not the obvious one. Improving the detector would have taken weeks. Widening the crop systematically took an afternoon and forced the transcription network to tolerate more variation in position and scale. It added ten percentage points of coverage.

Then the last few points came from hyperparameters, mostly making the model larger, which §13 licenses precisely because train and test error had stayed equal throughout, so the deficit was known to be underfitting.

Why every step depended on the one before

The crop discovery is the memorable part and it was only reachable because of what came first. Without a metric, nobody knows the system is stuck. Without the train/test comparison, the obvious response to "coverage is low" is more data or a bigger model, and both would have been wrong. Without §11's ranking, the images are just images.

And the final hyperparameter work was safe only because the diagnosis was continuously maintained: train and test error stayed equal, so making the model bigger was known to be the right direction rather than hoped to be.

§33 What the metric cannot tell you

Three gaps between the number and the thing, each of which has appeared in this book and is worth collecting in one place.

The loss is not the metric
You train on cross-entropy because it is differentiable and well-behaved (Part II §26). You care about something else. They usually move together and there is no guarantee, and where they diverge the optimiser follows the loss.
The metric is not the goal
Part XIII §6's mechanism, one level up. The metric is a measurable stand-in for something you actually want, and the substitution is invisible everywhere downstream. A recommender optimising engagement is not optimising usefulness, and nothing in the pipeline records the difference.
The goal is not the consequence
A system that achieves its goal can still be harmful, and this is not a metric problem at all. Part XXI is about it. The point here is only that no amount of diagnostic rigour in Parts A–E addresses it, and treating a good number as a complete answer is a category error.
Why Goodhart's law is the governing hazard

Any measure used as a target stops measuring what it measured. This is not cynicism about metrics; it is a structural consequence of optimising against a proxy, and the field runs into it at every scale: an individual overfitting a validation set (§20), a community overfitting a benchmark (§22), a deployed system optimising a proxy for user value (§33).

The defence is not to abandon measurement. It is to keep a measurement you are not optimising: a held-out benchmark used once, a labelled stream from live traffic (§27), a control group (§30). The moment every number you have is one you have been optimising, you have lost the ability to tell whether you are improving.

§34 The ladder

Part IX §29 gave a diagnostic ladder for a run that will not train. This is the general one, ordered by cost: each rung is cheaper than the one below and eliminates more.

When something is wrong, in this order
  1. Is the evaluation real? Score the model on training data through the test-time code path. Two minutes, and it separates a bad model from a broken measurement (§10).
  2. Can it fit ten examples? Regularization off. If not, it is a bug (§8).
  3. Is training error acceptable? If not, more data cannot help. Capacity, then optimiser, then suspect the data (§13).
  4. Is the gap the problem? Then regularize, reduce capacity, or gather more, and use a learning curve to decide how much (§14).
  5. What do the worst mistakes look like? Sorted by confidence, on the training set (§11).
  6. What do the instruments say? Gradient norms, dead units, the update ratio (§12).
  7. Is the difference real? Seeds, and the size of the search (§19, §20).
  8. Only now: a different architecture or optimiser.
Why the last rung is last

Changing the architecture is the most common first move and the least informative. It is expensive, it changes many things at once, and if it helps you have learned that something about the previous configuration was wrong without learning what.

Every rung above it is cheaper and returns a specific answer. Rungs one and two together take ten minutes and between them eliminate the two failure modes (broken evaluation and outright bug) that account for a large share of the cases where a model "does not work".

§35 When nothing works

The ladder terminates and the model is still not good enough. Four possibilities, in the order worth considering them.

The four

You are at the irreducible error. Part V §20. Estimate it (Part XIII §14's sample-and-relabel gives you the number), and if you are near it, you are finished. This is the possibility people check last and should check first, because it is the only one where the correct action is to stop.

The data cannot answer the question. The inputs do not contain the information. No model recovers what was never measured, and the response is new features or new instrumentation, not new architecture.

The task is mis-specified. Part XIII §6: the labels define the task, and if the labels encode an ambiguous or wrong question then a better model fits it better. High annotator disagreement (Part XIII §5) is the diagnostic.

It is genuinely open. Sometimes the problem is a research problem. This is the correct conclusion far less often than it is reached.

Why stopping is a legitimate outcome

§2 asked for a target so that this moment has an answer. A system that reaches the noise floor is done, and continuing to work on it produces §20's inflated numbers and nothing else.

The uncomfortable version: the honest conclusion is sometimes that the problem is not solvable with the data available, and that finding is worth more than a model that appears to work because the evaluation was not rigorous enough to say otherwise. Every technique in Part D exists to make that distinction possible.

§36 The habits

What actually transfers
  • Decide the metric and the target before training. Both, written down.
  • Get something end to end working badly, immediately.
  • Fit ten examples before anything else. Keep it as a test.
  • Look at the data. Especially the confident mistakes, sorted.
  • Change one thing. Then measure.
  • Run the baseline five times so you know the noise floor.
  • Keep one measurement you never optimise against.
  • Report the search size, the seeds, and the worst slice.
  • Write down what you expected before you look. It is the only defence against finding every result unsurprising.
The one thing to carry forward

Diagnose before treating.

Every remedy in this book treats a specific condition. Regularization treats variance and makes bias worse. More data treats variance and does nothing for underfitting. A bigger model treats underfitting and makes overfitting worse. Applied to the wrong condition, each of them is not merely useless: it moves you backwards while looking like progress.

The instruments that tell you which condition you have are cheap: two error numbers, ten examples, a sorted list of mistakes, five seeds. The expensive part is remembering to look before reaching, and that is the whole of this part.
