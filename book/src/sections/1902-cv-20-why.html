Part BWhy it works, measured

Three properties are usually listed as the reasons convolution works: sparse interactions, parameter sharing and equivariance. They are genuinely different claims, they buy different things, and two of them are routinely confused with a fourth property that convolution does not have.

§7 Sparse interactions

A dense layer has a parameter for every input–output pair. Making the kernel smaller than the input means each output sees only a few inputs, and §4's matrix is mostly zero.

What sparsity buys, in three currencies

With $m$ inputs and $n$ outputs, a dense layer needs $m \times n$ parameters and $O(m \times n)$ operations. Limit each output to $k$ connections and both become $O(k \times n)$.

  • Memory. Fewer parameters to store.
  • Compute. Fewer multiply-accumulates per example.
  • Statistical efficiency. Fewer parameters to estimate from the same data, Part VI §6's capacity argument, and the one that matters most.

In practice $k$ can be several orders of magnitude below $m$ without hurting.

Intuition: why locality is a defensible assumption about images

Sparsity is only free if the connections you removed were not carrying anything. For images they mostly were not, and the reason is physical rather than statistical.

A pixel's value is determined by the surface patch that projected onto it. Two pixels that are adjacent almost always come from the same surface; two pixels at opposite corners usually do not. So the strong statistical dependencies in an image are short-range, and a small kernel is not throwing away much.

It is not throwing away nothing, which §32 returns to. Long-range structure is real (the two ends of a shadow, the two sides of a symmetric object) and convolution has to reach it by stacking layers rather than directly. Whether that is enough is precisely the question vision transformers reopened.

§8 Parameter sharing

Sparsity says an output looks at few inputs. Sharing says that the same weights are used at every position. In §4's matrix, the first constraint made most entries zero and the second made the survivors repeat: 324 nonzero entries taking 9 distinct values.

The saving is different in kind from sparsity's. Sharing does not change the forward runtime at all (it is still $O(k \times n)$ multiply-accumulates) but it takes the storage from $k \times n$ to $k$.

Why this is Part VIII §15, arriving as architecture

Part VIII §15 distinguished a tying penalty, which says two weights should be similar, from parameter sharing, which says they are one weight. Sharing removes parameters outright rather than constraining them.

Convolution is that idea taken as far as it goes. The claim is not that the edge detector at the top-left should resemble the one at the bottom-right; it is that there is one edge detector, and it is evaluated in many places.

The consequence for learning is the one that matters. A dense layer must see an edge at every position to learn to detect edges at every position. A convolutional layer that sees an edge anywhere has learned to detect it everywhere. Every training example contributes to every position's detector, which is a form of data amplification that no penalty can imitate.

The middle ground: locally connected and tiled layers

Sparsity and sharing are separable, and the layers in between have names.

A locally connected layer, or unshared convolution, has convolution's connectivity and no sharing: a separate weight for every (position, offset, channel pair):

$$Z_{i,j,k} = \sum_{l,m,n} V_{l,\,j+m-1,\,k+n-1}\;w_{i,j,k,l,m,n}$$

Note the extra indices $j,k$ on $w$. This is right when features should be local but not the same everywhere: face images cropped and centred, where the top of the frame needs eyebrow detectors and the bottom needs a chin.

Tiled convolution interpolates: learn $t$ kernels and cycle through them as you move across the image, so neighbouring positions differ but positions $t$ apart share. Memory grows by a factor of $t$ rather than by the size of the feature map.

Neither is much used, and both are worth knowing because they show that convolution is a point on a spectrum rather than the only option. The spectrum runs from "every position gets its own weights" to "one set of weights for all positions", and convolution sits at the far end.

§9 Equivariance, precisely

In 60 seconds

Shift the input and the output shifts identically. That is equivariance, and it follows from sharing rather than from sparsity. It is exact, measured here at float32 rounding. And it holds for translation only: rotate or rescale the input and the output is essentially unrelated, which is the most over-assumed fact in this part.

Equivariance

$f$ is equivariant to $g$ when $f(g(\mathbf{x})) = g(f(\mathbf{x}))$: doing $g$ first and doing it afterwards give the same answer.

For convolution and any translation $g$: shifting an image and then convolving equals convolving and then shifting. A feature map is therefore a map of where things are, and moving the thing moves its entry.

Pitfall: the three things equivariance is not

It is not invariance. Equivariance says the output moves with the input; invariance says it does not change. They are close to opposite, and §13 is about the distinction because it is muddled constantly.

It is not rotation or scale equivariance. Figure 3 measures those failing. If your task has objects at arbitrary orientations, convolution gives you nothing for it, and you need augmentation (Part VIII §10), a different architecture, or pooling over rotated copies (§14). And the exactness measured here is exactness of the convolution: Part XXVII §5 measures what a strided downsample does to the same guarantee, which is five orders of magnitude worse and has a sixty-year-old remedy. Part XXVI generalises this section's argument: convolution prices one symmetry, and the same reasoning applied to permutation and to rotation gives the architectures for graphs and for molecules.

It is not exact at the boundary. Padding breaks it: a shifted object near the edge meets a different number of implicit zeros. Figure 3 excludes a border for that reason, and real networks do have a border artefact, which is one reason they can quietly learn to use absolute position.

§10 Receptive fields, and the gap you were not told about

Go and read

Luo et al. (2016), Understanding the effective receptive field in deep convolutional neural networks. The paper behind Figure 4, and one of those results that is obvious once stated and was not stated for twenty-five years. Everyone had been computing $1+2L$ and designing against it. The $\sqrt{L}$ argument is short and you should try to reconstruct it before reading, and the hint is that it is a random walk; you met the same argument in Part V §3.

An output unit's receptive field is the set of input pixels that can affect it. For one $3\times3$ layer it is $3\times3$. Stack two and it is $5\times5$; stack $L$ and the arithmetic gives $1 + 2L$. This is how a network built from tiny kernels comes to see whole objects, and the formula is in every tutorial.

The formula is also, in a sense that matters, wrong.

Intuition: why $\sqrt{L}$, and why you should have expected it

The gradient reaching an input pixel is a sum over every path from that pixel to the output. A pixel at the very edge of the receptive field is reachable by exactly one path: straight out, one step per layer. A pixel at the centre is reachable by an enormous number.

So the number of paths falls off sharply with distance, and the profile is a sum of many independent contributions with a distance that accumulates one step at a time. That is a random walk, and by Part V §3 a random walk over $L$ steps spreads like $\sqrt{L}$, not like $L$.

The same argument, in the same book, has now explained three things: why the standard error of a mean falls like $1/\sqrt{n}$, why a deep feedforward network's scale drifts rather than exploding (Part IX §10), and why stacking $3\times3$ kernels buys reach far more slowly than the arithmetic promises.

Why this changes design decisions

The naive calculation says a 20-layer stack of $3\times3$ kernels has a 41-pixel receptive field and can therefore see a 41-pixel object. Figure 4 says most of the gradient is confined to a fraction of that, so it effectively cannot.

Three standard practices exist because of it, and they look unrelated until you see the cause:

  • Downsampling (pooling and stride, §12, §15). Halving the resolution doubles what every subsequent pixel covers, which multiplies reach without multiplying depth.
  • Dilated convolution (§29), which spreads the kernel out and buys reach with no extra parameters or compute at all.
  • Skip connections (§23), which shorten the path and, as a side effect, widen the effective field by giving the gradient a route that does not decay.

And it is why "just add more layers" is a poor answer to "the model cannot see the whole object". You would need to quadruple the depth to double the effective reach.

§11 An infinitely strong prior

The cleanest statement of what a convolutional layer is, and the one that connects this part to everything in Parts V, VI and VIII.

Part V §18 established that a prior is a distribution over parameters expressed before seeing data, and that a penalty is a prior in disguise. A weak prior has high entropy and lets the data move the parameters freely; a strong prior has low entropy and insists. An infinitely strong prior places zero probability on some parameter settings: they are forbidden, whatever the data says.

Convolution as a prior over a dense layer

A convolutional layer is a fully connected layer with an infinitely strong prior on its weights, saying two things:

  • The weights of one hidden unit must be identical to its neighbour's, shifted.
  • The weights must be zero outside a small contiguous receptive field.

Equivalently: the function this layer learns has only local interactions and is equivariant to translation. Pooling (§12) adds a third: each unit must be invariant to small translations.

Implementing it that way would be absurd, and the point is not implementation. It is that architecture and regularisation are the same kind of thing, differing in degree. Weight decay says small weights are preferred. Convolution says non-shared weights are impossible. Part VIII §21's table of techniques that turn out to be one technique has a row waiting for this.

Why this framing pays: three consequences

Convolution and pooling can cause underfitting. A prior helps only when it is true. If a task depends on precise spatial information, pooling everything raises the training error, Part IX §29's rung 6 in a new setting, and one reason some architectures deliberately pool some channels and not others.

The prior is why the data requirement drops. Part VI §6's capacity argument says you need data in proportion to what the model can express. Forbidding most functions outright is the most direct way to need less data, and it is why convolutional networks worked in the 1990s on datasets that could not train anything else.

It changes what a benchmark measures. A model without convolution would learn just as well if you permuted every pixel of every image: it has no notion of which pixels are adjacent. A convolutional model would be destroyed. So the two are not comparable on the same benchmark: one has been told the topology of the input and the other has to discover it. Some image datasets maintain separate leaderboards for permutation-invariant models for exactly this reason, and a comparison that ignores the distinction is measuring the designer's knowledge, not the model's.

Try it

You are classifying images of galaxies, which appear at arbitrary orientations. Which of §§7–11's properties still helps you, which is now wrong, and what would you do about it?

Answer. Sparse interaction still holds: the physics of a galaxy image is still local, and neighbouring pixels are still more related than distant ones.

Parameter sharing still holds: a galaxy in the top-left is the same object as one in the bottom-right, so translation is a genuine symmetry of the task.

The prior is incomplete rather than wrong. Convolution encodes translation and nothing else, and this task has a rotational symmetry the architecture does not know about. So the model must learn separately to recognise each orientation, which is exactly the waste §1 objected to in the dense layer, one symmetry up.

Three remedies, in ascending order of cost and ambition. Augment with rotated copies (Part VIII §10): cheap, and it teaches the invariance from data rather than encoding it. Pool over rotated copies of each filter (§14): build the invariance in, at a multiplicative cost in compute. Or use a group-equivariant architecture, which generalises §9's argument from translations to a larger symmetry group and is the principled answer.

The useful habit is the question rather than the answer: what are the symmetries of this task, and which of them does my architecture already know? Every one it does not know is something the data has to pay for.

History: random filters, and why training them mattered

ch09 §9.9 covers a line of work, active roughly 2007–2013, that asked how much of a convolutional network's power comes from the structure and how much from learning the filters. The answer was surprising.

Random filters work remarkably well. Replace the learned weights with random Gaussian filters and freeze them; then train only the final classifier. Coates et al. (2011) showed that such networks, with enough random filters and a good classifier on top, matched carefully trained networks on several benchmarks of the time. The structure of the architecture (the locality, the sharing, the pooling) was doing most of the work, not the learned values.

Hand-designed and k-means filters work too. Gabor filters, difference-of-Gaussians, and filters found by k-means on small patches all give competitive representations. This is consistent with §31's observation that Gabor-like filters emerge from almost any learning algorithm applied to natural images: they are a property of the data, so a fixed filter matching that property is a reasonable prior.

Greedy layer-wise pretraining. Before end-to-end backpropagation became practical at depth, networks were trained one layer at a time using unsupervised objectives (sparse coding or restricted Boltzmann machines) and only the final layer was trained discriminatively. Convolutional DBNs (Lee et al., 2009) applied this to images and showed that the unsupervised features transferred across tasks.

This history matters for two reasons. First, it establishes that the inductive bias of the convolutional architecture is a large fraction of its value, which is exactly what §11's prior framing predicts, and which the ConvNeXt experiment (§26) rediscovered from a different direction. Second, greedy pretraining was superseded not because it was wrong but because better initialisation (Part IX §16), normalisation (§23–§24) and data augmentation (Part VIII §10) made end-to-end training competitive, and then dominant. The ideas in §9.9 were not refuted; they were outcompeted by tools that this book has already derived.
