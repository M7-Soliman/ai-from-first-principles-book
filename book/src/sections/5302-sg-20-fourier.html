Part BThe Fourier transform

A change of basis, an algorithm that made it practical, and a theorem that turns convolution into multiplication. The third of those is what Part X's entire subject rests on, and it has been used here for seventeen parts without being stated.

§8 Frequency as a basis

The idea, before the formula

Any signal can be written as a sum of sinusoids. The Fourier transform is the map from a signal to the amplitudes and phases of those sinusoids, a change of basis, from the basis of individual samples to the basis of frequencies.

The basis is orthogonal, which is why the coefficients can be read off one at a time by projection rather than solved for jointly, and it is the eigenbasis of every shift-invariant operation, which is §14's theorem and the reason the whole construction matters.

Why sinusoids and not something else

Part I taught that a basis is a choice, and that a good one makes the operations you care about simple. Here the operation is shifting. Shift a sinusoid and you get the same sinusoid with a different phase; the frequency does not change. No other family of functions has that property, which is what makes this the natural basis for anything that treats all positions alike.

So the argument is the same one as Part XXVI's: a symmetry picks the representation. There the symmetry was permutation; here it is translation, and it picks frequency.

§9 The transform

Continuous, and its inverse

$$\hat{x}(f) \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\, e^{-2\pi i f t}\, dt \qquad\qquad x(t) \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \hat{x}(f)\, e^{2\pi i f t}\, df$$

The integrand is the signal against a complex sinusoid: the result's magnitude says how much of that frequency is present, its argument says with what phase. The symmetry between the pair is the statement that no information has been created or destroyed: the transform is invertible, and §11 gives the exact sense in which it is an isometry.

§10 The discrete Fourier transform

What you can actually compute

For $n$ samples $x_0 \dots x_{n-1}$:

$$X_k \;=\; \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} x_j\, e^{-2\pi i jk/n}, \qquad k = 0,\dots,n-1$$

This is a matrix product: $X = Wx$ with $W_{kj} = e^{-2\pi i jk/n}$. Which is the first thing to notice about it: the transform is a linear map, so everything Part I says about linear maps applies, and the second thing to notice is that computing it this way costs $n^2$.

Pitfall: the DFT assumes your signal is periodic

The discrete transform treats the $n$ samples as one period of an infinitely repeating signal. If the first and last samples differ, the implied signal has a discontinuity at the seam, and a discontinuity has energy at every frequency.

That is where §21's leakage comes from, and it is the reason windows exist. It is also the most common way a spectrum gets misread: a broad noise floor that is not in the data, manufactured by the assumption.

§11 What the transform preserves

Parseval, and why it matters here

$$\sum_{j} |x_j|^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k} |X_k|^2$$

Energy is conserved. The transform is a rotation of the space, not a distortion of it, so "how much signal is there" is the same question in either basis.

This is what licenses talking about a power spectrum as a decomposition of the signal's energy across frequency (§27 measures one), and it is why an $L_2$ loss in one domain is an $L_2$ loss in the other, which occasionally makes a hard objective easy.

§12 The fast algorithm

Cooley–Tukey

Split the sum into even-indexed and odd-indexed terms. Each is a transform of length $n/2$, and the two combine in $n$ operations because the odd half only needs a phase factor applied. Recurse:

$$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n) \;\Longrightarrow\; T(n) = O(n \log n)$$

It is exactly the divide-and-conquer shape, and it works because the roots of unity used at length $n$ contain those used at $n/2$, an arithmetic coincidence, not a general property of matrix products.

1965, and 1805

Cooley and Tukey published the algorithm in 1965 and it changed what was computationally possible in signal processing overnight. Gauss had it in an unpublished notebook around 1805, before Fourier's own work appeared, which makes it one of the more thoroughly rediscovered results in numerical mathematics, at least sixteen times, by one count.

§13 Reading a spectrum

Four things to know before you plot one

Bin $k$ is frequency $k f_s / n$. The spacing is set by the record length, not by the sampling rate alone.

The second half is a mirror for a real signal, so only bins $0$ to $n/2$ carry information.

Bin zero is the mean. Subtract it before plotting on a log scale or it dominates everything.

Plot power on a log axis. Natural signals span many orders of magnitude across frequency (§27 measures how many), and a linear axis shows one peak and nothing else.

§14 The convolution theorem

The result this book has been using since Part X

$$\widehat{x * h} \;=\; \hat{x} \cdot \hat{h}$$

Convolution in one domain is pointwise multiplication in the other. A filter that mixes every input position with every output position becomes a per-frequency scalar.

Equivalently: the complex sinusoids are the eigenvectors of every convolution, and $\hat{h}$ is the vector of eigenvalues. That is the precise sense in which a convolutional layer is diagonal in frequency, and it is why "what does this filter do" has a one-line answer (it scales each frequency by a number) where "what does this dense layer do" does not.

What it explains about Part X

Why convolution and translation belong together. Shift-invariant linear operators are exactly the convolutions, and they are exactly the operators diagonalised by the Fourier basis. Part X §9's equivariance and this theorem are the same fact.

Why a small kernel is a smooth response. A short filter has a broad, slowly-varying frequency response (the uncertainty relation of §22 again), so a 3×3 kernel cannot be sharply selective in frequency however it is trained.

Why depth changes the picture. Stacking convolutions multiplies frequency responses, so a deep stack of small kernels can be selective where one cannot. The receptive-field argument of Part X §10 is this statement in the other domain.

§15 What it costs

Why deep learning almost never takes the right-hand branch

Kernels in vision are 3×3 or 7×7, three orders of magnitude below the crossover, so direct convolution wins by a wide margin and the transform is irrelevant. That is a fact about the architecture, not about the mathematics.

It reverses where kernels are long: convolutional sequence models with hundreds of taps, the long convolutions inside state space models, and any place a global filter is applied. In those the transform is not an optimisation but the only way the layer is affordable.

§16 What movement B establishes

Three things

The Fourier transform is the change of basis that translation picks out. Sinusoids are the only functions a shift leaves in place, which is why they diagonalise every shift-invariant operator.

The fast algorithm is divide-and-conquer on an arithmetic coincidence. Figure 2 measures it exact to 4.35×10−13 and pulling away from the definition without bound, and measures the gap between an asymptotic claim and a wall clock.

Convolution is multiplication in frequency. This is the theorem behind Part X, it says what a filter does in one line, and Figure 3 says when computing it that way is worth the trouble.
