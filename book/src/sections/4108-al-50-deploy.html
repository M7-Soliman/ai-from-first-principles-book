Part EDeployment and consequence

Everything so far has been about the artefact. This movement is about the system it becomes part of, and about the effects that are not properties of the model at all. They belong in a technical book because the technical choices determine them, and because the people who make those choices are the readers of books like this one.

§34 What changes at deployment

The three changes that matter

The input distribution stops being yours. Every evaluation in Part XIV and every measurement in movement D was made on a distribution you chose. Users choose the deployed one, and it moves: sometimes because the world moved, sometimes because users adapted to the system.

The stakes become asymmetric. A benchmark scores an average. A deployment is judged by its worst outputs, in volume, and the two are related only loosely, which is §27's arithmetic restated in the place where it costs money.

The system acquires parts you did not train. Retrieval, tools, filters, retries, a cache, a fallback model, a human in the loop. The behaviour you are responsible for is the system's, and the model is one component of it.

Part XIX §37 chose a serving configuration and Part XX §36 assembled an agent. Neither asked what happens after the first week. This section is that question, and the answer is that most of what goes wrong at deployment is not a model failure in the sense the earlier parts would recognise. It is a distribution that moved, a component that failed open, or a metric that was measuring something adjacent to what mattered.

Why the model is rarely the whole story

Consider a system that answers questions from a document store. Its failures divide roughly into: the retriever returned nothing relevant; the retriever returned something relevant and the model ignored it; the document store was stale; the model was right and the user asked the wrong question; the model hallucinated despite good context. Only the last two are the model in the sense a benchmark measures.

This is why the deployment metric and the model metric come apart, and why improving the model can leave the deployment unchanged. Measure the system, then attribute: Part XIV §21's ablation discipline, applied to something larger than a network.

§35 Monitoring

Monitoring is evaluation that continues after you stop looking. It is the only part of the safety story that survives contact with a distribution you do not control, and it is usually the part that is built last and least.

What to record, and what each one catches

Input distribution statistics. Length, language, topic clusters, rate. Catches the drift that invalidates every offline measurement you made.

Model confidence, calibrated. Part XIV §5's calibration is what makes a confidence number an actionable threshold rather than a decoration. Catches the region where the model has quietly stopped knowing.

Refusal and filter rates. A sharp change means either an attack or a regression, and the two look identical for the first hour.

User-side signals. Retries, edits, abandonment, thumbs. Noisy, biased towards the annoyed, and still the only measurement of whether the output was useful.

Sampled human review. Expensive, irreplaceable, and the only one of these that can discover a failure mode nobody anticipated. Everything else can only count failures of a kind you already named.

Pitfall: a monitor that only counts known failures

Automated monitoring measures the categories you defined. The failures that matter most are the ones outside them, and by construction those produce no alert. This is the same structure as §30's validity problem: the instrument sees what it was built to see.

The correction is the sampled review above, and the fact that it is expensive is not a reason to skip it. It is the only channel through which an unanticipated failure can reach you before a user publicises it.

§36 Open weights

Whether to release weights is the decision on which the technical community is most visibly split, and the split is not mainly about facts. It is about which risk you weight more heavily under shared uncertainty. Setting out the actual structure of the disagreement is more useful than adjudicating it.

What release actually changes

It is irreversible. An API can be withdrawn, rate-limited, filtered or patched. Weights, once distributed, cannot. Every argument on both sides runs through this asymmetry.

It removes the safety training's guarantee. Part XX §30 measured what alignment training changes; fine-tuning reverses most of it cheaply, and the published cost of doing so is small. Safety properties of open weights are properties of the default, not of the artefact.

It removes the monitoring channel. Everything in §35 requires that inference passes through infrastructure you operate. With open weights it does not, so nobody, including the releaser, can measure how the system is being used.

It distributes the capability to everyone at once, which is the argument for as much as against: researchers who can study the model, small organisations that can deploy without a gatekeeper, countries that would otherwise depend on a foreign vendor, and a check on the concentration §37 describes.

Where the disagreement actually lives

Both sides agree on the four facts above. The disagreement is about the marginal contribution: how much a released model adds to what a determined actor could already do with the open models that exist, the closed models they can jailbreak, and the internet. If the margin is small, the costs above buy little and the benefits are real. If it is large, the irreversibility dominates.

That is an empirical question about the near future, which is why it cannot be settled by argument, and why the evaluations in movement D, specifically §28's, are the thing that would settle it. Treat anyone confident in either direction as making a forecast, not stating a result.

§37 Concentration

Part XVII §16 counted what a frontier training run costs in memory and Part XIX §31 counted what adaptation costs. Put the numbers together and the structural consequence is visible: the set of organisations that can train a frontier model from scratch is small, and it is not growing as fast as the models are.

The three concentrations, which are separable

Compute. Concentrated in a handful of firms and a smaller handful of chip suppliers. Follows directly from the capital cost of a frontier run.

Data. Concentrated in whoever holds large proprietary corpora and whoever crawled the web before the licensing regime changed: Part XIII §34's point, arriving as an economic fact.

Capability. Less concentrated than either, and this is the interesting part. Distillation (Part XIX), open weights (§36), and the fact that a good small model beats a bad large one mean that the ability to deploy a capable system is far more widely distributed than the ability to create the frontier.

Conflating the three produces bad predictions in both directions. The first two are concentrating; the third is diffusing.

§38 Labour

What the technology actually does to a job

A model automates tasks, and a job is a bundle of tasks. Whether a job disappears depends on what fraction of its bundle is automatable, what the remaining fraction requires, and whether cheaper output raises demand enough to need the same number of people doing the residual.

That is why "which jobs will AI replace" is the wrong unit of analysis and why answers to it have historically been poor. The right unit is the task, and the composition of jobs from tasks is what determines the outcome.

Two things in this book bear on it directly, and are worth stating because they are technical results rather than opinions. First, the capability–reliability gap of Part XX §44: a system that is capable but unreliable does not remove the person, it changes their work into checking. Whether that is a better job or a worse one is a real question and is not answered by the accuracy number. Second, the annotation labour of Part XIII §5 and Part XX §27: the preference data that makes these systems usable is produced by people, often under conditions the resulting product does not advertise. A book that counts FLOPs and forgets that is not being rigorous. It is being selective about which costs it counts.

§39 Environmental cost

The arithmetic, and where the honest uncertainty is

Energy for a training run is $\text{GPU-hours} \times \text{power per GPU} \times \text{PUE}$, and each factor is knowable. What converts it to emissions is the grid the datacentre sits on, which varies by more than an order of magnitude between regions and by a factor of several within a day.

The larger term is usually not training but inference, integrated over the deployment. A frontier run is a one-off; serving it is continuous, and Part XIX's whole subject is that inference cost is where the engineering effort goes for exactly this reason. Any comparison that cites a training number alone is measuring the smaller quantity.

Pitfall: the comparison that is almost always missing

The number that matters is the difference between the system and what it displaced, not the system's absolute cost. A query that replaces a search plus three page loads, or a simulation that replaces a physical experiment, has a negative marginal footprint; one that replaces nothing has its full cost.

Both directions of this argument get abused. The honest position is that the counterfactual is hard to establish, that this makes the net effect genuinely uncertain, and that the uncertainty is not a licence to assume the favourable answer.

§40 The questions this book cannot answer

Four, stated plainly

How capable these systems will become. Part XVII's scaling laws extrapolate loss, and Part XX §44 showed that loss and capability are related loosely and non-monotonically. Nobody has a law that predicts capability, and the confident forecasts in both directions are not derived from anything in this book.

Whether current methods suffice for alignment at higher capability. §21's scalable oversight problem is unsolved and the proposals in §22 are not yet demonstrated at the scale where they would be needed.

What the aggregate economic effect is. §38's task decomposition tells you the shape of the analysis, not the answer.

Whether the governance mechanisms will keep pace. Out of scope for a technical book, and load-bearing for every outcome in it.

A book that claimed otherwise on any of these would be doing something other than what the rest of it does. The measurement discipline that governs the previous nine hundred pages does not suspend itself for the questions where measurement is hardest.
