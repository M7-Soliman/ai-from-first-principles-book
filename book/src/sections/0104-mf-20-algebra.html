Part BAlgebra that must be automatic

This is the part people are tempted to skip. Do not. Every failure to follow a derivation later traces back to an algebra step that took too long, not because you could not do it, but because doing it consumed the attention you needed for the actual idea.

§3 Rearranging expressions

Almost all of the algebra in this book is one activity: taking an expression that is correct but unhelpful and turning it into a different expression that is equally correct and more useful. Nothing is being discovered when you do this. The two forms mean the same thing and always did; you are choosing which of several equivalent faces of the same object to look at, the way you might rotate a physical thing in your hand to find the side with the label on it.

That framing matters because it tells you what "getting stuck" actually is. When a derivation loses you, it is very rarely because a new fact appeared. It is because the author rotated the object and you did not see the rotation, so the next line looks like it came from nowhere. Fluency in algebra is largely the ability to watch an expression change shape and register that nothing happened.

The distributive law, forwards and backwards

$$a(b + c) = ab + ac$$

Forwards this is expanding; backwards it is factoring. Both directions matter, and the backwards one is the one that atrophies:

$$3x + 3y = 3(x + y), \qquad x^2 + x = x(x + 1), \qquad e^x y + e^x z = e^x(y + z)$$

That last one is the pattern you will actually use: pulling a common factor out of a messy expression is how most derivations get shortened.

It is worth pausing on why one direction is easy and the other is not. Expanding is mechanical: the instructions are visible in the expression itself, you follow them, and there is only one place to go. Factoring is a search. Nothing in $e^x y + e^x z$ announces that you should pull out the $e^x$; you have to notice the repetition first, and noticing is a different skill from executing. This is why factoring decays so fast when you stop practising and why it comes back so fast when you start: the mechanics were never the hard part, and the mechanics are not what you lost.

What makes it worth recovering is that essentially every simplification you will watch happen in this book is a factoring step in disguise. A derivation that runs for half a page and then collapses into one line has almost always collapsed because something common was pulled out of a sum. When the sigmoid's derivative turns into $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ in §18, and when cross-entropy's gradient collapses to $a - 1$ in Part VII, both are this move. If you can see the common factor, those results look inevitable. If you cannot, they look like magic performed by someone cleverer than you, which is a discouraging and completely false impression.

Combining like terms

Only terms with identical variable parts combine. $3x^2 + 5x^2 = 8x^2$, but $3x^2 + 5x$ does not simplify at all; different powers are different species.

The reason is worth having in words rather than as a rule, because the rule is easy to misapply under pressure. The expression $3x^2 + 5x^2$ combines because it is literally three of something plus five of the same something: three $x^2$'s and five more $x^2$'s, which is eight of them, in the same way that three apples and five apples are eight apples. But $3x^2 + 5x$ is three of one thing and five of a different thing. Three apples and five oranges are not eight of anything; the only honest description is "three apples and five oranges", which is exactly what $3x^2 + 5x$ already says. An expression that will not simplify is not a failure on your part. It is a finished answer.

Pitfall: the errors that come back when you are rusty

Every one of these is wrong. Read them slowly; each is a genuine trap.

  • $(a + b)^2 \neq a^2 + b^2$.   It is $a^2 + 2ab + b^2$. The cross term is real.
  • $\sqrt{a + b} \neq \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$.   Check: $\sqrt{9+16} = 5$, but $3 + 4 = 7$.
  • $\frac{1}{a + b} \neq \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b}$.   Check with $a = b = 1$: $\frac12$ versus $2$.
  • $\log(a + b) \neq \log a + \log b$.   The log rule is about products, not sums. See §10.
  • $e^{a+b} \neq e^a + e^b$.   It is $e^a \cdot e^b$.

The unifying pattern: most operations do not distribute over addition. Squaring, square roots, reciprocals, logs, and exponentials all fail this. When in doubt, test with actual numbers; it takes ten seconds and settles it permanently.

§4 Powers, roots, and fractions

The exponent rules

Six rules, and every one of them follows from what an exponent means. $x^3$ is three copies of $x$ multiplied, so:

RuleWhy
$x^a \cdot x^b = x^{a+b}$3 copies then 2 more copies is 5 copies
$\dfrac{x^a}{x^b} = x^{a-b}$dividing cancels copies
$(x^a)^b = x^{ab}$$b$ groups of $a$ copies each
$x^0 = 1$forced by the rule above: $x^a / x^a = x^0$ and also $=1$
$x^{-a} = \dfrac{1}{x^a}$same reason: it makes the division rule keep working
$x^{1/2} = \sqrt{x}$because $x^{1/2} \cdot x^{1/2} = x^1$

The last three are worth dwelling on, and they are worth more than the one line above, because they are your first sight of a habit that mathematics uses everywhere and rarely explains.

Start from the only meaning an exponent has when you first meet it: $x^3$ is $x$ multiplied by itself three times. Under that definition, the first three rules are not really rules at all, they are observations. Three copies then two more copies is five copies, so $x^3 x^2 = x^5$. Cancelling copies is division. Groups of groups multiply. Fine, but now ask what $x^0$ means. Zero copies of $x$ multiplied together is not a sentence that means anything. The definition has run out.

So the question changes. Instead of asking what does $x^0$ mean, mathematics asks: if this symbol is going to mean something, what would it have to mean for everything we already established to keep working? The division rule says $x^a / x^a = x^{a-a} = x^0$. But anything divided by itself is 1. So if $x^0$ is to have a value at all, the value must be 1, not by decree, not by convention, but because every other choice breaks a rule that was true before the question arose.

Run the same argument again and negative exponents fall out: $x^0 / x^a$ is $1/x^a$ by arithmetic and $x^{-a}$ by the rule, so $x^{-a}$ must be $1/x^a$. Once more and you get fractional ones: $x^{1/2} \cdot x^{1/2} = x^1 = x$, and the thing which gives $x$ when multiplied by itself is $\sqrt{x}$. Three new species of exponent, none of them postulated, all of them forced.

This is how mathematics extends itself almost every time, and recognising the pattern will save you real confusion later. When you meet a definition that seems arbitrary ($0! = 1$, a matrix raised to a power, the entropy of a continuous distribution), the right question is almost never "why would anyone define it that way". It is "what would break if they had defined it otherwise", and the answer is usually that the whole surrounding structure would.

Fractions

$$\frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}, \qquad \frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{d}{c}, \qquad \frac{a}{c} + \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a+b}{c}$$

The one that trips people: to add fractions with different denominators you must find a common one first. $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{3}{6} + \frac{2}{6} = \frac{5}{6}$.

Pitfall: cancelling across a sum

$\dfrac{a + b}{a}$ does not equal $b$. You may only cancel factors, never terms of a sum. Correctly: $\dfrac{a+b}{a} = 1 + \dfrac{b}{a}$.

Test it: $\frac{2+6}{2} = 4$, and $1 + \frac62 = 4$. ✓. Whereas the wrong version would have given 6.

§5 Solving for a variable

One principle, applied repeatedly: whatever you do to one side, do to the other. An equation is a claim that two things are equal; any operation applied to both keeps them equal.

The image that makes this stick is a balance scale. An equation says the two pans are level. You may add a kilogram, provided you add it to both pans; you may halve what is there, provided you halve both. Any of those leaves the scale level, which is to say leaves the equation true. What you may never do is act on one side alone, and if you have ever produced a confidently wrong answer in three lines of algebra, that is almost certainly what happened.

The strategy is to peel operations off the variable you want, in reverse order: outermost first, exactly like undoing a stack.

That reversal deserves a moment, because it is the part people execute backwards. Think about how the expression was built. To construct $3(x+2)^2 - 5$ from $x$, you would add 2, then square, then multiply by 3, then subtract 5, in that order, innermost first. Taking it apart runs the film backwards: undo the subtraction, undo the multiplication, undo the square, undo the addition. Last thing done is first thing undone.

It is exactly getting dressed and undressed. Socks then shoes; shoes then socks. Nobody has to be taught the second order, because attempting it wrongly is physically impossible. Algebra's only real disadvantage here is that doing it in the wrong order is not impossible, merely wrong. So when you look at an expression and cannot see where to start, do not hunt for the variable. Ask which operation was performed last, and undo that one. There is always exactly one answer, and it is always at the outside.

Worked: solve $3(x + 2)^2 - 5 = 43$ for $x$

The operations wrapping $x$, from the outside in: subtract 5, multiply by 3, square, add 2. Undo them in that order.

$3(x+2)^2 = 48$    (add 5 to both sides)

$(x+2)^2 = 16$    (divide both sides by 3)

$x + 2 = \pm 4$    (square root; note the $\pm$)

$x = 2$ or $x = -6$    (subtract 2)

Check $x = 2$: $3(4)^2 - 5 = 48 - 5 = 43$. ✓

Pitfall: losing the negative root

Taking a square root of both sides introduces two solutions, because both $4^2$ and $(-4)^2$ equal 16. Forgetting the $\pm$ silently discards half your answers. This is the single most common error in solving, and it survives well into graduate work.

Rearranging without solving

Often you do not want a number; you want the formula rewritten to isolate a different symbol. This is the skill that matters for reading derivations.

Worked: solve $y = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$ for $x$

This is the sigmoid from §18, and inverting it produces something you will meet as the logit. Peel from the outside in.

$y(1 + e^{-x}) = 1$    (multiply both sides by the denominator)

$1 + e^{-x} = \dfrac{1}{y}$    (divide by $y$)

$e^{-x} = \dfrac{1}{y} - 1 = \dfrac{1-y}{y}$    (subtract 1, combine)

$-x = \ln\!\left(\dfrac{1-y}{y}\right)$    (take $\ln$ of both sides: §10)

$x = \ln\!\left(\dfrac{y}{1-y}\right)$    (negate; flipping a log's fraction negates it)

That final expression is the log-odds, and it is what a classifier's raw output actually represents. You have just derived it.

Try it

Solve each for $x$:

(a) $2x + 7 = 3x - 4$    (b) $\dfrac{x}{x+1} = \dfrac{2}{3}$    (c) $ax + b = c$, for general $a \neq 0$

Answers. (a) Collect: $7 + 4 = 3x - 2x$, so $x = 11$. Check: $2(11)+7 = 29$ and $3(11)-4 = 29$. ✓

(b) Cross-multiply: $3x = 2(x+1) = 2x + 2$, so $x = 2$. Check: $\frac{2}{3}$. ✓

(c) $ax = c - b$, so $x = \dfrac{c-b}{a}$. The condition $a \neq 0$ matters: dividing by zero is undefined, and stating such conditions is a habit worth keeping.
