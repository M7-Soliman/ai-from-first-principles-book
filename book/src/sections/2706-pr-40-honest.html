Part DMeasuring honestly

Everything so far assumed the numbers mean what they appear to. This movement is about the ways they do not, and it is where Part V's statistics finally get applied to the activity that generates most of the field's published claims.

§19 Seed variance

In 60 seconds

Train the same configuration twice with different random seeds and the results differ. If you do not know by how much, you cannot tell whether an improvement is real, and on many benchmarks the seed-to-seed spread exceeds the improvements being claimed.

What varies with the seed

Initialisation, data order, dropout masks, augmentation draws, and any nondeterminism in the kernels. Every one of them changes the final parameters, and on a hard problem they change the final score.

The measurement is one line of experimental design: run the baseline five times and report the mean and the spread. That is your noise floor, and any difference smaller than it is not a result.

Pitfall: one run of each is not a comparison

Method A scores 0.847 and method B scores 0.851. Reported as an improvement, this is not evidence of anything: with a seed standard deviation of, say, 0.006, the difference is well inside a single run's noise.

Part V §16 gives the apparatus: a difference needs an interval, and an interval needs repeated measurements. Report mean and standard deviation over $n$ seeds, state $n$, and if you cannot afford five runs of everything, afford five runs of the baseline so at least the noise floor is known.

The asymmetry to watch for: a new method whose seeds were run and a baseline whose seed was not. That is Part X §26's complaint about attribution, and it is also the mechanism §20 describes.

§20 The winner of a search is biased upward

Go and read

Dodge et al. (2019), Show your work: improved reporting of experimental results. Argues that a reported score is meaningless without the compute budget that produced it, and proposes reporting expected best performance as a function of the number of trials, which turns Figure 7's inflation from a hazard into a curve you publish. The most actionable proposal in this movement, and largely ignored.

Part V §14 established that the best of many noisy measurements is not an unbiased estimate of anything. Hyperparameter search is that situation, run deliberately, hundreds of times.

Why this is worse than it looks

Two things compound it.

The bias grows with effort. The harder you search, the more inflated the reported number, so a well-resourced search produces a more optimistic result than a lazy one, from the same underlying model.

It is invisible. Nothing in the pipeline records that 200 configurations were tried. The validation score is a number, it is higher than the baseline's, and every subsequent decision treats it as an estimate of quality.

The fix is Part V §15's and it is not optional: the configuration you selected must be evaluated on data used in no part of the selection. Three splits: train, validation for selection, test used once. If you look at the test set and then change something, it is a validation set now and you need a new one.

And report the search size. "Best of 200 configurations, test score measured once" is an honest claim. "Validation accuracy 0.85" from the same experiment is not.

§21 Ablations that isolate

A method with five changes and one aggregate number tells you nothing about which change mattered. Ablation is the remedy and it is usually done in a way that does not deliver it.

Two kinds, answering different questions

Leave-one-out. Remove each component from the full system in turn. Answers: is this component necessary, given everything else?

Add-one-in. Add each component to the baseline alone. Answers: is this component sufficient, on its own?

These give different answers when components interact, which they usually do. Two changes that are individually useless and jointly essential will look worthless under add-one-in and essential under leave-one-out, and reporting only one of the two is how a paper attributes an effect to the wrong thing.

Pitfall: an ablation that changes two things

Removing a component often changes something else by implication. Remove a regulariser and the optimal learning rate moves. Remove a normalisation layer and the model may need warmup. Halve the width and the parameter count changes, so the comparison is now about size.

An honest ablation re-tunes what the removal invalidated, or states plainly that it did not. The common failure is to remove a component, keep every other setting fixed, observe a large drop, and attribute it to the component, when part of it was the settings that were tuned for the component's presence.

This is Part X §26's ConvNeXt lesson at the scale of a single table, and it is the most common way an ablation overstates.

§22 What a benchmark measures now

Go and read

Recht et al. (2019), Do ImageNet classifiers generalize to ImageNet?. Rebuilt a test set from scratch following the original protocol as closely as possible, and found every model dropped by a substantial margin, while the ranking was almost perfectly preserved. Read both halves: the drop is the community overfitting, and the preserved ranking is why benchmarks remained useful anyway.

Part XIII §13 established contamination. Here is what it does to the practice of comparing systems.

Three ways a benchmark stops measuring what it did

Contamination. The test items are in the training corpus. Part XIII §13 measured the inflation; the specific problem here is that for a model whose corpus is unpublished, nobody outside can check.

Saturation. Scores approach the ceiling, so the remaining headroom is label noise (Part XIII §9) and the differences between systems are differences in how they handle mislabelled items.

Community overfitting. Everyone tunes against the same public test set for years. No individual did anything wrong; collectively the field ran §20's search with thousands of configurations and no held-out data. The leaderboard is the validation set.

Why the strongest available check is a calendar

You cannot search a corpus you cannot see. The substitutes, in descending order of strength:

Evaluate on material created after the training cutoff. Contamination is impossible by construction. This is the only strong evidence available, it decays as time passes, and it needs continuous work to maintain.

Build a fresh equivalent of an existing benchmark (same task, same difficulty, new items), and report the gap. A model that scores well on the old and poorly on the new has told you something specific.

Test for completion. Prompt with the first part of a benchmark item and see whether the model continues it verbatim. Catches memorisation, misses paraphrase.

None of these is free, which is why the honest position is usually to state what you did check, and that the rest is unverified.

§23 Evaluating output with no right answer

Everything above assumes a label to compare against. Summaries, translations, explanations and generated code have no single correct output, and the evaluation problem changes shape.

Four approaches, with what each buys and costs

Reference overlap: compare against one or more human-written answers by $n$-gram overlap. Cheap, reproducible, and correlates weakly with quality: it penalises a good answer phrased differently and rewards a bad one phrased conventionally.

Human judgement. The standard everything else is validated against. Expensive, slow, and it is a measurement with all of Part XIII §5's properties: it needs a guideline, agreement statistics and adjudication.

Model judgement. §24.

Task-grounded. Where the output has a consequence you can check: does the code pass its tests, does the query return the right rows, does the proof verify. By far the most reliable when available, and available more often than people look for.

Why to reach for a grounded proxy first

The generic instinct is to grade quality directly. The better instinct is to ask whether the output feeds anything with a decidable outcome.

Generated code has tests. A summary can be evaluated by whether a reader who saw only the summary answers questions correctly. A retrieval system can be evaluated by whether the downstream answer improved. Each replaces a judgement with a measurement, and Part XIII §6's warning applies in reverse: these are proxies, they should be named as proxies, and they are still better than a similarity score against one reference.

§24 Model-as-judge

Go and read

Zheng et al. (2023), Judging LLM-as-a-judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena. The paper that measured the practice instead of assuming it: agreement between a model judge and human preference, and the biases (position, verbosity, self-preference) quantified rather than waved at. Read §3 on the biases and note that the headline agreement number is computed after the mitigations.

Use a strong model to grade outputs. It is fast, cheap, reproducible, and it has a set of biases specific enough to be worth listing.

Pitfall: the known biases, all measurable

Position. Presented with two answers, judges favour one position. Check: present both orders and compare: the disagreement rate is the bias.

Length. Judges prefer longer answers, roughly independent of content. Check: correlate score with length.

Self-preference. A judge tends to prefer output from itself or its family. Check: use a judge from a different family than any system being compared.

Style over substance. Confident, well-formatted, fluent wrong answers score well. Check: the calibration of the judge against human labels on items where the answer is verifiably wrong.

Why it is usable anyway, under one condition

The condition is that the judge is itself validated, on your task, against human labels, with the agreement reported.

That converts an unexamined assumption into a measurement: "our judge agrees with human annotators 84% of the time on a sample of 300, against 91% inter-human agreement" is a usable statement. It bounds what any judged comparison can establish, and it is exactly Part XIII §5's apparatus applied to an automated annotator.

Without that validation you have a number produced by a process nobody has checked, being used to decide which system is better. The cost of checking is a few hundred human labels once.

§25 What a leaderboard is

A short section, because the conclusion follows from §§19–24 and is worth stating in one place.

Why a rank is weaker evidence than it looks

A leaderboard position is a single number, from a single run, on a fixed public test set, selected as the best of an unreported search, on a benchmark that may be contaminated, saturated, or collectively overfitted.

Each of §§19–22 removes some of its evidential value; together they remove most of it for differences of a point or two. That is not an argument against benchmarks: they made the field comparable and they are why progress was measurable at all. It is an argument for reading a small gap as weak evidence rather than as a result.

The practical question to ask of any comparison, and it is the one Part V has been building toward: what would have to be true for this difference to be noise? Usually: one seed, an untuned baseline, a large search, and a test set the whole field has been tuning against. If all four hold, the difference is noise until shown otherwise.
