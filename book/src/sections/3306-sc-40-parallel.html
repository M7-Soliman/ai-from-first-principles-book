Part DParallelism

Four ways to split a training job across devices, each cutting along a different axis, each with a communication pattern that decides where it stops working.

§21 Data parallelism

Split the batch

Every device holds a full copy of the model and processes a different slice of the batch. The gradients are averaged across devices, an all-reduce, and every device applies the same update, so the copies stay identical.

It is the simplest thing that works, it is what every framework does by default, and it is the only form of parallelism that requires no change to the model.

The all-reduce, and what it costs

A ring all-reduce over $W$ devices moves $2N \cdot \frac{W-1}{W}$ values per device: about twice the model size, and, crucially, independent of the batch.

So the communication is a constant per step and the compute is proportional to the tokens each device processes. The ratio of the two is the whole story.

§22 Why it stops scaling

Why strong scaling fails and weak scaling works

Strong scaling (fix the total batch, add devices) shrinks the compute per device while the communication stays fixed. Efficiency falls, and past a point adding devices makes the step slower.

Weak scaling (add devices and grow the batch to match) keeps the per-device work constant, so efficiency holds. This is why large runs use enormous total batch sizes: not because a large batch optimises better (§39 says it does not, past a point), but because it is the only way to keep the devices busy.

The two remedies are visible in the figure. Better interconnect moves the curve left. Overlapping communication with computation (starting the all-reduce for early layers' gradients while later layers are still computing) hides much of it, and is why gradient buckets exist.

§23 Tensor parallelism

Split the matrices

Cut individual weight matrices across devices. For a feed-forward block, split the first matrix by columns and the second by rows: each device computes a slice of the hidden dimension independently, and a single all-reduce at the end sums the partial outputs.

Attention splits naturally by heads, which is the same pattern: the heads are already independent (Part XII §12) and only the output projection needs combining.

Pitfall: it communicates inside every layer

Data parallelism communicates once per step. Tensor parallelism communicates twice per layer, on the critical path, with everything waiting.

That volume is proportional to (batch × context × width) rather than to the model size, and it cannot be overlapped with anything because the next operation needs the result. So tensor parallelism is used within a node, across devices joined by a fast local interconnect, and essentially never across nodes. The degree is typically 8, because that is how many devices share a board.

§24 Pipeline parallelism

Split the layers

Give each device a contiguous block of layers. Activations flow forward from stage to stage and gradients flow back. The communication is small (one activation tensor at each boundary), which is why this is the form that works across nodes.

The problem is that stage $k$ cannot start until stage $k-1$ has produced something, so the naive version has one device busy at a time. Splitting the batch into microbatches and streaming them through fills the pipeline, and the figure is what is left over.

The same split is useful with no pipeline at all: compute several microbatches' forward and backward passes and sum their gradients before a single optimiser step. That is gradient accumulation: how a batch too large to fit in memory is trained as if it did, spreading the same total arithmetic over several smaller calls instead of one wide one rather than adding to it, and paying none of the bubble, because there is no stage boundary to wait on.

Why the bubble is the binding constraint

Filling and draining the pipeline costs $P-1$ slots regardless of $M$, so the idle fraction is $(P-1)/(M+P-1)$, falling with more microbatches and never reaching zero.

And more microbatches is not free: every microbatch in flight holds its own activations, so the memory cost grows with exactly the quantity that reduces the bubble. That tension is why schedules matter. 1F1B interleaves forward and backward passes so a microbatch's activations are released as early as possible, holding roughly $P$ microbatches' worth rather than $M$; interleaved schedules give each device several non-contiguous blocks of layers, which shrinks the bubble further at the cost of more communication.

§25 Sequence and context parallelism

Split the tokens

When the context is long enough that a single sequence's activations do not fit, split along the sequence axis. Everything except attention is pointwise across positions and needs no communication at all; attention needs each query to see every key, so the keys and values are passed around the device ring while each device holds its own queries.

This is what makes contexts of hundreds of thousands of tokens possible, and its cost is a communication volume that grows with the context, the same quadratic that §17's memory discussion described, arriving as network traffic instead.

A typical arrangement is tensor parallel 8 within a node, pipeline parallel across a few nodes, and data parallel across everything else, with the three degrees multiplying to the device count.

Why this is a search over a small discrete space

The degrees must multiply to the number of devices, each choice has a different memory and communication profile, and the objective (tokens per second at a fixed total batch) is computable from the arithmetic in this movement plus a few measured bandwidths.

So the configuration is chosen by enumerating the factorisations and evaluating a cost model, not by experiment. The space is small enough to search exhaustively, and doing so before the run costs an hour and routinely finds a configuration twice as fast as the obvious one.

§27 Asynchronous SGD, and why it lost

The approach the source book describes

The 2016 account of distributed training is asynchronous SGD with a parameter server: workers read parameters without a lock, compute gradients, and apply them without a lock, accepting that some updates overwrite others. It states that this "remains the primary strategy for training large deep networks".

It is now essentially unused. The reason is not that the analysis was wrong (stale gradients do cost less than the synchronisation they avoid, at moderate scale) but that the situation changed underneath it.

Synchronous training got cheap. Ring all-reduce made the communication scale with the model rather than with the number of workers, and overlapping hid much of what remained. The cost async was avoiding largely went away.

Asynchrony is a debugging nightmare. A synchronous run is deterministic given a seed and reproduces exactly. An async run does not, so a loss spike cannot be reproduced and every Part XIV instrument becomes harder to use.

Staleness scales badly. With thousands of workers the average gradient is many steps out of date, and the effective learning rate has to fall to compensate, which cancels the throughput it bought.

Worth keeping as a case where a correct engineering analysis was overtaken by a change in the hardware it assumed, which is the most common way infrastructure advice expires.

§28 Failure, and checkpointing

At scale, hardware failure is a scheduled event

With thousands of devices running for weeks, the mean time between failures is measured in hours. A run that cannot survive losing a node will not finish.

The arithmetic that decides the checkpoint interval is standard: the expected cost is roughly $\frac{T_c}{I} + \frac{I}{2 T_f}$ per unit time, for checkpoint cost $T_c$, interval $I$ and mean time to failure $T_f$, minimised at $I = \sqrt{2 T_c T_f}$. It is worth computing rather than guessing, because both extremes are expensive.

What must be saved is more than the weights: optimiser states, the data-loader position, the RNG state and the schedule step. Anything omitted makes a resumed run differ from an uninterrupted one, and the difference will be blamed on something else.
