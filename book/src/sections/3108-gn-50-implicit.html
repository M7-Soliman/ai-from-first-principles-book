Part EImplicit models

§34 Generating without a density

Go and read

Goodfellow et al. (2014), Generative adversarial networks. Read §4, which proves that substituting the optimal discriminator turns the objective into the Jensen–Shannon divergence. That is the clearest available statement of what a learned loss function is, and it is worth having before §36's measurement complicates the picture.

The idea

Every family so far represented a density and then worked around the difficulty of computing it. The implicit approach declines to represent one: a network maps noise to samples, and the distribution is whatever that map induces. There is no $p(\mathbf{x})$ anywhere in the model.

Which raises the obvious question: with no likelihood, what is the training signal? The answer is to bring in a second network whose job is to tell the samples apart from the data.

The game

A generator $g$ maps noise to samples. A discriminator $d$ estimates the probability that its input is real. They play

$$\min_g \max_d \;\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{data}}}\log d(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\log\big(1 - d(g(\mathbf{z}))\big)$$

The discriminator wants to separate; the generator wants to fool it. At equilibrium the samples are indistinguishable from data and $d$ outputs $\tfrac{1}{2}$ everywhere.

Why the objective is a divergence in disguise

For a fixed generator the optimal discriminator is

$$d^*(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})} {p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}) + p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x})}$$

and substituting it back turns the generator's objective into the Jensen–Shannon divergence between data and model, up to a constant. So the game is minimising a divergence after all; the discriminator is estimating the divergence that the loss cannot compute.

That is the design's real content: a learned loss function. Where maximum likelihood fixes what counts as a discrepancy in advance, a GAN discovers it, which is why these models produced sharp images while maximum-likelihood models produced blurry ones. The discriminator notices blur, so blur is penalised.

Two cautions on that derivation. It assumes the discriminator is optimal, which it never is. And Jensen–Shannon is a symmetric compromise between §4's two directions, so a GAN is not purely mode-seeking in theory, whatever it does in practice.

§35 Why the training is hard

Three problems, and none of them is ordinary optimisation

It is not minimisation. There is no single objective descending. The target is a saddle point of a two-player game, and gradient descent on both players can cycle around one indefinitely without approaching it. Everything Part IX established about convergence assumed one objective and does not apply.

The gradient can vanish. If the discriminator gets too good, it outputs near-zero on every sample, and $\log(1 - d(g(\mathbf{z})))$ saturates. The generator's gradient goes to zero exactly when the discriminator is winning. The standard fix (the non-saturating loss, maximising $\log d(g(\mathbf{z}))$ instead) has the same fixed point and a usable gradient.

Mode collapse. Nothing in the objective rewards covering the data. A generator producing one perfect sample forever is penalised only insofar as the discriminator notices the lack of variety, and a discriminator that sees examples one at a time cannot.

§36 Mode collapse, measured

Why the loss form is not the culprit

The saturating objective is standardly named as the reason GAN training misbehaves, and on this problem it was the best of the four conditions. The vanishing-gradient argument of §35 is correct as far as it goes; it evidently is not what determines whether modes survive.

What did determine it was the relative progress of the two players. Over-train the discriminator and the generator is chased into whichever single region it can currently defend, and it stays there. Give Adam a high first-moment decay and the same thing happens more slowly. Both are properties of the optimisation dynamics, not of the objective's algebra.

This is consistent with which fixes actually worked historically. They were about controlling the discriminator (gradient penalties, spectral normalisation, two timescales) rather than about changing what the loss measures. The literature arrived there empirically; the measurement says why.

And note the failure's shape, because it is what makes it dangerous. Every sample the collapsed generator produces lands on a real mode. Each one, inspected alone, is perfect. What is wrong is invisible in any individual sample and visible only in the distribution, which is precisely what §42 says visual inspection cannot see.

§37 What was tried

A decade of stabilisation, briefly
  • Wasserstein GAN replaced Jensen–Shannon with a distance that stays informative when the two distributions do not overlap, removing one source of vanishing gradient. Requires the critic to be Lipschitz, which the gradient penalty supplies.
  • Spectral normalisation constrains the discriminator's Lipschitz constant directly, by dividing each weight matrix by its largest singular value: the operator norm of Part I §14, doing a job.
  • Two-timescale updates give the two players different learning rates, which is the balance §36 identified, turned into a knob.
  • Minibatch discrimination lets the discriminator see a batch rather than one example, so lack of variety becomes detectable at all.
  • Progressive growing and heavy architectural work produced the high-resolution results the family is remembered for.

§38 Why GANs lost

Four reasons, and the first is the one that mattered

Scaling was unreliable. Diffusion training is a regression: make it bigger, train it longer, it improves. GAN training is a game whose stability depends on a balance that must be re-established at each new scale. When compute became the main lever, the family that converts compute into quality monotonically won, and that is a statement about the optimisation problem, not about sample quality.

Coverage. §36's failure is intrinsic to an objective that never mentions how much of the data was produced. As the goal shifted from plausible images to models that respond faithfully to arbitrary prompts, coverage stopped being negotiable.

No likelihood. Nothing to report, nothing to compare, no anomaly detection, no principled model selection. §41 shows this is less of an advantage for the others than it appears, but zero is still worse than a loose bound.

Conditioning is harder. Adding a text condition to a diffusion model is an extra input and §30's guidance knob. Doing the same in a GAN means the discriminator must also judge whether the sample matches the condition, which makes an already delicate balance harder.

The honest coda: GANs are fast, sampling in one forward pass where diffusion takes tens. That advantage is real and it is why adversarial losses survive inside other systems: in the decoders of latent diffusion models, and in distilling many-step samplers into few-step ones. The family did not turn out to be wrong. It turned out to be the wrong thing to put at the centre.

§39 Boltzmann machines, as history

The family this part was originally about

A Boltzmann machine is §3's energy-based model with a specific energy: quadratic in binary units. The restricted form forbids connections within the visible and hidden layers, which makes the conditionals factorise, which makes Gibbs sampling easy. Stacking them gives deep belief networks and deep Boltzmann machines, and those were the models that restarted deep learning in 2006.

The source this part is built on devotes a chapter to them. They are now essentially unused, and the reasons are §7's, in a specific form: training needs samples from the model, sampling needs a chain to mix, and the chain mixes worse as the model gets better. Contrastive divergence made it affordable by running the chain a few steps from the data rather than to convergence, which works and biases the gradient.

What survived is the machinery, which is why it is in this part at all. The partition-function problem, the positive and negative phases, MCMC and score matching were all developed here, and score matching, invented to avoid $Z$ for these models, is what §29 is built on. The destination changed and the toolkit did not.
