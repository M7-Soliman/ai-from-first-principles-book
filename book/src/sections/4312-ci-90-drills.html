PracticeDrills

Thirty-nine drills. The harness in parts/22-causal/drills/ covers twenty of them with twenty-six tests. Several assert a negative result, because that is this part's subject: a correctly computed estimate reporting the wrong sign, and an adjustment that makes the answer worse.

Tier 0 Estimate first

EstimateE1 · How big an experiment

A randomised trial to detect a 2 percentage point lift on a 10% base rate, at 80% power. Write down the sample size per arm.

Answer. 3,600 per arm, from n ≈ 16p(1−p)/δ2. Halve the effect you care about and it quadruples. Do this arithmetic before the experiment, because afterwards it only tells you the experiment could never have answered the question. Score yourself: within a factor of two is a pass, and an order of magnitude out is worth understanding before you go on.

Tier 1 Warm-up

Warm-upW1 · Which rung

Classify each: "smokers have worse outcomes", "smoking worsens outcomes", "this patient would have recovered had they not smoked".

Answer. Association, intervention, counterfactual. Each needs strictly more than the one before, and no amount of the first delivers the second.
Warm-upW2 · The verb test

Take three abstracts from your field. Underline every verb implying action, then check whether anything was assigned rather than observed.

Answer. The gap is usually large and usually unremarked. This is the cheapest reading habit in the part.
Warm-upW3 · Three variables, three prescriptions

For a confounder, a collider and a mediator, say whether to adjust.

Answer. Adjust; do not adjust; depends on whether you want the direct or total effect. And you cannot tell which is which from the correlations (§5).
Warm-upW4 · Why bias does not shrink

Figure 1's naive estimate is computed on 40,000 points. Would 40 million help?

Answer. No. It would make the wrong number more precise. Confounding is bias; every diagnostic in Part XIV is about variance and fit, and all of them pass.
Warm-upW5 · Berkson's admission

Two unrelated conditions each independently make hospitalisation likely. Among inpatients, what association do you find?

Answer. Negative. Admission is a collider; conditioning on it opens the path. Figure 2 measures the same structure at −0.519.
Warm-upW6 · What randomisation deletes

State what a coin flip does to the structural causal model, and what it therefore does not require you to know.

Answer. It deletes the equation producing the treatment. So it severs every backdoor path, including through confounders you never measured or thought of. That is the entire advantage.
Warm-upW7 · The weights in the formula

Backdoor adjustment averages $p(y \mid x, z)$ over $p(z)$. Why not over $p(z \mid x)$?

Answer. Averaging over $p(z \mid x)$ returns $p(y \mid x)$: the conditional you were trying to escape. The marginal is what represents the whole population under intervention.
Warm-upW8 · The checkable assumption

Of no-unmeasured-confounding, positivity and consistency, which can you check, and how?

Answer. Positivity. Plot the propensity distribution by arm and look for regions with only one. One line of code, and Figure 3 is why it matters.
Warm-upW9 · Which model has to be right

State the doubly robust property, then say what Figure 4's fourth bar shows.

Answer. Consistent if either the outcome or the propensity model is correct. The fourth bar, neither correct, carries a bias of +0.98 on a true effect of 2.00, and reports it with a standard deviation of 0.04.
Warm-upW10 · The three instrument conditions

Name them and say which is testable.

Answer. Relevance, exogeneity, exclusion. Only relevance is testable, and it is the one that gets tested. Exclusion is asserted in a sentence and is where instruments usually fail.
Warm-upW11 · Whose effect

Give the estimand for: an instrument, a discontinuity, a matched analysis.

Answer. Compliers; units at the cutoff; the matched subset. All three are routinely written up as if they were the population average.
Warm-upW12 · Read Figure 6

Which model is best on training-like data, and which is best when the environment reverses?

Answer. The one using both features (0.060), and the one using only the cause (0.255). The ordering inverts completely, and a random split cannot detect it.

Tier 2 Core

CoreC1 · Build the graph tools

Implement descendants, paths, is_collider and path_is_blocked.

Answer. The collider rule is the one to get right: a collider blocks unless it or a descendant is conditioned on. Forgetting the descendant clause is the usual bug and it is exactly the case that bites in practice, because you rarely condition on the collider itself.
CoreC2 · d-separation, and the reversal

Implement d_separated. Verify that a fork is open and closes under conditioning, and that a collider is closed and opens.

Answer. The reversal is the whole content. Every other structure behaves the way intuition expects; the collider does the opposite, and it is why "control for more" is not conservative.
CoreC3 · The backdoor criterion, in code

Implement satisfies_backdoor. Check that it rejects a mediator and accepts the confounder.

Answer. The first condition, no descendants of the treatment, is what makes it reject the mediator. Having written it, run it over the covariate set of a model you have deployed.
CoreC4 · Reproduce Figure 1

Generate from a known structural causal model, sweep the confounding strength, and plot naive against adjusted.

Answer. The naive estimate crosses zero at a confounding strength of 2.25 and reports −0.476 at 3.0, against a true effect of +1.00. Setting the truth yourself is what makes the comparison meaningful.
CoreC5 · Stratify it

Plot the same data with per-stratum regression lines and a pooled line.

Answer. Every stratum positive, the pool negative. Nothing has gone wrong with either calculation: they answer different questions.
CoreC6 · Manufacture a correlation

Generate two independent variables and a common effect. Measure the correlation before and after conditioning, sweeping the collider's strength.

Answer. From −0.003 to −0.519. Then do it by selection rather than regression, drop the units with low $C$, and confirm it is the same thing. That equivalence is §6.
CoreC7 · Adjust for a mediator on purpose

Build $X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$ with no direct path. Estimate the effect with and without adjusting for $M$.

Answer. Adjusting gives approximately zero, which is the correct direct effect and a wrong summary of whether $X$ matters. Report which one you computed.
CoreC8 · The adjustment estimator

Implement backdoor_adjust by stratification. Weight the strata correctly.

Answer. By each bin's share of the whole sample, not of the treated: W7's point, in code. Weighting by the treated share silently returns the conditional.
CoreC9 · Fit a propensity score

Implement logistic regression by Newton–Raphson and check it recovers a known assignment rule.

Answer. Mean absolute error under 0.02 on 40,000 points. Write it out rather than importing it: the doubly robust figure in this part was wrong for an afternoon because a least-squares line pushed through a logistic is not logistic regression.
CoreC10 · Weighting against stratification

Estimate the same effect by IPW and by stratification. Compare estimates and spread.

Answer. They agree when overlap is good. IPW is more efficient and more fragile, and the fragility shows up as variance before it shows up as bias.
CoreC11 · Destroy the overlap

Reproduce Figure 3: sweep how strongly the confounder drives treatment, and track bias, spread and effective sample size.

Answer. Effective sample size 0.96 → 0.04; spread 0.066 → 0.791; bias under 0.03 almost throughout. Variance goes first, which is the friendly failure: it warns you.
CoreC12 · The four arms

Implement AIPW and run all four combinations of correct and incorrect models.

Answer. +2.0012, +2.0015, +2.0010, +2.9831 against a truth of 2.00. Make the "wrong" model genuinely wrong: a linear function of $U$ against a true dependence on $U^2$, so no coefficient can rescue it.
CoreC13 · What clipping costs

Break positivity, then sweep the weight clip and measure bias and spread separately.

Answer. Clip 0.05 gives bias 0.187 and spread 0.050; clip 0.001 gives 0.045 and 0.215. The remedy for the variance is a bias, and they move in opposite directions. This is Figure 4's right panel and it exists because the left panel would not come out.
CoreC14 · Two-stage least squares

Implement it and recover an effect with the confounder never observed.

Answer. +0.998 against a truth of +1.00 while OLS gives well over 1.2 on the same data. Nothing measured the confounder; the design supplied variation instead.
CoreC15 · What the F > 10 rule is worth

Sweep instrument strength. Record the first-stage F and the spread of the estimate across repeated samples.

Answer. At the weak end the estimate is not a wide interval around the right answer: it is unstable across samples with a spread several times the effect. Find where F sits when that happens and form your own view of the threshold.
CoreC16 · Violate exclusion

Add a small direct path from the instrument to the outcome and re-estimate.

Answer. The estimate moves and nothing warns you: the first-stage F is unchanged, the interval is tight. This is why exclusion is argued rather than tested, and why the argument belongs in the write-up.
CoreC17 · Differences and discontinuities

Implement did_estimate and rdd_estimate. Then break each: give the groups diverging pre-trends, and let units manipulate the running variable.

Answer. Both break, and both breakages are detectable: a pre-trend plot and a density check at the cutoff. Run them by default.
CoreC18 · Off-policy evaluation

Implement the inverse-propensity and self-normalised estimators. Evaluate a target policy from logs of a different one.

Answer. Recovers the value when support holds. Self-normalising has lower variance and is biased. Then remove support entirely, an action the logging policy never took, and confirm the estimator returns a confident number about a policy it has no information on.
CoreC19 · Reproduce Figure 6

Build a target with a causal parent and a spurious child whose relationship varies by environment. Train three predictors and test across environments.

Answer. The causal model holds at 0.242–0.258 across all four; the others go from best (0.060) to 3.211 and 5.484. Then run a random split instead of an environment split and confirm it sees nothing.

Tier 3 Stretch

StretchS1 · Draw the graph for something you built

Take a deployed model. Draw the graph, classify every feature, and find the descendants of the treatment in your covariate set.

Answer. Most feature sets contain at least one. Part XIII §11's leakage is the extreme case; the ordinary case is a mediator that improved validation accuracy.
StretchS2 · Sensitivity analysis

Implement the two-parameter sweep of §22 and produce the contour at which your effect crosses zero.

Answer. Report it as "a confounder would need to be about as strong as $Z_3$." That is checkable against domain knowledge; the point estimate alone is not.
StretchS3 · CATE, and why you cannot score it

Implement an S-, T- and X-learner. Try to validate them, and write down why each attempt fails.

Answer. The label does not exist for any unit. What you can do is score the implied policy off-policy, or check that groups ranked by predicted effect show the predicted ordering. Both are weaker than a held-out loss.
StretchS4 · Causal discovery, honestly

Run a constraint-based discovery algorithm on data from a graph you generated. Compare what it returns with the truth.

Answer. A Markov equivalence class, with many edges unoriented, and it degrades fast with sample size and with a hidden confounder. Useful as a check on a graph you drew, not as a substitute for drawing one.
StretchS5 · A synthetic control, with placebos

Build one for a single treated unit, then run the placebo test on every donor.

Answer. If the real effect does not stand out from the placebo distribution, the pre-period fit was Part XIV §20's biased maximum over the weight space.
StretchS6 · Audit a published causal claim

Take a paper in your field and answer §41's six questions from it.

Answer. The positivity check is usually absent and the estimand usually broader in the conclusion than in the design. Doing this once changes how you read the literature.
StretchS7 · Log the probability

Take a system you run that makes choices. Add logging of the action probability and the policy version. Then answer a counterfactual question about last month.

Answer. The single highest-return change in this part, and it is an engineering change rather than a modelling one. It cannot be applied retroactively, which is why it is worth doing before you need it.
